## What this episode covers

- Sinclair's information theory of aging: aging as loss of epigenetic information — cells forget which genes to read, like a scratched record — while the DNA itself stays largely intact and a youthful backup copy may be reinstalled.
- DNA breaks and repair proteins that do not all return to their gene-regulating posts cause cellular identity to drift; his ICE mice, engineered for epigenome disruption without mutations, aged rapidly.
- Three genes delivered into the optic nerve and switched on for six to eight weeks reset measured cell age by about seventy five percent in animal and lab work; very old mice given the genes systemically had their remaining lifespan doubled in an independent lab.
- A first human trial submitted to the FDA targets blindness — glaucoma and eye stroke first — because the eye is enclosed and safer.
- Lifestyle levers today: fasting as his top lever, plus hormesis from exercise, cold and sauna; a study he cites found people jogging about thirty minutes five days a week had markers looking about ten years younger.
- Caution: no human age-reversal result exists yet, mouse results often fail to translate, his supplements have no proven lifespan benefit in humans, he has commercial interests in the field, and a pill within about ten years is a forecast, not a timeline.

## Script

Mimi: Welcome to Health Tidbits, your daily four to five minute dive into the best health conversations on the internet. I am Mimi, and with Alex, I take one episode from the doctor health podcast library and pull out the ideas that matter. Today we are asking a big question. Can aging itself be reversed?
Alex: This one comes from The Diary Of A CEO, where Steven Bartlett sat down with Doctor David Sinclair, a professor of genetics at Harvard Medical School and the author of Lifespan. His central idea is called the information theory of aging. He argues that aging is mainly a loss of information inside our cells. The DNA itself stays largely intact, but cells gradually forget which genes they should be reading. His analogy is a scratched record. The music is still there, it just cannot be played properly anymore.
Mimi: And he believes there is a backup copy of that youthful information in every cell, waiting to be reinstalled. The damage, in his telling, starts with constant breaks in our DNA. Repair proteins rush to fix those breaks, and over time not all of them return to their regular jobs of controlling genes. So cellular identity slowly drifts, and that drift is what we experience as aging.
Alex: His lab tested that idea in mice engineered to have this kind of disruption without actual mutations. Those mice aged rapidly, even turning grey. Then came the striking experiment. Three genes were delivered into the optic nerve and switched on for six to eight weeks. The measured age of those cells reset by about seventy five percent, and then stopped, rather than going all the way back to zero. In mice, the same approach has also been tried in the brain, the skin, and hearing.
Mimi: There is one more animal result worth knowing. In an independent lab, very old mice, roughly equivalent to eighty to eighty five year old humans, were given the genes throughout their bodies, and their remaining lifespan doubled. Sinclair describes it like giving a seventy year old who expects ten more years another ten years. A first human trial has been submitted, and it targets blindness, starting with glaucoma and eye stroke, because the eye is enclosed and safer to treat.
Alex: Now, before anyone gets too excited, here is the caution we always include. That seventy five percent figure comes from measures in animal and lab studies. At the time of recording, the human trial had not yet begun, so there is no human age reversal result yet. The doubled lifespan was in very old mice, in a single experiment, and mouse results often fail to translate to people. Sinclair also has commercial interests in this field, which he discusses openly, but listeners should keep that in mind.
Mimi: So what can you actually do today, according to Sinclair? His top lifestyle lever is fasting. He skips breakfast and often eats his first meal in the mid afternoon. He calls three meals a day craziness, and he says not to eat if you are not hungry, while warning that fasting must never become malnutrition.
Alex: The deeper principle is something called hormesis, the idea that mild adversity switches on our repair defences. Fasting counts, and so do exercise, cold plunges, and saunas. He cites a study where people jogging about thirty minutes, five days a week, had markers that looked about ten years younger than sedentary people. Modern comfort, with constant food and no exertion, leaves those defences switched off. He also warns that smoking, heavy drinking, ultra processed foods, and even loud concerts that damage the ear can accelerate aging.
Mimi: He does take supplements himself, including an NAD precursor, resveratrol, and spermidine, but be careful here too. None of those has a proven lifespan benefit in humans, and he frames his own use as self experimentation. Predictions like a rejuvenation pill within about ten years are his forecasts, not established timelines.
Alex: So here is today's one concrete takeaway. This week, try his simplest hormesis habit. Take a brisk thirty minute walk or jog on five days, and let yourself get properly hungry before your first meal, without skipping nutrition altogether. You are using the levers he says switch on repair, with no pill and no price tag.
