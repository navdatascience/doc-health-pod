## What this episode covers

- Vegetables at every meal — about half a cup to one cup, in a rainbow of colours — because typical tiffin breakfasts and dinners often contain no vegetables at all; local gourds and pumpkin over exotic options.
- The NOVA classification: group 1 whole foods, group 2 minimally processed, group 3 processed with added salt/sugar, group 4 ultra-processed with additives — live close to group 1.
- Thirty different plant varieties a week for gut health; Dr Pal teaches five to six plant points a day.
- Saturated fat under ten percent of calories as a ceiling, not a target; ghee capped at one teaspoon for the whole day.
- Eat only when hungry: the stomach needs about three hours to empty, three meals plus two snacks is too much, and an early light dinner finishing around six thirty.
- A glucose monitor story: rava upma spiking one older patient's sugar to three hundred changed her behaviour.
- South Asians face diabetes and heart disease about a decade earlier, so prevention should start before diagnosis.
- Caution: the Ornish heart study was small (about forty eight patients) and multi-component, so diet alone cannot claim the benefit; and there is no clear-cut evidence milk is inflammatory, with curd protective in some conditions.

## Script

Mimi: Welcome to Health Tidbits, your daily four to five minute dive into the best health conversations on the internet. I am Mimi, and with Alex, I take one episode from the doctor health podcast library and pull out the ideas that matter. Today we are talking about food, protein, and diabetes, and what many Indian meals are quietly getting wrong.
Alex: This one comes from the Dr Pal podcast, where Dr Pal sat down with Dr Nithya Subramaniam, a lifestyle medicine and family physician who practises whole food, plant based medicine. Her opening observation is uncomfortable. A typical South Indian breakfast and dinner, the tiffin meals, often contain no vegetables at all, and even lunch is mostly grain with very little vegetable.
Mimi: So her first rule is simple. Put vegetables at every meal, about half a cup to one cup each time, and eat a rainbow of different colours across the day, because each colour brings different vitamins and minerals. And she means ordinary, local, economical vegetables, like bottle gourd, snake gourd, ridge gourd, and pumpkin, not expensive exotic ones. In her own home, more than half the plate is vegetables, plus a portion of dal, and sweet potato often replaces rice as the main carbohydrate.
Alex: Her second idea helps you judge any food quickly. She explains the Nova classification in four groups. Group one is whole foods eaten as they are, like an apple or a carrot. Group two is minimally processed, like rice or bread. Group three is processed food with added salt or sugar, where that same carrot becomes carrot halwa. And group four is ultra processed food with additives, like biscuits, packaged snacks, and namkeen. Her advice is to live as close to group one as you can.
Mimi: Third, variety for your gut. She cites evidence for eating thirty different varieties of plants a week, and Dr Pal adds that he teaches patients five to six plant points a day across nuts, seeds, vegetables, and fruits, without repeating the same item. That variety, they argue, is what feeds a healthier gut.
Alex: Fourth, watch saturated fat, not just sugar. She says saturated fat should stay under ten percent of total calories. On a two thousand calorie diet, that is two hundred calories, and she stresses that is a ceiling, not a target. If you insist on ghee, keep it to no more than one teaspoon for the entire day, because swapping oil for ghee does not help. Saturated fat, she says, raises the risk of both heart disease and diabetes.
Mimi: Fifth, stop eating all day long. She says three meals plus two snacks is too much, because the stomach takes about three hours to empty and needs rest, and people should eat when they are actually hungry. For many of her patients, genuine hunger first arrives around midday. She also eats an early, light dinner, finishing by about half past six in the evening, and she tells a striking story of an older patient whose glucose monitor showed rava upma spiking her sugar right up to three hundred, and seeing that number changed her diet more than any lecture could.
Alex: And there is a reason she pushes prevention early. She notes that South Asians carry an inherent risk for diabetes and heart disease, and that these diseases tend to arrive about a decade earlier in Indians, so waiting for a diagnosis is waiting too long.
Mimi: A quick caution, as always. She mentions a famous study by Dean Ornish, where about forty eight heart patients on a plant based diet plus exercise, stress management, and social support saw their blocked arteries improve slightly. But that was a small study, and the benefit came from the whole package, not diet alone. And on milk, she is honest that there is no clear cut evidence that milk itself is inflammatory, and curd appears protective in some conditions.
Alex: So here is today's one concrete takeaway. At your very next meal, before you serve the rice or roti, put vegetables on the plate first, a good half cup to a cup, in at least two colours. Do that at every meal this week, and you have made the single change she asks for first.
Mimi: The full conversation between Dr Pal and Dr Nithya Subramaniam, with the complete transcript, is in the doctor health podcast library on GitHub. We will be back tomorrow with another episode of Health Tidbits. Take care, and eat your vegetables first.
