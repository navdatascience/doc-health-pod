## What this episode covers

- Bloating and constipation as common gut complaints, especially in night-shift workers; incomplete evacuation is more common in Dr Vatsya's practice than true constipation, and functional symptoms ease during sleep and when the mind is busy.
- Types of constipation: slow-transit, normal-transit (IBS-type), and pelvic dyssynergia (straining against a closed sphincter).
- Red flags to check before labelling IBS: recent onset, unintentional weight loss over five percent in six months, blood in stool or vomit, anaemia, progressive vomiting, and a first-degree relative with GI cancer; his four-C workup and celiac screening (about one in ninety six in parts of North India).
- Fibre added too fast causes bloating; increase gradually over four to six weeks toward twenty five to forty grams a day, with movement and at least three litres of water, a footstool or squatting, and no straining or phones on the toilet.
- Psyllium husk (isabgol): sixty to seventy percent soluble fibre, needs water, and should be spaced two to three hours from blood pressure, diabetes and blood-thinner medicines.
- Detox verdict: colon cleansing and liver-detox products called a major scam — the liver and kidneys already detox the body; collagen supplements and lemon-water detox also dismissed.
- Caution: some predictive and population figures are stated without full citations, his dashboard targets are stricter than some common cut-offs, and higher water advice was qualified for people with normal kidneys and no fluid-retention issues.

## Script

Mimi: Welcome to Health Tidbits, your daily four to five minute dive into the best health conversations on the internet. I am Mimi, and with Alex, I take one episode from the doctor health podcast library and pull out the ideas that matter. Today we are talking about a very common complaint. Why is your stomach always bloated?
Alex: This one comes from the Dr Pal podcast, where Doctor Pal Manickam sat down with Doctor Shubham Vatsya, a gastroenterologist from Delhi. He says bloating and constipation are among the most common gut complaints he sees, especially in night shift workers whose sleep is out of sync with the clock. And here is his first surprise. In his practice, incomplete evacuation is even more common than true constipation. Many people go every day, but they never quite empty properly.
Mimi: That distinction matters, because the fix is different. He separates slow transit constipation, where there is no urge for days, from normal transit, irritable bowel type constipation, and from a pelvic problem where someone strains against a closed sphincter. Functional symptoms, he says, characteristically ease during sleep and when the mind is busy or resting. Poor sleep and stress make them worse.
Alex: Before anyone labels it irritable bowel syndrome, though, he checks for red flags. Those include a very recent onset, unintentional weight loss of more than five percent of body weight in six months, blood in the stool or vomit, low haemoglobin or anaemia, progressive vomiting, and a close relative with gut cancer. He says iron deficiency after age fifty should be treated as a possible gut malignancy until proved otherwise. He also uses four tests, covering blood count, inflammation markers, a stool test, and celiac screening, since celiac disease affects about one in ninety six people in parts of North India.
Mimi: Once danger is ruled out, his main culprit for bloating might surprise you. Fibre, added too fast. He says India is grossly deficient in fibre, with about seventy percent of people falling short, but suddenly loading up on oats or fibre feeds gut bacteria and creates gas. Increase fibre gradually, he advises, and many patients improve in four to six weeks.
Alex: His natural prescription has three parts. First, build toward twenty five to forty grams of mixed fibre a day. Second, move, aiming for ten thousand brisk steps, or about one hundred and fifty to two hundred and forty minutes a week of aerobic and strength work. Third, drink at least three litres of water, because fibre pulls water, and dehydration makes bloating worse. He also suggests squatting, or putting a stool under your feet on a Western toilet, not straining, and never taking your phone to the toilet, because straining swells the veins and causes piles.
Mimi: He is also careful about psyllium husk, known as isabgol. It is sixty to seventy percent soluble fibre, it forms a gel, slows sugar spikes, and can help lower cholesterol, but it needs water, otherwise it can cause bloating and cramps, and it should be spaced two to three hours from blood pressure, diabetes, and blood thinner medicines because it can reduce their absorption.
Alex: And he is blunt about detoxes. He calls colon cleansing and liver detox syrups a major scam, saying your body does not need a detox, because your liver and kidneys already do that job. He gives the same verdict to collagen supplements, and calls lemon water detoxing the liver a myth.
Mimi: Now for our caution, as always. Some numbers in this conversation are his stated positions without full citations in the episode, including a stool test ruling out gut inflammation in ninety nine to one hundred percent of cases, and population figures for fibre and vitamin B twelve deficiency. His personal health dashboard targets are also stricter than some common cut offs, and his higher water advice was qualified for people with normal kidneys and no fluid retention issues. Treat those as prompts to discuss with your own doctor, not as rules.
Alex: So here is today's one concrete takeaway. For the next two weeks, add fibre slowly instead of all at once, drink water alongside it, take a brisk walk after meals, and use a small footstool in the bathroom. Small, gradual, and consistent beats any expensive cleanse.
Mimi: The full conversation between Doctor Pal and Doctor Shubham Vatsya, with the complete transcript, is in the doctor health podcast library on GitHub. We will be back tomorrow with another episode of Health Tidbits. Take care, and be kind to your gut.
