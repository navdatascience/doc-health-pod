## Important tidbits
- Dr van Tulleken says poor diet has overtaken tobacco as the leading cause of early death, and that around 75% of the world's calories now come from just six companies.
- Ultra-processed food (UPF) is defined using the NOVA classification developed in Brazil around 2009–2010; he says 1 in 5 people in the UK get about 80% of their calories from UPF.
- In his month-long self-experiment eating 80% UPF (which he describes as a normal British teenager's diet), he gained weight at a rate that would have doubled his body weight in a year.
- Brain scans from that experiment showed increased connectivity between the brain's habit/automatic regions and its reward/addiction regions, and his hunger hormones stayed sky-high even after a standard meal — he argues UPF interferes with the satiety mechanism itself.
- He identifies softness, high energy density, and formulation testing as harm mechanisms: products are focus-group tested and a formulation eaten just 5% faster wins, so UPF is engineered to be eaten quickly and in excess.
- He dissects Diet Coke (caramel colour E150d, aspartame, acesulfame K, phosphoric acid that can leach minerals) and notes it still scores four green traffic lights — and says a health claim on a packet is roughly proof the product is ultra-processed.
- He argues willpower research is largely a proxy for poverty: the famous marshmallow-test effect disappears once you adjust for maternal education.
- He dates the obesity inflection point to around 1975, appearing across all demographics at once, which he says points to the food environment rather than individual behaviour.
- He cites Clara Davis's 1920s study in which toddlers self-selected from 34 whole foods — including a boy with rickets who drank cod liver oil daily until his rickets healed and then never touched it again — as evidence the body can regulate appetite when the food environment is whole foods.
- Citing Herman Pontzer's work with the Hadza, he presents the constrained ("fixed") energy model: sustained activity does not raise total calorie burn; exercise instead reallocates energy away from inflammation and anxiety.
- Citing twin research by Claire Llewellyn, he says removing poverty would remove roughly 60% of diet-related disease; his own identical twin Xand gained 30 kg while living stressed above a burger shop in Boston.
- He defines addiction as continued use despite harm plus failed attempts to quit, and says speed of consumption is part of what makes a substance addictive — which is why UPF qualifies.
- He cites 22% of all deaths as linked to high-UPF diets, per figures in his book.
- His policy asks: tackle poverty first, introduce light-touch labelling regulation, and remove industry from policy-making — he notes the British Nutrition Foundation, Cancer Research UK, and Diabetes UK receive funding from UPF makers.
- His personal method is to move from addiction to disgust — calling UPF an "industrially produced edible substance" (a phrase he credits to Fernanda Rauber) — and he advises people to stop nagging loved ones about their weight.

## Claims to be careful with
- The headline framing (a food "worse than smoking," 22% of deaths linked to high-UPF diets) reflects figures as cited in his book and his advocacy position; the causal share attributable to processing itself, versus nutrients like sugar, salt, and fat, is still debated in nutrition science.
- His strong anti-personal-responsibility and industry-capture framing is an advocacy stance; individual behaviour change and food-environment reform are not mutually exclusive in mainstream public-health thinking.
- The self-experiment results (weight gain, brain-scan changes, hormone levels) are an n-of-1 demonstration, not a controlled trial.
- The constrained-energy model and the "removing poverty removes ~60% of diet-related disease" figure are presented as he cites them from other researchers and simplify contested research areas.

## Summary
Dr Chris van Tulleken argues that ultra-processed food — engineered by a handful of giant companies for softness, energy density, and speed of eating — has overtaken smoking as the leading driver of early death, hijacking appetite and reward circuits in ways his own month-long 80%-UPF experiment made visible in his weight, brain scans, and hunger hormones. He contends obesity and diet-related disease are primarily products of poverty and a captured food environment rather than weak willpower, and calls for poverty reduction, labelling rules, and removing industry from nutrition policy, while personally reframing UPF as an "industrially produced edible substance" to break its hold.
