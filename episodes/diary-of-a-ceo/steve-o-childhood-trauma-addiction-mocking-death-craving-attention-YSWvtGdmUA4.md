# Steve-O: Childhood Trauma, Addiction, Mocking Death & Craving Attention!

- **Source:** The Diary Of A CEO
- **Guest:** Steve-O (Stephen Gilchrist Glover), entertainer and stunt performer known for *Jackass*, now a stand-up comedian

- **Video ID:** YSWvtGdmUA4

## Important tidbits

- Steve-O traces his attention-seeking to his childhood: born in Wimbledon, London in 1974, he is a triple national (British, Canadian, American) and moved constantly — Brazil, Venezuela, Canada, the USA — never staying anywhere more than a couple of years, raised largely by live-in maids (his first words were Portuguese) while his father, a businessman who became president of Pepsi-Cola Brazil, was away more than home.
- His mother was an alcoholic whose binges lasted days or weeks; by the time he was eight or nine it was, in his words, "terrible." He says he understood the disease early: once she had one drink, "all bets were off," and she pursued the illusion of controlled drinking despite repeatedly swearing she would never drink again.
- He describes a genetic pattern: addiction "never skipped a generation" on his mother's side of the family, while his father's side is highly academic (theologians, clergymen, master's and PhD holders). He sees himself as a hybrid — the deviance and alcoholism from one side with the drive of the other.
- Despite watching alcoholism destroy his mother, he started drinking regularly at 16, convincing himself he would be different because he would "enjoy it" — which he calls the insanity of the disease.
- As a child he performed for his father's approval — doing 100 push-ups in a row for his father's friends for $1, and diving headfirst for baseballs. He links this to stunts becoming his route to validation; after a heartbreak he escalated to genuinely dangerous stunts (jumping off rooftops into pools, climbing balconies) and mailed the videos to the girl who had dumped him.
- He dropped out of the University of Miami in 1993 to become a "crazy famous stunt man" when no such career existed, and was effectively homeless — couch-surfing — for the first three years.
- His mother suffered a brain aneurysm on October 10, 1998, leaving her severely disabled for her last five years: wheelchair-bound, lifted in and out of bed, developing bed sores and crying in pain. He says those five years traumatised him more than anything else in his life.
- During that same hospital crisis, his father apologised for not supporting his chosen path and pledged to back him — a turning point that later brought them together; his father, now 80, has come out of retirement and works on his payroll managing business and insurance matters.
- His mother died in November 2003, when he was 29. The overwhelming emotion he records in his memoir *Professional Idiot* was relief — her suffering was over. He says he only really processed the grief years later in recovery.
- By mid-2007 he was, in his words, practically living on a diet of cocaine, booze and nitrous, experiencing hallucinations and hearing voices. He describes psychosis as substances eroding barriers between "compartments" or dimensions, letting in "demons" and "angels."
- He broadcast his collapse in real time via his "rad" email list — a barrage of emails and disturbing videos sent around the clock to roughly 200 influential people in entertainment — which helped precipitate an intervention (the episode's intro recounts him being manhandled into a psych ward).
- He is now over 15 years sober. His central claim: addiction is the only disease where, once treated, you become a *better* version of yourself than before you got sick, rather than merely returning to baseline.
- After getting sober he pursued stand-up comedy in earnest from 2010 — a through-line he openly calls attention-seeking — spending 11 years working the US comedy club circuit and taping specials, before creating the multimedia "Bucket List" tour built around stunts crazier than Jackass.
- He stresses that his happiness depends on separating himself from the Steve-O persona: basing self-worth on the persona guarantees a "dark and upsetting future" as the spotlight wanes. Learning to be in a healthy relationship became a deliberate project.
- He credits his fiancée Lux with teaching him to love — she naturally embodies the honesty, openness and willingness he had to train himself into through the 12 steps. They have a rule of never being apart for more than two weeks (which they recently broke badly, spending only two days together in six weeks).
- His closing advice: every study on longevity, health and happiness points to the quality of relationships, not bank balances — hustle because you love it, not out of fear.

## Claims to be careful with

- This is a personal memoir-style account, not medical evidence: his views on addiction genetics, psychosis as "dimensions/demons," and recovery come from his own experience and 12-step framing, not clinical research.
- His claim that addiction is "the only disease" where treatment leaves you better than before is a rhetorical point from recovery culture, not a medical consensus.
- Details of his mother's illness, his father's naivety about her drinking, and family dynamics are his one-sided recollections of events decades past.
- The relationship-longevity point he cites at the end (a "95-year study" making people live ~14 years longer) is quoted loosely and conversationally, not from a source he names precisely.

## Summary

Steve-O — Stephen Gilchrist Glover — explains how a childhood of constant moving, an absent businessman father and an alcoholic mother produced a lifelong craving for attention that he channelled first into dangerous stunts, then fame with Jackass, and then into severe cocaine, alcohol and nitrous addiction complete with psychosis, even as his mother spent five disabled years dying after a 1998 aneurysm. Now over 15 years sober, he argues honesty and recovery made him better than his pre-addiction self, and that his survival depends on separating the man from the Steve-O persona and investing in his relationship with his fiancée Lux rather than in external validation.
