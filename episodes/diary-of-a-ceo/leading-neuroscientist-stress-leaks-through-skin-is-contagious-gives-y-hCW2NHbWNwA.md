- **Guest:** Dr Tara Swart — neuroscientist, medical doctor (former psychiatrist), executive advisor, and author of *The Source*

## Important tidbits
- Dr Swart says stress is literally contagious: cortisol leaks out through sweat and enters other people's skin, with hierarchical amplification — a leader's or CEO's stress affects everyone below them, which she says explains the drained feeling after a meeting with a stressed person. She gives the same sweat-leak mechanism for menstrual synchronisation among women living or working closely together, led by the "alpha female."
- She describes cortisol above its normal range, persistently, as making brain receptors read imminent survival threat and driving inflammation (vascular, cardiac, and gut); stress itself she defines as perceived load exceeding capacity, with a healthy response being a spike that comes back down. Her background observation: executives treated the body as a vehicle for the brain while traders dropped dead of stress heart attacks with no hypertension, cholesterol problem, or smoking.
- She says cortisol, as a survival mechanism, stores hard-to-shift abdominal fat — recounting bank clients lifting their shirts to show her, and a CEO's PA shouting "so he's the reason that I'm fat." Signs of high cortisol: sleep disruption, belly fat, reflux/indigestion, irritability and snapping at home, and dry skin or skin problems (she calls skin the psychological boundary). Her remedies include aerobic exercise — literally sweating cortisol out — and journaling or speaking it out.
- On sleep: she cites 8 hours 15 minutes as the ideal in population studies, warns more than 9 hours can be depressogenic, and says you need 8–9 hours in bed. The glymphatic system, discovered around 2012–14, actively flushes tau, amyloid, and neurofibrillary tangles overnight and needs 7–8 hours of actual sleep; side-sleeping is best for it. She says she would "never" sleep apart from her partner, noting her HRV monitor showed her highest resilience spike when her husband kissed her goodbye at 5 a.m. while she slept on.
- On bonding: right-eye-to-left-eye eye contact (from the mother–baby amygdala resonance loop), touch such as a handshake plus a hand on the arm, laughing together, shared struggle, and baths over showers all raise oxytocin.
- She defines intuition as unconscious stored experience pushed deep by Hebbian learning — cortex to limbic system to brainstem to spinal cord to gut neurons — which is why "gut instinct" is physical. She also notes people suppress illness until holidays when cortisol drops and the immune system runs down.
- She recommends purpose beyond the self, time in nature, and neuroaesthetics — a weekly creative activity — because appreciating beauty signals safety to the brain; and she says social contagion extends beyond stress (divorce and obesity statistics cluster in social groups), so you should curate your tribe for psychological level and growth.
- Her three-step method for changing habits through neuroplasticity, plus a fourth factor: (1) raised awareness — spot the pattern and get below the thought to the underlying belief, which she calls 50% of the battle; (2) focused attention — map where and when the pattern fires and marry the decision to its consequence ("you are basically the sum of every decision that you've made in your life"); (3) deliberate practice — rehearse the new behaviour in real scenarios, accepting early partial success (50%, then 75%) until the new pathway is stronger than the old; and (4) accountability, via a friend, a professional, or an "action board" of images of what you said you would achieve.
- She says the field has moved from "hardwired" to "soft-wired": even childhood trauma pathways can change substantially, though some people live with trauma's consequences for life. The brain grows and changes until about 25, then plateaus from 25–65 unless forced by intense new learning such as a language, which yields global executive-function benefits.
- She explains three mechanisms of neuroplasticity: myelination (myelin coats repeated pathways, making them fast — which is why a reflex snatches your hand from fire before the slower pain signal arrives), synaptic connection (existing neurons linking into new pathways — the effortful route), and neurogenesis (embryonic cells maturing into neurons, mainly around the hippocampus in adults), driven by the growth factor BDNF. Her "fun fact" on feeding neurogenesis: regular aerobic exercise turns over brain cells at about 13–14%, but restarting aerobic exercise after a period of inactivity raises the rate to about 30% (weight training, she says, does not relate to neurogenesis as much), and dark-skinned foods contribute via anthocyanin antioxidants — black beans over white beans, blueberries over strawberries, dark over milk chocolate, purple sprouting broccoli, and good coffee.
- She recounts the weightlifter visualisation experiment: over two weeks, one group lifting finger/elbow weights gained about 40% muscle mass in the targeted muscles, while a group that only imagined lifting gained 13% — evidence, she says, of the power of visualisation and intention, as used by athletes.
- On generational and epigenetic trauma: she distinguishes generational trauma (psychological spillover in groups such as First Americans or enslaved people) from intergenerational epigenetic trauma, where an external event changes gene expression — the famous examples being the Holocaust and the Dutch famine, with stress-response changes seen three-plus generations on, sometimes as resilience, sometimes as anxiety. "You aren't born with the genes that your parents had when they were born — you're born with the expression of the genes that your parents had around the time of conception"; a stressed mother's cortisol crosses the placenta and can switch on genes for anxiety liability and start inflammation before birth, which she says is a reason to manage stress in pregnancy and to teach affected children meditation and emotional skills, not to blame mothers.
- On grief: she believes you have to "go to the bottom of the hole" and process all the emotions fully rather than glossing over them with work or partying, or the grief will "come back and bite you later."
- She reframes manifestation as brain-based: thoughts, beliefs, then actions. She calls vision boards "action boards" — a representation of what you want that you still have to go out and make happen. To manifest a partner, list the attributes you want and then make sure you are everything on that list, because "you meet people at the level of psychological evolution that you're at" and equally at the level of your psychological wound. Wanting states are cortisol-inducing; focusing on what you can offer is oxytocin.
- On ageing and priming: she recounts a study of three groups of people in their 80s — a control group, a group that reminisced about being in their 60s, and a group that lived for a week in homes retrofitted to 20 years earlier, carrying their own suitcases — after which the live-in group stood taller, had better musculoskeletal coordination, and were rated younger in after-photos; the reminiscing group improved less. She also cites Florida medical students who walked more slowly after unscrambling sentences containing retirement-associated words (Florida, beach, sunshine, walk, bungalow), and says her own eyesight improved after she refused her optician's prediction that she would need reading glasses, simply by not moving reading material further away.
- Her practical foundations for neuroplasticity: roughly 8 hours' sleep with regular sleep and wake times (within an hour, e.g., asleep between 10 and 11 p.m.), not being sedentary but avoiding too much high-intensity exercise (it spikes cortisol), 30 different plant products a week varied by colour, managing stress, staying hydrated, and — only once foundations are right — time-restricted eating (she eats between noon and 8 p.m.) for blood-sugar regulation and brain benefits. Her closing first step: spend 5 minutes visualising what you want as already true, feeling it in your body, then give gratitude for it — moving the brain from a fear state to a trust state.

## Claims to be careful with
- The claim that cortisol "leaks" through sweat and enters others' skin, transmitting stress (and synchronising menstrual cycles), goes well beyond mainstream evidence; menstrual synchrony itself is disputed in the literature, and her cave-evolution account of it is speculative storytelling.
- The visualisation study figures (about 40% versus 13% muscle-mass gains) and the aerobic-exercise neurogenesis rates (13–14%, rising to 30%) are as she recounts them from memory; the underlying studies measured strength gains under specific conditions, and adult human neurogenesis rates remain an unsettled research area.
- The ageing-priming study and the Florida priming experiment are classic social-priming results; priming research of this era has faced replication difficulties, and her personal eyesight-improvement story is an anecdote, not evidence that refusing glasses improves vision.
- Epigenetic inheritance of trauma across three-plus generations is an emerging, contested field; the human evidence is much thinner than the animal evidence, and her framing is more confident than the consensus.
- Her manifestation framework is a neuroscience-flavoured reinterpretation of law-of-attraction ideas; the actionable parts (clarity of goals, self-work, deliberate practice, accountability) are conventional behaviour-change advice, while any "attraction" mechanism is not established science.
- The episode contains paid sponsor segments (Eight Sleep, Huel — the host discloses he is a Huel investor and board member), and Dr Swart is promoting her book *The Source* and her podcast.

## Summary
Neuroscientist and former psychiatrist Dr Tara Swart argues that the brain and body are one system: stress leaks cortisol through sweat into other people and stores belly fat, sleep's glymphatic system needs 7–8 hours to flush the brain's waste, and intuition is stored experience reaching the gut. The bulk of the conversation is a practical manual for neuroplasticity — spot the pattern and the belief beneath it, attend to its consequences, practise the new behaviour deliberately, and stay accountable — supported by myelination, synaptic connection, and exercise- and dark-food-driven neurogenesis, and extended into epigenetic trauma, grief, ageing mindsets, and a brain-based version of manifestation in which you attract what you are, not just what you want.
