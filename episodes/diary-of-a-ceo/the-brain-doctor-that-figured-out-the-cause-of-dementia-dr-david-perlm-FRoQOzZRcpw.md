## Important tidbits
- Perlmutter says Alzheimer's is the third leading cause of death in America, that if you live to 85 your risk of dementia, generally Alzheimer's, is close to the high 40% — "a flip of a coin" — and that women have twice the risk of Alzheimer's compared with men; he says it is significantly preventable and that there is no meaningful treatment yet.
- He frames Alzheimer's as primarily a metabolic condition of the brain, not primarily a beta-amyloid disease: he says you can have a brain full of amyloid plaques and not be demented, that only about 14% of Alzheimer's research now focuses on amyloid (down from about a third 10 years ago), and points to England's £33 million / $44 million AD SMART trial of metformin, a diabetes drug, and the ADHD drug Strattera in about 1,000 patients over 18 months.
- His top-5 list, in his order, is: (1) offloading agency for your brain to the pharmaceutical industry instead of recognising you are the architect of your brain's destiny, (2) being sedentary, (3) lack of social connection, (4) non-restorative sleep, and (5) poor diet.
- On genetics: he says about 25% of people carry one APOE4 allele, which he puts at about a fivefold Alzheimer's risk, and two APOE4 copies at up to a twelvefold risk, but stresses APOE4 is a predisposition, not a determinant — "genetics loads the gun, lifestyle pulls the trigger."
- On exercise: he cites a November 2025 JAMA study by Moreno from the Framingham Heart Study (about 4,000 people in 4 age groups) finding the most active middle-aged people had about a 45% lower dementia risk than the least active, and older APOE4 carriers who were most active had about a 67% reduced risk; he says a meta-analysis supports doing both aerobic and resistance training.
- He explains exercise via muscles as an endocrine organ: contracting muscle releases myokines that help the brain produce BDNF, grow and nurture neurons and synapses, improve insulin function and blood flow, and reduce inflammation — which is why he holds up a pair of running shoes as the most powerful brain tool.
- His central mechanism is microglia, the brain's immune cells (about 10% of brain cells): supportive "M2" microglia nurture synapses and the blood-brain barrier, while destructive "M1" microglia destroy synapses; he says high blood sugar, inflammation, poor sleep, stress, alcohol, social isolation and failing mitochondria shift M2 to M1, and that TSPO PET scans can show this activation in living brains.
- On sleep: he says restorative sleep consolidates memory in REM, activates the glymphatic "street cleaner" system in deep sleep to clear waste, improves insulin function and reduces inflammation, and that even one night of poor sleep dramatically impairs insulin / blood-sugar control, decision-making and amyloid clearance.
- On sugar and diet: he pours out 8 teaspoons of sugar for a 12 oz glass of orange juice (10 for soda), notes barbecue sauce and balsamic glaze at 40–50% sugar and a fat-free French dressing at 35% sugar, and recommends using a continuous glucose monitor for personal feedback — his own CGM unexpectedly spiked on cashews he had overeaten.
- On polyphenols: he says polyphenol-rich foods such as blackberries, blueberries, onions, dark chocolate and Himalayan Tartary buckwheat signal gut bacteria and activate the NRF2 pathway, raising antioxidant production and lowering inflammation; he names coffee as the No. 1 polyphenol source by consumption, extra virgin olive oil as a key Mediterranean-diet component, and dark chocolate above 80% cacao.
- On metabolic risk: he corrects a 300–400% figure to say more recent data support at least a 75–85% increased Alzheimer's risk with type 2 diabetes, and about an 85% increased Parkinson's risk in type 2 diabetics; he describes visceral fat around organs as an organ secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines.
- On alcohol: he says the old U-shaped "sweet spot" (1 glass of wine a day for women, 2 for men) has not held up and any alcohol is probably not good for the brain because alcohol is directly toxic to neurons and shifts microglia to M1; he cites his book's study that binge-level alcohol for 24 hours cut microglia's ability to clear beta-amyloid by 15%.
- On prevention starting early: he cites a study of women who conceived in England in 1953 under post-war sugar rationing, finding children exposed to rationing through pregnancy and the first 1,000 days had about a 40% lower Alzheimer's risk 60–80 years later.
- For people already affected: he cites a Harvard-associated lifestyle study by Tanzi and Ornish of 51 people with Alzheimer's or mild cognitive impairment — exercise, a plant-forward Mediterranean-type diet, stress reduction and 1 hour of meditation a day for 20 weeks — in which 70% stabilised or improved.
- His personal supplements: methylated B vitamins because of an MTHFR variant and elevated homocysteine (which he says applies to probably about 25% of people), and fish oil providing about 1,000 mg a day of DHA omega-3.

## Claims to be careful with
- Perlmutter states his vote is that FDA-approved amyloid-targeting drugs are "for the most part ineffective and significantly dangerous" and "not to be used," citing a Cochrane analysis of 20,342 people in 17 studies over 18 months that he says found no meaningful slowing of progression — this is his contested position against the current approved standard of care, not a consensus statement in the transcript.
- The 40 Hz light and sound approach from Dr. Li-Huei Tsai at MIT — which he says restores gamma rhythm, reverts microglia to supportive, beat amyloid drugs in trials, and which he uses via a device on his desk — is presented from early animal and human research; he acknowledges he cannot prove it is doing anything for him personally.
- The ketogenic-diet figures read out in the episode — ketones rescuing roughly 10–18% of the brain's total energy supply, closing up to 80% of the fuel gap from impaired glucose metabolism, with up to 15% improvement in memory test scores and up to 20% gain in daily functioning — are strong claims attributed to clinical-trial summaries in the conversation, and he notes keto is hard to adhere to and metabolic flexibility is his actual recommendation.
- His claims that glyphosate sprayed on wheat in America is a mitochondrial toxin affecting gut bacteria and mitochondria, and that non-celiac gluten sensitivity is much more common than celiac disease (illustrated by a single headache patient who improved gluten-free), go beyond the settled evidence presented in the episode.
- An intranasal erythropoietin study from Havana, Cuba, in which Alzheimer's patients improved 4 points on a standard scale while placebo declined 4 points, is described by him as a relatively small, promising study — not an established treatment.
- He discusses metformin favourably as mimicking some effects of exercise, but the episode itself notes the consensus against routine metformin use in healthy non-diabetics, including that it can blunt exercise adaptations and cause B12 deficiency; he says he prefers exercise and is not recommending everyone take it.

## Summary
Board-certified neurologist Dr. David Perlmutter, whose brain-surgeon father died after about 5 years with Alzheimer's, argues dementia is largely a preventable metabolic and brain-immune disorder: when metabolism, inflammation and mitochondrial function fail, supportive M2 microglia become destructive M1 cells that destroy synapses. His prescription is to reclaim agency and act in your 30s and 40s — exercise (aerobic plus resistance), restorative sleep, social connection, and a polyphenol-rich, low-sugar diet monitored by blood-sugar measures such as A1C or a CGM — while avoiding alcohol, visceral fat and ultra-processed food, and he closes by urging people to "reconnect" with each other, themselves and the planet.
