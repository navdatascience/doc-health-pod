## Important tidbits
- Cole defines functional medicine against conventional medicine: conventional care is trained to "diagnose a disease and match it with a medication," while functional medicine interprets labs against narrower "optimal" ranges rather than population reference ranges, runs more comprehensive labs, and hunts root causes — gut problems, chronic infections, nutrient deficiencies, hormonal imbalances, trauma and shame. His litmus test for any treatment: "the most effective option that causes you the least amount of side effects."
- He says about 50 million Americans have an autoimmune condition, with millions more on an "autoimmune inflammation spectrum" (chronic fatigue, fibromyalgia, brain fog) who are often told they are "just depressed" or "just stressed" — what he calls medical gaslighting. Hashimoto's thyroid disease is one of his clinic's top patient groups.
- His book *Gut Feelings* is built on the "cytokine model of cognitive function": inflammation affects how the brain works, so anxiety, depression, brain fog and fatigue have an inflammatory component. About 75% of the immune system is in the gut (he also says two-thirds later), and the gut and brain form from the same fetal tissue and stay linked via the gut-brain axis.
- Neurotransmitter figures he cites: 95% of serotonin and 50% of dopamine are made/stored in the gut, and 20% of thyroid hormone is converted in the gut in the presence of healthy bacteria. The gut holds upwards of 100 trillion bacteria, and gut bacteria imbalances can drive food cravings.
- He describes a three-stage inflammation spectrum: stage 1, silent inflammation/autoimmunity (markers like high-sensitivity CRP and homocysteine are off but the person feels fine); stage 2, inflammation reactivity — brain fog, fatigue, "wired and tired," digestive problems, anxiety/depression, hormonal issues — where he says most people sit; stage 3, a full diagnosable condition. Researchers estimate problems brew 4 to 10 years before diagnosis.
- His root-cause frame is "evolutionary mismatch": human genetics haven't changed in about 10,000 years, but food, stress, trauma, toxins and soil-microbiome depletion now trigger dormant genetic predispositions. He uses a bucket analogy — you can't change your bucket size (genetic tolerance) but you can change what you put in it.
- "Shameflation" is his coined term for how shame, chronic stress and trauma raise inflammation. He cites a study in which people doing public speaking and maths tests had elevated interleukin-6, higher still on day two, while those practising self-compassion had the lowest inflammation levels. His clinic gives every telehealth patient an ACE (adverse childhood experiences) score, and higher scores track with later autoimmune, mental health and metabolic problems.
- Intergenerational trauma: he cites research on the Ukrainian famine (Holodomor) and the Holocaust showing descendants carried the same methylation gene variants as survivors, with increased likelihood of mental health issues, autoimmune issues, type 2 diabetes and hormonal problems — but adds "as trauma can be inherited, so can healing."
- Polyvagal theory: the vagus nerve is the main parasympathetic ("rest and digest") nerve; most people have weak vagal tone and an overactive sympathetic fight-or-flight system, on a spectrum running to dysautonomia. He estimates 60–80% of Western countries have major metabolic issues and that the vast majority of people sit between the stressed and hypervigilant states.
- His "inflammatory core four plus one" foods: (1) gluten-containing grains (wheat, rye, barley, spelt — he blames hybridisation, spraying, poor preparation and overconsumption more than the grain itself); (2) industrial seed oils (canola, vegetable, soybean — a problem of overconsumption and omega-3/6/9 ratio imbalance, not inherently bad); (3) conventional dairy (most milk is now inflammatory beta-A1 casein rather than ancestral A2; fermented dairy like kefir, cheese and yoghurt is more digestible); (4) sugar, including euphemisms like agave nectar — watch grams of added sugar per day; plus (5) alcohol, which he calls a gut saboteur and neurotoxin linked to leaky gut and lower brain volume/hippocampus size even at small amounts a few times a week.
- Gut-care protocol: soups and stews from the GAPS protocol (gut and psychology/physiology syndrome) — broths with cooked vegetables and meats as a "proverbial siesta for your gut"; supermarket list of fibre-rich vegetables, a favourite protein (chicken, grass-fed beef, fish, plant-based), bone broth or plant-based broth (galangal, ginger, seaweed), plus fermented foods (sauerkraut, kimchi, kefir) started "low and slow."
- "Acts of stillness" to support vagal tone and the microbiome: breathwork, meditation, forest bathing (shinrin-yoku, which he says research shows lowers inflammation and stress hormones and improves the microbiome) and hygge — consistency matters more than which one you pick.
- His own story: vegan for 10 years, he quit because of fatigue, brain fog and digestive problems, and deficiencies in bioavailable iron, B vitamins and true vitamin A (retinol, unavailable from plants). He has a double MTHFR gene variant impairing folate conversion and homocysteine clearance; adding wild-caught fish, grass-fed beef and bone-broth soups fixed his symptoms "100%," he says. He now eats predominantly plant-based but omnivore.
- On juice detoxes: he is sceptical — the lack of fibre means days of unbuffered fructose; whole-food smoothies keep the fibre. Any benefit of a cleanse is mostly from not eating junk for 7 days, and it only works with a long-term plan after.
- His family's routine: his 16-year-old son does morning intermittent fasting ("time-compressed feeding") for metabolic flexibility, breaks the fast around lunch with vegetables, clean protein and healthy fats (avocado, extra-virgin olive oil), and meditates. Cole cites US Surgeon General Dr Vivek Murthy's position that children under 14 shouldn't have social media; his son got a phone at 16.

## Claims to be careful with
- Functional medicine itself, and several of its signature tools used here (narrower "optimal" lab ranges, MTHFR-based personalisation, the GAPS protocol, "leaky gut"), are not part of mainstream medical consensus, and Cole acknowledges the field gets called "quacks or woo-woo" by conventional doctors.
- The intergenerational-trauma epigenetics he cites (Holodomor and Holocaust descendant methylation studies) is a real but young and contested research area; effect sizes and mechanisms in humans are not settled, and his phrasing ("trauma is literally stored in the cells") goes beyond what the studies show.
- Claims that seed oils, gluten and A1 dairy are broadly inflammatory for the general population are debated; Cole himself hedges that the issue is overconsumption and modern processing rather than the foods being inherently harmful.
- The statement that even small amounts of alcohol a few times a week shrink the hippocampus reflects observational associations, not proven causation.
- Gut-bacteria causation of cravings, and bacteria "causing" specific food desires, is an emerging research area presented here more definitively than the evidence supports.
- Cole sells books, a telehealth clinic and wellness programmes, and is a paid expert for brands like Goop, so his recommendations carry a commercial interest.

## Summary
Functional medicine doctor Will Cole explains his root-cause approach to the autoimmune and inflammation "spectrum," arguing that mental health is physical health: the gut (home to most of the immune system and serotonin production) and the emotions (shame, chronic stress, even inherited trauma) jointly drive chronic inflammation. He lays out his inflammatory "core four plus one" foods, vagal-tone and gut-healing practices like soups, breathwork and forest bathing, and his own journey from a decade of veganism back to omnivory — all framed by bioindividuality and an "evolutionary mismatch" between unchanged genes and a changed world.
