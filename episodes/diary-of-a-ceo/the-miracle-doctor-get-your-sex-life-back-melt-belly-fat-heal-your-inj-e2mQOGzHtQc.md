## Important tidbits

- Pelz's core thesis: the body is a self-healing organism and nobody cures you — "you cure yourself." Illness, in her framing, comes from physical, emotional, spiritual or chemical "interference" with that self-healing ability, and fasting is her primary tool for removing it.
- She describes two metabolisms: a "sugar burner" system when you eat and a "fat burner" system when you don't. The metabolic switch begins at roughly 8 hours without food; by about 12 hours the body makes a good dose of ketones, which she says feed the brain (she claims the brain needs 50% ketones, 50% glucose), sharpen mental clarity and switch off the hunger hormone.
- Her fasting timeline: at ~15 hours growth hormone rises (the hormone that "keeps you young and helps you burn fat") and, she says, research shows a 1,300% increase in testosterone in men from a 13–15-hour fast, while inflammation falls and stiff joints ease. At ~17 hours autophagy begins — the body recycles senescent ("bad") cells, including, she claims, cells turning into cancer, and strengthens usable ones; she cites Dr Ohsumi's Nobel Prize for autophagy research.
- She busts two dietary myths: "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" was, she says, a Kellogg's cornflakes advertising tagline from the 1970s with no research behind it, and the idea that eating six meals a day speeds up metabolism has no evidence — a faster metabolism comes from learning to switch between sugar-burning and fat-burning.
- Humans evolved for "feast–famine cycling": primal ancestors hunted in a fasted, ketone-fuelled state (ketones also raise GABA, producing calm focus), then feasted. Her practical hack mirrors this: exercise fasted to drain stored muscle glucose, then eat ~30 g of protein to trigger mTOR and muscle growth.
- You can have abs and still be metabolically unhealthy — "skinny fat," with excess sugar driving fat around the liver. She says haemoglobin A1C should be under 5; above that, sugar "gums up" red blood cells so they can't deliver oxygen properly.
- From her book, she quotes CDC-style statistics for women: 41% of women 21 and older are obese, 45% have high blood pressure, 1 in 2 will develop cancer in their lifetimes, 1 in 5 Alzheimer's, 1 in 9 type 2 diabetes, 1 in 8 a thyroid problem — and 80% of all autoimmune conditions occur in women, which she attributes largely to toxins and hormonal disruption.
- Her "hormonal hierarchy": oxytocin sits on top and calms cortisol; cortisol drives insulin resistance; insulin resistance throws off the sex hormones (estrogen, progesterone, testosterone). Hence her claim that "women are perishing in this modern world more than men," and that restoring insulin sensitivity and managing stress calms the immune system.
- Her signature line: "When cortisol goes high, progesterone becomes shy." Because stress suppresses progesterone, women shouldn't fast, race or overtrain in the week before their period — that week should prioritise sleep, yoga and recovery. She endorses Spain's 3-day menstrual leave and suggests using it in the days *before* the period starts.
- Her cycle guide for partners: a cycle runs 28–32 days, with day 1 the first day of bleeding (lasting 3–7 days). Give her space on days 1–2; resolve conflicts between days 2 and 12 while estrogen builds; libido peaks around ovulation (days 10–15, when she has the most testosterone); expect a low patch after day 15, and from about day 17–18, as progesterone comes in, "cater to her like the queen she deserves to be." For men, she jokes, hormones are simpler — testosterone pulses roughly every 15 minutes on a 24-hour cycle.
- On cardio: chronic steady cardio can break down muscle (her contrast is a marathon runner versus a tennis or soccer player), so weight loss should come from weight lifting (more muscle = more insulin receptor sites), fasting to clear stored glucose, and breaking the fast with protein. She does cardio herself for mental health, not weight loss.
- Fasting only *appears* to shrink muscle because it empties stored sugar; followed by a high-protein meal it rebuilds stronger. Followed by poor eating, muscle genuinely is lost.
- Waking at 2–3 a.m. is often a blood-sugar event: after a late dinner, falling glucose triggers a cortisol spike that jolts you awake ("we're running from a tiger"). Not eating late and fasting to stabilise blood sugar are her fixes.
- For people with eating disorders she is explicit: don't fast unsupervised — bring a therapist and doctor in. In her own practice she started one client with food and blood-sugar stabilisation first, adding fasting only later.
- Her starter prescription for Bartlett (who was eating in a 2 p.m.–midnight window): compress to an 8-hour eating window that ends before dark (he chose 1–9 p.m.), elongate the fast to ~24 hours / one meal a day once a week, and use MCT-oil coffee or pure-fat "fasted snacks" (e.g. keto cups) to ease into ketosis. Her closing philosophy: be your own study — "n of one."

## Claims to be careful with

- Many headline numbers are Pelz's own claims presented without named studies in the episode — notably the 1,300% testosterone increase from a 13–15-hour fast and the assertion that the brain "needs" 50% ketones / 50% glucose. The testosterone figure in particular is far beyond what mainstream fasting research reports.
- Her statement that fasting "literally takes cells that are turning into cancer and gets rid of those" extrapolates autophagy research well beyond established clinical evidence; autophagy is a real, Nobel-recognised mechanism, but fasting is not a proven cancer treatment. (She also dates Ohsumi's Nobel Prize to 2015; it was awarded in 2016.)
- The "hormonal hierarchy," the idea that a missed period is the body failing to "detox," and claims like "progesterone can't handle stress" are her functional-medicine framework, simplified well beyond endocrinology consensus.
- The Kellogg's origin story for "breakfast is the most important meal of the day" is a popular anecdote she repeats; the phrase's history is more diffuse than a single 1970s tagline.
- Her women's-health statistics are quoted from her own book conversationally; treat them as approximate. She also sells books, programmes and clinic services built around fasting, and her advice (e.g. HbA1c "should be under 5") is stricter than standard clinical thresholds.

## Summary

Dr Mindy Pelz, author of *Fast Like a Girl*, argues that the body heals itself once interference is removed, and that fasting is the cheapest way to do it: switching the body from sugar-burning to fat-burning produces ketones, growth hormone and autophagy, melts belly fat, and — especially for women — rebalances the oxytocin–cortisol–insulin–sex-hormone hierarchy that she blames for much of modern female ill-health. She maps fasting and stress around the menstrual cycle, gives partners a day-by-day guide to that cycle, and prescribes an 8-hour eating window plus a weekly 24-hour fast as the entry point, while cautioning that anyone with an eating disorder should only fast under professional supervision.
