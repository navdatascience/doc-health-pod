## Important tidbits

- Haver says about a third of the world's female population — 1.2 billion women, with roughly 47 million more entering perimenopause each year — is in peri-, full or postmenopause. Average age of menopause (defined as one year after the last period) is 51, with a normal range of 45–55, and perimenopause can begin 7–10 years earlier, so symptoms starting at 35 are completely reasonable.
- Women are born with their full egg supply (peaking at five months in the womb); by age 30 about 10% remains and by 40 about 3%. As the supply fails, cycles enter what she calls the "zone of chaos" — irregular or very heavy periods, surging then vanishing estrogen, and declining testosterone driving muscle loss and sexual changes.
- There are estrogen receptors in every organ system, and her community has catalogued roughly 70 symptoms: brain fog, weight gain centred on the midsection (visceral fat), sleep disruption, skin/hair/nail changes, vertigo, tinnitus, dental and inner-ear changes — plus the musculoskeletal syndrome of menopause: frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis, which can take ~2 years of therapy to break up), joint aches and pains, because estrogen is a powerful anti-inflammatory hormone.
- Menopause independently raises risk of cardiovascular disease, diabetes and insulin resistance (she notes how many women's cholesterol "shot up" in their 30s and 40s with no change in diet or exercise), and it brings new onset or worsening of depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder and ADHD. She says hormone therapy started in perimenopause is associated with lower incidence of new-onset depression and can rescue an SSRI that has stopped working; there is also an uptick in suicide among perimenopausal and menopausal women, particularly Caucasian women in the US.
- The treatment gap is stark: citing 2023 FDA figures, 85% of women complain of menopausal symptoms but only 10.5% receive treatment or therapy, and a woman is more likely to be prescribed an antidepressant for her menopause than hormone therapy. Haver admits she herself was "a horrible menopause provider for probably 15 years" — she received perhaps 6 hours of menopause lectures in a 4-year residency, and residents used the code "WW" ("whiny woman") for midlife patients with long symptom lists.
- On the Women's Health Initiative: the study (stopped in 2002, average participant age 63) found in the estrogen-plus-progestin arm an absolute breast cancer risk rising from 4 to 5 per 1,000 women per year — a 25% relative increase that was announced as "estrogen causes breast cancer." In the estrogen-only arm, she says, women had about a 20% decreased risk of breast cancer and ~40% lower relative mortality. Her position: estrogen may feed an existing breast cancer cell but does not cause breast cancer, and the study's reporting has been walked back.
- Timing matters: the "window of opportunity" is starting hormone therapy in perimenopause or within the first 10 years of menopause, when it can reduce risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease and dementia — estrogen is "better at prevention than cure." Women on HRT have lower all-cause mortality in the observational data and the WHI, per her account.
- Route matters too: oral estrogen passes through the liver and bumps clotting factors, while non-oral forms (patch, gel/cream, ring, pellet) bypass the liver and do not carry that increased clotting risk. Vaginal estrogen is local therapy, not systemically absorbed, and is the most effective treatment for genitourinary syndrome of menopause — thin tissue, painful intercourse and recurrent UTIs, which are a major cause of death in older women — and she says it is safe for everyone, even breast cancer patients. Side effects to expect: headaches (a red flag), possible migraine worsening, some vaginal bleeding in ~40% of patients (usually self-limited), and ~10% reacting to patch adhesive.
- Muscle and bone are the longevity stakes: women can lose 10–15% of muscle mass in the first 10 years of menopause, muscle mass drives insulin sensitivity and fall recovery, and low muscle accelerates osteoporosis. Peak bone density is reached around age 35; 50% of women will have an osteoporotic fracture before they die; of women who break a hip, she states 30% die within a year with surgery and 70% without. Her prescription: resistance training ("strength over skinny"), higher protein, creatine (studies in over-65s show bigger muscle and strength gains when creatine is combined with lifting), vitamin D, and hormone therapy.
- Nutrition is "always first" in her toolkit (the Galveston Diet): keep added sugars under 25 g, and fix fiber — most women get ~12 g/day against a 25 g minimum. Soluble fiber feeds the gut microbiome, whose compounds entering the bloodstream are associated with less coronary artery disease and dementia. About 85% of her patients are vitamin D deficient, so she checks levels on everyone; deficiency is linked to hypertension, diabetes, stroke and poor mental health.
- She is a fan of 16:8 intermittent fasting — 16 hours fasting, 8-hour eating window, based on Mark Mattson's research — for systemic and neuro-inflammation and insulin benefits, not reliable weight loss. Build up in 15-minute increments over ~6 weeks. She has fasted since about 2014–15, typically breaks her fast around noon, finishes eating by 8 p.m., eats protein-centrically with nuts and seeds through the day, and no longer counts calories — she tracks fiber, vitamin D, magnesium and protein instead. (She notes fasting is not for everyone, e.g. people with diabetes, hypoglycemia or eating-disorder history.)
- Her clinical workup rules out mimics first — thyroid disease, autoimmune conditions and nutrient deficiencies can all look like perimenopause — with a full blood panel, then treats immediately. On sexual health she works through five buckets (relationship, arousal, orgasm, desire, and pain/function); about 10% of women never have an orgasm; testosterone can help desire but has no FDA-approved female formulation, so she compounds it; Addyi and Vyleesi are FDA-approved alternatives.
- Her case study: a patient who arrived overweight, not sleeping, with brain fog, aching joints and lost desire combined a nutrition plan, a personal trainer and serious lifting with hormone therapy — and lost roughly 60 lbs of body fat while gaining ~10 lbs of muscle (measured on an in-body scanner), with her intimacy restored. The patient drove 3.5 hours from San Antonio because she couldn't find menopause care locally; Haver's own clinic waiting list is "longer than this wall," and she maintains a follower-recommended provider list on her website alongside The Menopause Society's certified-provider list.
- For partners: don't diagnose, but say it with love and gently — bring it up, find a provider, and come to the appointment; her best visits are with partners present. A divorce-attorney patient of hers believes a significant percentage of divorces are menopause-driven, through women no longer tolerating one-sided emotional and organisational labour. There is no male equivalent: men's testosterone peaks around 19, declines slowly, then stays broadly stable from ~35–40.
- Her personal stakes: one of eight children, she lost her oldest brother to childhood leukemia, her brother Bob in 2015 (HIV, hepatitis, alcoholism, stroke — she mistook her own menopause symptoms for grief while doing his end-of-life care), and her brother Jude to stage 4 esophageal cancer at 57; at 55 she is determined to see her grandkids. Her daily toolkit includes 5–10 minutes of morning meditation with the Headspace app and gratitude practice, therapy, sleep treated as the first priority (sleep apnea rates also rise in menopausal women, even thin ones), incline-treadmill work with a weighted vest, and lifting. Her mantra: "Menopause is inevitable. Suffering is not" — but women will have to advocate for themselves.

## Claims to be careful with

- Haver is one of the world's most prominent hormone-therapy advocates and sells the Galveston Diet program and books (*The Galveston Diet*, *The New Menopause*) and runs a menopause clinic; her framing consistently favours HRT and her own toolkit.
- Her WHI reinterpretation — including "estrogen doesn't cause breast cancer" and the protective framing of the estrogen-only arm — is stronger than mainstream guidance, which still treats combined HRT's breast cancer risk, and the "timing hypothesis," with more caution and individualisation.
- Many statistics are stated conversationally as she presents them (the 85%/10.5% treatment figures, menopause research funding of ~$15 million, the 30%/70% hip-fracture mortality figures, the 50% lifetime fracture figure) and were not independently verified in the episode.
- The fasting and neuroinflammation benefits lean substantially on animal research (Mattson's maze studies), which she partially acknowledges; testosterone therapy for women is off-label and compounded, with long-term safety data still limited.

## Summary

OB-GYN Dr Mary Claire Haver argues that menopause is a whole-body, decades-long event affecting 1.2 billion women that medicine has systematically under-taught, under-funded and dismissed — leaving most symptomatic women untreated or on antidepressants. She lays out the biology (egg depletion, estrogen loss, rising inflammation), re-litigates the Women's Health Initiative as a misreported study that scared a generation off hormone therapy, and prescribes a toolkit of early hormone therapy, strength training, protein, creatine, fiber, vitamin D, fasting and sleep — underpinned by her own family's losses and her mantra that menopause is inevitable but suffering is not.
