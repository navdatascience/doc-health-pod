## Important tidbits
- Huberman's framework is the cortisol curve: you wake because of a "cortisol awakening response," and you want the highest possible cortisol peak in the first 60 minutes of the day, because a high morning peak is what sets up low cortisol in the afternoon and night, which is required for falling and staying asleep. A flattened cortisol curve, he says, is associated with shorter lifespan and longer, bigger stress responses.
- Protocol 1 is hydration: 16–32 oz of water on waking (electrolytes optional) and roughly 80 oz across the day; being well hydrated sharpens the mind and helps boost morning cortisol.
- Protocol 2 is light: view sunlight — or daylight, even on an overcast day — within the first 30–60 minutes of waking, because low-solar-angle light optimally stimulates the melanopsin ganglion cells that set the suprachiasmatic (master circadian) clock. If you can't get daylight, use a 10,000 lux artificial light; if you miss a day, go out for twice as long the next day.
- Bright days and dark nights are additive, he says, citing UK Biobank data: the risk of dying early rises "immensely" with bright nights, and by the studies' definition anyone on devices after about 6 p.m. counts as a night-shift worker.
- Getting daylight in your eyes in the late afternoon buffers the eye against artificial light at night — his "Netflix inoculation" — but he says this buffering does not work for adolescents.
- Long-wavelength (red/infrared) light penetrates up to about 8 cm into the body and improves mitochondrial function: he cites Glen Jeffrey's lab at UCL, where red light shone on people's backs cut the peak of a glucose-tolerance test by more than 25%, and where people over 40 who looked at red light for 1–3 minutes a few times a week, in the first 3 hours of the day, improved their vision.
- Some UVB exposure on skin each day increases testosterone and estrogen in both men and women and supports libido, he says — the goal is full-spectrum sunlight when the UV index is low, not sunburn.
- Protocol 3 is exercise in the first three hours of the day: alternate resistance and cardio days — three lifting days built on compound movements with two to three work sets per exercise, and three cardio days (one ~1 hour slow, one ~30 minutes moderate, one short max-heart-rate interval session). A 45-minute weights session taken near failure triples or quadruples cortisol, which he frames as good: cortisol is an energy-mobilising "preparation hormone," not simply a stress hormone.
- Deliberate cold exposure, he insists, does not meaningfully raise cortisol — increases are trivial in men and women — but produces huge increases in adrenaline, noradrenaline and dopamine, and trains you to stay calm while flooded with adrenaline. In the first 20–30 seconds of any stressor, he says, your prefrontal cortex is suppressed — "you are brain damaged" — so the skill is to not react until it comes back online.
- Doing hard things you don't want to do grows the anterior mid-cingulate cortex, the brain's "tenacity" area, making future hard things easier — the neurological case for deliberately choosing discomfort.
- The fastest real-time stress tool, from his lab's study (with Melis Yilmaz Balaban and David Spiegel, published in Cell Reports), is the physiological sigh — a double inhale followed by a long exhale. Five minutes a day of cyclic sighing beat meditation, box breathing and cyclic hyperventilation for stress reduction, and even a single sigh slows heart rate immediately via respiratory sinus arrhythmia, because exhales engage the calming arm of the vagus nerve.
- To fall back asleep at 2–3 a.m., he recommends 10 long deliberate exhales plus an eye-movement routine with eyes closed — move them side to side, up, down, then in counterclockwise and clockwise circles, then point them toward the nostrils and do a long exhale — which he says shuts down the cerebellum's proprioceptive circuits so you stop sensing your limb position.
- Sleep clears waste from the brain via the glymphatic system, and side sleeping with the head slightly elevated improves that clearance — also the reason poor sleep leaves bags under the eyes. He cites a PNAS study in which a short meditation increased daytime glymphatic clearance, and says regular meditators can shave about an hour off their sleep with 20 minutes of daily meditation.
- For learning, he prescribes one effortful 90-minute to 2-hour bout daily, with the phone out of the room entirely — a study he cites found a phone anywhere in the room reduces cognitive flexibility even when off. Plasticity is driven by errors at roughly a 15% error rate, and self-testing beats re-reading: students who read once and self-tested retained far more at six months and two years than re-readers.
- His five supplements, if forced to choose: magnesium threonate or bisglycinate for sleep; at least 1 g per day of EPA omega-3 (he recommends prescription Lovaza); vitamin D at 5,000–10,000 IU per day; alpha-GPC 600–900 mg for focus (paired with a 600 mg garlic capsule to counter the TMAO risk); and creatine 5–10 g per day. He also notes 200–400 mg of caffeine per day has been shown to offset dementia, while stressing the data are correlative, not causal.
- Walking matters more than step counts: he aims for 7,000–8,000 steps a day, and cites the "soleus push-up" study showing the soleus — about 1% of total musculature — has an outsized effect on blood-glucose regulation; a 5–10 minute walk after meals flattens glucose peaks. The age-related "metabolism slowdown," he says, is mostly people moving less, not ageing itself.

## Claims to be careful with
- His peptide discussion is self-experimentation and anecdote: pinealon for REM sleep, a friend microdosing tirzepatide every 10 days for chronic fatigue, and BPC-157 healing claims rest largely on animal data; he himself notes the concern that BPC-157 could vascularise tumours, and retatrutide was not an approved drug.
- The Swedish finding that smokers who got lots of sunlight outlived non-smokers who got little is an observational study, not evidence that sunlight offsets smoking.
- The red-light glucose and vision results come mainly from one laboratory (Glen Jeffrey's) and are early-stage findings, not established clinical practice.
- His dismissal of claims that GLP-1 drugs are causing widespread blindness is his expert opinion in a live scientific dispute; he acknowledges real cases in people with a structural abnormality of the optic nerve head.
- Vitamin D at 5,000–10,000 IU per day is well above standard recommended allowances; he acknowledges the pushback and that blood testing is the usual advice.
- The eye-movement sleep trick has, by his own words, only "indirect scientific support," and the claim that meditation can substitute for an hour of sleep rests on a single cited study.
- The "15% error rate" for optimal learning is a computational-modelling figure, not a precise prescription for every learner.

## Summary
Neuroscientist Andrew Huberman walks Steven Bartlett through the ten protocols from his book *Protocols* — morning hydration, sunlight, early exercise, deliberate discomfort, physiological sighing, afternoon light, dark nights, deep focused work, and side sleeping — all organised around one idea: spike cortisol early and keep it low at night. Along the way he covers red light and mitochondria, cold exposure, glymphatic clearance, peptides, his five core supplements, walking and glucose control, and a long detour into his nightly prayer practice and faith.
