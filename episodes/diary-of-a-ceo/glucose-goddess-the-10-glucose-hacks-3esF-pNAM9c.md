# Glucose Goddess: The 10 Glucose Hacks!

- **Source:** The Diary Of A CEO
- **Guest:** Jessie Inchauspé, biochemist and best-selling author known as the "Glucose Goddess"
- **YouTube:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3esF-pNAM9c

## Important tidbits

- Inchauspé says 1 billion people worldwide have type 2 diabetes or pre-diabetes, and that up to 80% of the population experiences glucose spikes every single day, even without diabetes.
- She describes three processes triggered by big glucose spikes: mitochondrial stress and dysfunction (producing fatigue and free radicals/inflammation), glycation (the "browning" process that accelerates aging), and insulin release.
- The brief "rush" after eating sugar is dopamine — pleasure, not energy — while on the inside the mitochondria are being overwhelmed, she says.
- There is no such thing as "good sugar," according to Inchauspé: the body does not differentiate between sugar in a fruit smoothie and sugar in a chocolate cake, and modern supermarket fruit has been bred to be far sweeter than ancestral fruit.
- Hack 1 — eat a savory breakfast: she says it prevents the late-morning exhaustion, coffee dependence and brain fog that follow a sweet breakfast.
- Hack 2 — vinegar: 1 tablespoon of any kitchen vinegar in a big glass of water, drunk about 10 minutes before the biggest meal of the day, cuts the glucose and insulin spikes of that meal by up to 30%, because acetic acid temporarily inactivates some digestive enzymes and slows glucose arrival in the blood. It must be diluted to protect tooth enamel, and sweetened vinegar drinks should be avoided.
- She says studies also link vinegar/acetic acid to benefits for visceral fat (the fat between the organs) and cholesterol levels.
- Hack 3 — veggie starter: eating vegetables first puts fiber into the gut that forms a protective mesh coating the intestine, slowing how fast glucose from the rest of the meal reaches the bloodstream — the principle behind antipasti and crudités traditions.
- Hack 4 — move for 10 minutes after eating (walk, tidy up, or do calf raises under a desk): muscles soak up glucose from the blood, and the calf's soleus muscle is especially good at it.
- Eating food in the right order — veggies first, then proteins and fats, then starches and sugars last — reduces the glucose spike of the identical meal by up to 75%, she says.
- "Clothes on carbs": never eat starches or sugars on their own; pair them with fat, protein or fiber (bread with avocado, carrot cake with Greek yogurt). Related hacks: choose savory snacks between meals, eat sweets as dessert after a meal rather than on an empty stomach, and eat fruit whole — never juiced, dried or smoothied.
- In her four-week pilot experiment with about 2,700 people recruited online from 110 countries (ages 20–70), layering in the four core hacks week by week, 90% of participants reported reduced cravings and 90% reported more energy, and roughly 40% of those who wanted to lose weight did — with no calorie restriction or foods banned.
- She explains the weight effect via insulin: when insulin is present in the body you cannot burn fat, so reducing spikes lowers insulin, hunger and cravings.
- She cites rat research in which more glucose spikes were associated with slower signal speed between neurons, offered as a possible mechanism for brain fog and the post-meal "food coma."
- She cites a 30-year study finding that people with elevated fasting glucose in their 40s were more likely to develop Alzheimer's later, and notes some scientists call Alzheimer's "type 3 diabetes" because of shared inflammation, glycation and insulin resistance.
- Her popular CGM graphs are, she stresses, illustrations of published clinical studies using her own or her team's data — she says she does not draw conclusions from her own glucose readings.

## Claims to be careful with

- Her 2,700-person experiment was self-reported, recruited from her own Instagram audience, and had no control group or placebo — she acknowledges this herself in the episode, so the 90% figures should not be read as clinical-trial results.
- The links she draws between glucose spikes and Alzheimer's, PCOS, infertility and aging are largely associational; "type 3 diabetes" is a label used by some researchers, not an official diagnosis.
- Individual glucose responses vary a lot between people, and her CGM demonstrations are single-person illustrations.
- The visceral-fat and cholesterol claims for vinegar rest on a small number of studies; undiluted vinegar can damage tooth enamel and irritate the throat.
- Inchauspé has strong commercial interests: best-selling books (her work has sold over 3 million copies), a large social-media business, and an "anti-spike" supplement formula promoted at the end of the episode.

## Summary

Biochemist Jessie Inchauspé returns to argue that everyday glucose spikes — experienced by most people, diabetic or not — drive fatigue, cravings, brain fog, faster aging and long-term disease risk through mitochondrial stress, glycation and insulin. Rather than banning foods, she offers ten sequencing and timing "hacks": a savory breakfast, a tablespoon of diluted vinegar before the biggest meal, a veggie starter, ten minutes of movement after eating, eating foods in the order veggies → protein/fat → starches/sugars, putting "clothes" on carbs, savory snacks, sweets only as dessert, and whole fruit only. She reports striking self-reported results from her 2,700-person pilot and is candid that it was not a controlled trial.
