## Important tidbits
- New fathers' brains lose grey matter volume — about 1% in Dr Saxby's studies — concentrated in the "mentalizing network" (the midline cortex that helps us read other people's feelings and intentions) and in visual areas used to read an infant's face. Mothers lose more, around 2.5% of brain volume across pregnancy and birth, with additional changes in the subcortex tied to hormones and reward.
- The pattern differs by sex: mothers change all across the brain, while fathers change mainly in the cortex, the higher-order thinking layer. She calls fatherhood a "facultative" adaptation — dads calibrate their investment to culture and context (some cultures even prohibit men holding babies in the first year), whereas mothers have a high hormonal "floor" of investment.
- Men typically show about a 25% drop in testosterone around the transition to fatherhood, alongside rising prolactin.
- The "dad bod" has biological drivers: prolactin, sleep deprivation, reduced physical activity and stress-driven diet changes. In a tamarin monkey study, expectant fathers gained more weight than non-expectant males even on an identical standardized diet — suggesting the weight gain is partly adaptive (size deters rivals; fat buffers against scarcity).
- Studies of single women shown a series of male body types found the dad bod the most popular: women read it as a signal that the man is more likely to be a hands-on, attentive, caring father.
- Parenthood may be neuroprotective: UK Biobank data show that men and women who report having had more children have markers of a younger-looking brain age, with the greatest benefit at two or three children and a levelling off after that.
- She likens parenting to "Sudoku for your social cognition" — constant practice at reading minds and intentions builds brain reserve. Oxytocin released through bonding is part of the proposed mechanism; in her lab, dads who did more vigorous "proprioceptive" touch (picking babies up, moving them around, physical play) subsequently had higher oxytocin levels.
- That rough-and-tumble paternal play also benefits children: the fear of being thrown in the air followed by the relief of being caught is small-scale practice in emotion regulation and risk tolerance.
- New fathers' depression rates are about twice those of non-fathering men (though lower than new mothers'). Risk factors are the same cluster: hormone changes, sleep loss, identity change, relationship conflict, and unresolved experiences of being parented oneself.
- In her lab's study, couples who fought more negatively during pregnancy — criticism, contempt, stonewalling — went on to have more medically complex births and rated the birth as more stressful. The "magic ingredient" that shifted conflict from negative to positive was validation: simply conveying "I understand what you're saying."
- A drop in couple relationship satisfaction after a baby is one of the most robust findings in parenthood research; afterwards couples' trajectories diverge — some dissolve, others report a better relationship than before.
- Every couple in her study expected the dad to do more baby care before the birth than he actually did afterwards — yet the dads who did more, and who used more "we" language when describing care plans, reported happier relationships after birth.
- She pushes back on the claim that under-threes in daycare suffer "profound separation trauma": humans are cooperative breeders (alloparenting), though she says very young babies do best with a stable caregiver and group care suits children from around 18 months, when social interaction becomes a benefit.
- Humans conceal ovulation, which she says encourages pair bonding — a man maximises his chances by investing in one partner. Humans are "sort of monogamous": our modest male–female size difference sits between fully monogamous species (no size difference) and harem species like gorillas.
- Her advice for new fathers: before the birth, work on communication, explicitly agree how baby care and values questions will be divided, and build a social support network (including dad communities such as the Daddy Stroller Social Club); protect sleep hygiene in advance; afterwards, take bonding opportunities like nappy changes rather than avoiding them, and give yourself grace.
- Context from the conversation: the US total fertility rate has hit a record low of 1.6 (replacement is 2.1), a 2024 Lancet study projects 97% of countries will be below replacement by 2100, and the US still has no universal paid parental leave — FMLA guarantees only 12 weeks unpaid.

## Claims to be careful with
- The neuroprotective case for oxytocin (reduced amyloid-beta toxicity, improved memory in ageing animals) comes from animal studies, mainly mice, not human trials.
- The younger "brain age" in parents is a correlation from UK Biobank data; it does not prove that having children causes the effect.
- The dad-bod preference finding comes from studies asking single women to rate pictured body types — stated preference, not real-world behaviour.
- The ~25% testosterone drop is stated in the episode without the underlying study details being discussed.

## Summary
Psychologist Dr Darby Saxby explains how becoming a father physically rewires men: grey matter shrinks in the brain's mentalizing network, testosterone falls by roughly a quarter, prolactin and weight rise — the "dad bod" — and yet parenthood appears to protect the brain in later life. Her core message is that great fathers are made, not born: the brain changes follow investment and practice, so communication, shared care and community support before and after birth matter more than instinct.
