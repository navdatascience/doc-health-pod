## Important tidbits

- Bublé says his son Noah's cancer diagnosis — which arrived around Halloween and was treated at Children's Hospital Los Angeles — was "a sledgehammer to my reality" that removed the filter he had been living behind in a single moment: "I will never be carefree again in my life, and that's okay." Noah is now 10 years old and doing great. He deliberately keeps the details private because his son has his own life and story.
- Standing in the hospital, he made a promise to himself: "If we get out of this, I'm living a different life. A better life" — kinder, more empathetic, and never letting ego take over. He describes the experience as a privilege to exist through, pain and all.
- He is candid that before the diagnosis his priorities were not straight — career, ambition, ego, money and power came first, behind "blinders." Now his stated order is: faith number one, family number two, and career "a really distant third."
- He talks about the "fault self" (his term for the ego, influenced by Eckhart Tolle's *The Power of Now*) feeding negative messages, and says learning that he could control those messages was helpful — while admitting his priorities still slip at times.
- On men's mental health, he calls out the culture directly: "We don't talk about these things. No, dude, you must be soft. Don't be soft and no crying." He says grief and trauma take a piece of everyone — a child, a sibling, a parent — and that nobody gets away without it.
- He has tried therapy but is unsure it worked for him: it felt good to talk, but after about four sessions he felt he was just filling the hour. What brings him more satisfaction is his relationship with God and being radically honest with his wife, who "calls him out" — which he frames as part of a partner's role.
- To protect family life he tours on a self-imposed rule — three weeks on, two weeks off, two weeks on, two weeks off — which he calls financially one of the most irresponsible touring schedules in the business. He and his wife (an actress) sit down with their managers at the start of each year and plan their calendars around each other.
- His father was a commercial salmon fisherman who was away for long stretches; every two weeks he would queue at a payphone with a sock full of quarters to call home, and he hid notes around the house for the kids to find. Bublé remembers crying while holding his father's clothes as a 7- or 8-year-old, and now replicates that connection from tour — nightly video calls, watching movies with his kids remotely, and his children falling asleep to the sleep story he recorded for the Calm app.
- The career underneath it all took a decade of rejection: 10 years of clubs, fishing boats and restaurant gigs while every agent gave him the same line — "You're really talented… we just don't know what to do with you." Producer David Foster told him, "I will never produce your record and we will never sign you." Bublé raised the recording money himself (he recalls roughly $100,000 a track), a replacement deal via Paul Anka collapsed four songs into making the record, and only after producer Humberto Gatica coached him on exactly what to say did Foster let him pitch Warner's president in person — where his pitch was: "With all due respect, Frank's dead. Don't bury the music. I will keep it alive."
- Even after signing at 26, his debut album entered the Billboard chart at 198, and he built his audience by saying yes to showcases everywhere — the Philippines and South Africa first, then hotel-lobby showcases across Asia and Europe — tasting real fame only around 28–29. He admits there was a fear of losing it, and workaholism with it; Paul Anka still calls him out of the blue to say: don't panic, stay true to yourself, peaks and valleys are normal.
- His grandfather Mitch, a plumber, was his best friend and engine: he traded plumbing work for singing lessons, drove him to auditions and mall gigs paying $20 an hour, and filled their home with the Great American Songbook. Bublé's creative method came from him — imitating Sinatra, Dean Martin, Nat King Cole and the Mills Brothers until, as Tony Bennett told him, "if you steal from one person, you're just a thief; when you steal from everybody, it's research." A recording from age 14 shows his voice was already identical.
- After 20 years of the same cycle — write a record, promote it in 40 countries, tour for a year and a half — he wants to do something different (acting and other projects), distinguishing "Michael Bublé," the Teflon stage persona in the suit, from "Mike," the hockey- and fantasy-football-loving ordinary guy he wants to spend more time being.
- His whiskey, Fraser and Thompson, took three years to develop with expert help; it is named for the two British Columbia rivers of his grandfather's camping trips (his grandfather's 1927 birth year is hidden on the bottle), priced around $35–40 to be approachable, and Ryan Reynolds — whose Aviation Gin shares the same partners — advised him simply to "just have fun" and let the experts do what they know.
- His advice to his four kids on a good life: "Rich isn't what you think it is" — rich is a strong faith, a great family and great friendships; the people he knows with the most stuff are the most miserable. He says he has no regrets and would change nothing. He also had an epiphany in the hospital room about his Christmas fame: being synonymous with a season of kindness and family memory is a legacy — "200 years from now… people are going to be singing Have a Holly Jolly Christmas."

## Summary

Michael Bublé traces a straight line from a music-obsessed childhood in a close fishing family, through ten years of industry rejection and a self-funded breakthrough, to the moment his young son's cancer diagnosis shattered his career-first priorities. He describes rebuilding his life around faith, family and presence — restructuring his tours, confronting the ego he calls his "fault self," and speaking openly about grief and men's reluctance to talk — while still wrestling with fame, fear of losing it, and the pull to become "Mike" again after 20 years of being Michael Bublé.
