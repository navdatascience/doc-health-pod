## Important tidbits

- Eyal's mission: minimise regret. We almost always know what to do (exercise, eat well, be present with family, do the hard work) — the problem is not knowledge but follow-through, which is why he calls becoming "indistractable" the skill of the century: every part of life depends on controlling your attention.
- Distraction defined: from the Latin *trahere*, "to pull." The opposite of distraction is not focus but *traction* — both end in "action," reminding us distraction is something we do, not something that happens to us. Traction is any planned action that pulls you toward your values; distraction is any action that pulls you away from what you said you would do — including work tasks like checking email or Slack instead of the project you sat down to do, which he calls the most pernicious distraction of all because you don't notice it.
- Only ~10% of distractions come from external triggers (pings, dings, phones). The other ~90% are internal triggers — uncomfortable emotional states we seek to escape. His maxim: "Time management is pain management" — and so are weight management and money management, because all human behaviour is driven by a desire to escape discomfort.
- His four-step model: (1) master internal triggers, (2) make time for traction, (3) hack back external triggers, (4) prevent distraction with pacts. The book *Indistractable* took him five years and carries 35 pages of citations.
- He speaks from experience: he was once clinically obese and says he did not overeat from hunger but from loneliness, boredom and guilt about how much he had just eaten — "we're eating our feelings."
- A starter technique for step 1: keep a Post-it note and pen on your desk; when you feel the urge to get distracted, write down the sensation you felt just before (boredom, anxiety, fear, uncertainty). Then reframe it — the discomfort is a signal happening *for* you, not *to* you. High performers feel the same triggers but use them as "rocket fuel" toward traction instead of escaping them.
- Step 2 is timeboxing from values, where values are "attributes of the person you want to become." Schedule three life domains in order: yourself first, then relationships, then work — and within work, protect *reflective* work (thinking, creating) rather than only *reactive* work (messages, requests). Don't give relationships "whatever scraps of time are left over."
- His wife once told him he had turned her into the "residual beneficiary" — the business term for whoever gets whatever is left over. Their fix was a standing Friday date night: scheduled time is the minimum guarantee, and spontaneity can happen on top of it.
- He argues to-do lists are one of the worst productivity tools: they are unconstrained lists of outputs, so you end every day with most items uncrossed, training a self-image of someone who doesn't do what they said. A timeboxed calendar budgets the only real inputs of knowledge work — time and attention — the way a baker budgets ingredients.
- On burnout, he cites research by British researchers Stansfeld and Candy: the jobs that raise rates of depression and anxiety combine *high expectations with low control*. High expectations with high control is fine; high expectations without agency produces learned helplessness — "it's giving up."
- On ADHD, his headline concern: 10% of children in the United States are diagnosed versus about 1% in Europe — "a big red flag." He insists ADHD is real and can be debilitating, but argues for "skills before pills": stimulant medications are amphetamines with side effects, and jumping straight to them trains a generation to believe solutions come in pill bottles.
- He rejects the dopamine-deficiency justification: the chemical imbalance theory is, he says, "scientifically false" — there is no brain scan or blood test for ADHD, and diagnosis rests on a short, "completely gameable" symptom checklist that doctors may rubber-stamp to avoid bad reviews.
- He demands an "undiagnosis plan": like a cast that comes off a healed arm, treatment should aim at making the condition functionally stop debilitating you, through medication, behavioural skills or both. He hates ADHD being treated as an identity — comparing it to addiction, which he frames as a temporary confluence of the person, their pain and the product, not a lifelong identity ("addict" derives from *addictio*, Latin for slave).
- He blames TikTok-style content and recency bias for driving over-diagnosis ("if you lost your keys, that's ADHD"), and notes the psychology and pharmaceutical industries benefit from more diagnoses while nobody acts as a brake asking whether 10% can be right.
- For an indistractable workplace he cites UK nurses whose "Drug round in progress — do not disturb" vests cut prescription mistakes by 88%, and three cultural traits: psychological safety (Amy Edmondson's work — being able to raise the distraction problem without fear), a forum to discuss it (Slack's internal complaint channel, where management replied with 👀/✅ emojis so staff felt heard), and management modelling it (Slack's "Work hard and go home" ethos — the car park empties at 6 p.m. and using Slack at night or weekends is reprimanded).
- His changed-mind answer: although secular and unable to accept the supernatural, he has come to respect what organised religion provides — community care, rituals marking the year, higher purpose and forced disconnection — and thinks sceptics give up a lot by dismissing it.

## Claims to be careful with

- Eyal's ADHD positions are contested. The 10% US vs 1% Europe comparison reflects diagnostic practice and awareness differences as well as possible over-diagnosis, and mainstream psychiatry does not accept that ADHD medication is generally a dangerous first resort; stimulants are considered first-line, evidence-based treatment by major guidelines. His "skills before pills" stance is his opinion, not consensus.
- The claim that the chemical imbalance theory is simply "scientifically false" oversimplifies an active scientific debate; likewise there is no consensus that ADHD is typically something people should expect to be "undiagnosed" from.
- The 90/10 internal/external trigger split and the 88% nurse error-reduction figure are numbers he cites from studies in his book; they are quoted conversationally here without the underlying study details.
- His frameworks (traction/distraction, timeboxing, the four steps) are productivity advice from a behavioural-design author who sells books and consulting on exactly these topics — useful heuristics, not clinical guidance.

## Summary

Behavioural designer Nir Eyal argues that distraction is rarely caused by technology: 90% of it comes from internal triggers — discomfort we escape through phones, food, email or busywork — so the fix starts with noticing and reframing those feelings, then timeboxing your calendar around your values (self, relationships, then work), cutting external triggers and using pacts. He applies the same scepticism to ADHD, contending that a 10% US childhood diagnosis rate, a discredited chemical-imbalance rationale and pill-first treatment are failing people who should be offered skills — and an "undiagnosis plan" — before medication.
