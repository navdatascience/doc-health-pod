## Important tidbits
- Badri says he only became aware of the importance of mental health later in his career; his analogy: going to the gym doesn't mean you have a health problem, and likewise you shouldn't wait for a "mental injury" before taking care of your mind — you train batting in practice sessions, so the mind needs training too.
- He says he has no special fitness routine, but if he doesn't exercise he gets stressed; even now, working indoors on shoots for two days stresses him, and he feels sunlight has to fall on him. He still plays golf tournaments and says, "If I don't compete, I feel dead."
- On handling pressure: Badri says he actually got out in easy matches but thrived in tough situations — he attributes this to a very strict upbringing, with his father putting him into cricket coaching at age 8 and running extra practice after regular sessions, including facing balls thrown randomly from all directions (he once took a ball on the nose and bled heavily).
- Hydration in his early playing days was basic: at Pachaiyappa's College matches, players drank water from a drum with a chained tumbler; later it became glucose water (Glucon-D), and when budgets were low, fresh lime water with salt.
- He makes the point that glucose was often given only after a player got cramps, when it should have been drunk before the cramps set in.
- He recounts a heat-exhaustion episode from a hot match full of ones and twos: his body overheated and he started shivering; Faf du Plessis told coach Stephen Fleming he felt like he was going to die, Badri's shots kept crawling along the ground, and he lay down for about half an hour before he could get up.
- Because umpires and the fielding side restrict drinks breaks, players sneak sips of water while changing gloves — and Badri says Dhoni himself does this, taking a quick sip while returning his helmet, to stay hydrated.
- Post-match recovery in his era meant ice baths — tubs filled with water and large ice blocks — which he describes as "like a punishment," plus moving the limbs in the swimming pool to relax; he was relieved after retirement that he no longer had to do ice baths.
- On Dhoni's discipline, Badri describes a school-like daily process: Dhoni eats by 9 am, has lunch by 12 pm, and plans exactly how many stretches, how much swimming, how many balls to hit and how many catches to take each day.
- On Dhoni's calmness: in tight matches while Badri was sweating badly, Dhoni would sit calmly asking for tea; Badri says Dhoni got angry with him only once — after a bad shot against Anil Kumble in an RCB match.
- Badri says Dhoni did not practice wicketkeeping much, yet he never saw him miss a single stumping.
- On sudden money: Badri describes salaries jumping from ₹10,000 to ₹10 crore almost overnight; after signing a huge IPL contract he bought a sports car and crashed it because he didn't know how to handle it.
- On career insecurity, he notes a player can wake up dropped from the team or with the IPL contract gone, and "we know only cricket" — normal people don't understand what players go through, which adds to the mental strain.
- The personal cost of the dream: it took him 28 years to fulfil his childhood dream of playing for the country, and the constant travel meant his young daughter initially wouldn't come to him and would cry when he came home.

## Claims to be careful with
- This is a light, anecdote-driven conversation rather than a medical episode; the health points (mental fitness, hydration, heat exhaustion, recovery) are Badri's personal experiences and views, not clinical guidance.
- The view that glucose should be consumed before cramps occur is his experiential take from playing days, stated without supporting evidence in the episode.

## Summary
Former CSK and India cricketer Badri joins Dr Pal for a cricket-and-life conversation built around why Dhoni is "unstoppable": his fixed daily routine, calm under pressure and freakish wicketkeeping. Along the way Badri opens up about the mental-health side of professional cricket, the crude hydration practices of his early days (glucose water, lime water with salt, sneaking sips during glove changes), a frightening heat-exhaustion episode, ice-bath recovery, and the whiplash of going from ₹10,000 to ₹10 crore contracts.
