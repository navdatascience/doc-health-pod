# Is Going Vegan Actually Healthy or Harmful?
- **Source:** Dr Pal Podcast Playlist
- **Guest:** Arvind, vegan and animal-rights activist (with Dr Pal Manickam, host)
- **YouTube:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vn3zrVFzoxU

## Important tidbits
- Arvind and Dr Pal draw a sharp distinction: "vegan" is an ethical position (no meat, dairy, eggs, honey, leather, wool or silk) and says nothing about health — a vegan can live on fries, chips and beer — while "whole food plant-based" (whole grains, legumes, nuts, seeds, fruit, vegetables) is the pattern done for health. Dr Pal says he recommends a whole food plant-based diet to most of his patients.
- On gut diversity, Dr Pal cites the Hadza tribe of Africa: about 2,500 gut bacterial species and ~100 trillion gut bacteria, versus only about 1,500 species in a Westernized adult — species are being lost each generation through low fibre and environmental factors, lost species cannot be replaced, but remaining bacteria are malleable and can be cultivated with variety and a "rainbow" fibre-rich diet.
- Protein target discussed is 0.8 g per kg body weight (e.g. 48 g for a 60 kg person). Arvind's main source is soya chunks: 100 g dry gives 50–55 g protein (he eats ~100 g/day), plus ~18–19 g fibre; tofu and tempeh (~18 g protein per 100 g, fermented so bacteria consume antinutrients like phytates, lectins and trypsin inhibitors) are other options.
- Seitan (vital wheat gluten) has ~75 g protein per 100 g but a poor amino-acid profile, so Arvind combines it with soy/legumes for a complete protein; soy itself is a complete protein. On PDCAAS scores he cites soy isolate as nearly as high as whey and pea protein isolate at ~0.94; whey is still "king" for bioavailability, per Arvind.
- Vitamin B12 is the acknowledged gap in a vegan diet: it exists in tempeh/kombucha but not in absorbable amounts, so it must be supplemented — Arvind suggests ~1,500 micrograms twice a week, and Dr Pal notes one B12 injection can last 2–3 months. Low B12 causes neuropathic pain/numbness and macrocytic anaemia; Dr Pal says the first things he asks such patients are "are you a vegan?" and "are you an alcoholic?", because alcohol also depletes B12 and folic acid.
- Dr Pal says he is against most supplements (probiotics, turmeric/curcumin) and that whole-food protein is better for the gut than whey powder — but B12 supplementation is a medical treatment, has no real risk, and people should not fear it; whole-food omnivorous eating has at most a slight gut-health edge, with probably no difference in overall health outcomes.
- On the soy–thyroid myth, Dr Pal says soy isoflavones can interfere with thyroxine absorption only at concentrations far above what Indian-grown soy reaches; his advice for a 48 g protein target was ~30 g from soy and the rest from other sources rather than all-soy, and he says soy is not negatively associated with breast cancer.
- Fibre is nearly automatic on a plant-based diet: Arvind reports hitting ~40 g/day without trying (soy chunks, whole wheat, chana), while Dr Pal — eating 80% plant-based — gets only 25–30 g/day. Dr Pal says bowel frequency from once in 2 days to 3 times a day can all be normal; what matters is Bristol stool type 3–4 without pain or bloating, in which case he advises against wasting money on stool testing.
- On red meat, Dr Pal says multiple studies associate higher red-meat intake with more colon polyps and colorectal cancer (screening colonoscopy from age 50 in the US), and that fibre intake attenuates the risk by an unknown amount; his own approach is to avoid red meat as much as possible.
- On gluten, Dr Pal lists three categories: true coeliac disease (autoimmune intestinal damage, more prevalent in Punjab than South India) — the only group that must fully avoid gluten; IBS patients, who may need a 4–6 week elimination diet while gut health is fixed; and the "fad" group, for whom gluten-free eating gives no health benefit. Seitan is fine for everyone else.
- Practical habits from Arvind: batch-cook a big raw salad (beetroot, carrot, red cabbage, cucumber, tomato, lettuce) that keeps ~5 days in the fridge; chickpeas with tahini, lemon and onion; swap ghee/butter for oil, paneer for tofu and curd for coconut milk — he estimates ~90% of everyday Indian food is already vegan-friendly once dairy is removed.
- Arvind says he is 6 months sober (recording date given as October 9, 2025) after heavy drinking, prompted by a Dr Pal video about a binge-drinking patient vomiting blood; Dr Pal says his research found even one drop of alcohol depletes a few percent of good gut bacteria, which he hypothesises Arvind's high fibre intake partly offset.
- Ethically, Arvind argues eating animals is not a purely "personal choice" because another being's body is used; he describes dairy as depending on forced impregnation of cows/buffaloes and separation of calves, notes cows only produce milk after giving birth, and urges meat-eaters to at least witness slaughterhouse/dairy processes before deciding.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Pal's statement that "even one drop of alcohol" depletes a few percent of good gut bacteria is presented as his research finding without a cited study in the episode.
- The Hadza figures (2,500 vs 1,500 gut species, species permanently lost across generations) are stated by Dr Pal without a named source in the episode.
- Arvind's graphic claims about standard dairy and slaughterhouse practices (forced impregnation, routine killing of day-old male calves) are his activist characterisation of industry practice, presented without independent verification in the episode.
- Arvind's B12 dose suggestion (~1,500 mcg twice weekly) and protein-source scores (PDCAAS ~0.94 for pea isolate) are his recommendations/figures, not prescriptions verified in the episode.
- The suggestion that a vegan eating whole foods can be "just as healthy, maybe even more" than an omnivore is the speakers' opinion; both agree it depends on eating whole foods, not vegan junk food.

## Summary
Dr Pal hosts vegan activist Arvind for a debate on whether veganism is healthy or harmful, covering both the medical angle — gut-microbiome diversity, hitting protein targets with soy/tempeh/seitan, mandatory B12 supplementation, fibre, red meat and colon cancer, and gluten — and Arvind's ethical case against meat, dairy and eggs. They converge on whole food plant-based eating as healthy for the gut, while agreeing that "vegan" by itself does not mean healthy and that change has to come from education rather than force.
