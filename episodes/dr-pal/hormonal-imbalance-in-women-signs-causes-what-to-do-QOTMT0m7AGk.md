## Important tidbits
- Dr Ragavan defines menopause as complete absence of periods for about a year. She says the average Indian age of onset is 46–50 years; menopause below 45 is early menopause and below 40 is premature menopause, both of which she says she is seeing more often.
- She calls stress "one understated determinant of premature menopause": constantly high cortisol disturbs hormonal homeostasis and, in her thinking, can contribute to ovarian depletion. In her health camps, urban-slum and rural cohorts had a far higher chance of early or premature menopause than the literature suggests.
- She distinguishes "eustress" (good stress that drives achievement) from "distress" (a reaction that causes inflammation emotionally, physically and hormonally), and says how a person perceives and responds to stress — shaped by childhood security or trauma — determines whether it harms them.
- Perimenopausal symptoms can start 3–5 years before periods stop. The first real sign she names is the hot flush: a sudden feeling of heat and sweating from vasodilation as hormone levels fluctuate, often felt behind the ears and over the whole body. Night sweats then disrupt sleep.
- Other perimenopausal signs she lists: sleep problems (trouble falling asleep, early awakening, inability to get back to sleep), low or anxious mood, and sexual dysfunction — loss of interest or vaginal dryness making intimacy painful — which she says can appear even before periods stop.
- She says sleep quality matters more than hours in bed: inadequate deep sleep or dream sleep leaves a woman groggy and unenergetic despite enough hours, and poor sleep plus stress act as counter-regulatory, cortisol-raising forces that block weight loss even when diet and exercise are unchanged.
- On obesity, she says it is generally related to delayed, not early, menopause: fat behaves like an endocrine organ producing estrogen excess, raising risk of breast stimulation/breast cancer and endometrial thickening with heavy periods. Women who do not reach menopause beyond age 55 generally have overweight or obesity as a factor, she says.
- On hormone replacement therapy (HRT), she says not all women need it, but women who need it should be given it and should not be denied it over fears about breast cancer, dementia or cardiovascular risk. She compares it to replacing thyroid hormone in thyroid deficiency.
- She lists strong contraindications to HRT as active endometrial cancer, active breast cancer, active bleeding, stroke or active heart attack (MI) — but says a family history alone is not a reason to deny it when quality of life is seriously compromised by vasomotor symptoms, which typically persist about 2 years while the body acclimatizes.
- On timing, she says HRT is a "must" for suitable women within 10 years of menopause or under 60 years of age, but she would not start it in a woman of 70 or someone 10–15 years past menopause, where risks outweigh benefits. She also says severe, prolonged vasomotor symptoms are associated in studies with higher future cardiovascular morbidity, making them a red flag.
- To separate PMS from true anxiety or depression, she uses a menstrual diary paired with a mood diary: mood changes (tearfulness, snapping, irritability, anger), cravings, bloating, leg cramps and breast tenderness starting about 10 days before the period fit premenstrual syndrome. She says short-course SSRI or SNRI medication given only for that 10-day window has helped many of her patients, but any concern about self-harm or harm to others escalates care to a psychiatrist.
- She says repeated visits for chronic pelvic pain, bladder symptoms or PMS-like complaints can hide sexual, domestic or cyber abuse and adverse childhood experiences, which clinicians should ask about directly. Bedwetting beyond about age 7, and certainly beyond 10–12, invariably has a significant psychological element to rule out, she says, alongside worm infestation, gut and neurological causes.
- Endocrine disruptors, she explains, are chemicals that mimic hormones and attach to hormone receptors without producing the correct effect — blocking the desired effect (e.g. irregular periods, vaginal dryness) while free natural estrogen acts elsewhere, predisposing to breast tenderness, fibroids and possibly breast cancer. Sources she names: cosmetics with phthalates, BPA and heavy metals; microplastics (highest load in bigger fish); processed dairy; plastic food storage; microwave cooking; non-stick Teflon cookware; and char-grilled animal protein.
- Her exposure-reduction advice: read cosmetic labels like food labels, store food in steel, glass or earthenware, avoid packaged, tinned and canned food, sauté or boil rather than char-grill, alkalinize water with a lime slice, and use fasting as a detox practice. She says she personally drinks at least 3 litres of water a day from a steel bottle with mint or tulsi leaves.
- On dairy, she distinguishes milk — which she calls inflammatory and a common cause of bloating in her practice, alongside gluten — from fermented curd, yogurt and buttermilk, which she counts as beneficial probiotic foods.
- Supplements she emphasizes for menopausal women: vitamin D (she says more than 80% of Indian/Asian women are deficient; ideal is half an hour of morning sun between 8 and 10 am) and calcium — 800–1,000 mg per day in menopause — from buttermilk, curd, cabbage, broccoli, sesame seeds, ragi and drumstick (moringa) leaves. She says musculoskeletal pain and weakness, not hot flushes, are the predominant menopausal symptoms in Indian women.
- She describes "emotional inflammation": constant anger (e.g. from news and politics) triggers inflammatory chemicals such as interleukin-6 and cytokines, keeping the body inflamed and, she says, worsening or predisposing to metabolic syndrome, hypertension, diabetes and even cancer — distinct from sadness, which resolves.
- For PCOS, Dr Pal describes his clinic's circadian eating window of 7 am to 7 pm: stuck to for about 6 weeks, patients feel emotionally better and more energetic before any weight change (typically none in the first 4 weeks). Dr Ragavan links night-shift IT work — long sitting hours, deranged eating, caffeine at abnormal times — to worsening PCOS.
- On caffeine, she advises women with urogynecological problems to limit it to two cups (150 ml cups, not mugs) a day and avoid caffeine after about 4–5 pm: it is a diuretic that stimulates the bladder, can contribute to stone formation, and disturbs sleep.
- Her top five foods for permenopausal symptoms: spinach and dates for iron; buttermilk and curd for calcium; guava (vitamin C aids iron absorption; fibre helps constipation); soaked almonds and walnuts for good fats and protein; and plain water, which she calls the most underrated "food" of all.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Ragavan's view that chronic stress and high cortisol cause premature menopause via ovarian depletion is presented as her clinical thinking from health-camp observations, not as an established causal finding.
- Her endocrine-disruptor claims — that microplastics are permanent in the body, that roadside paneer carries a microplastic load, and that disruptors predispose to breast cancer — are stated strongly and go beyond settled consensus.
- Claims that a lime slice "alkalinizes" water and detoxifies body fluid, and that fasting is a detox, reflect her lifestyle-medicine framing rather than established physiology.
- "Emotional inflammation" from anger (via IL-6/cytokines) predisposing to cancer is a hypothesis she describes as "now being studied," not a proven link.
- Her statement that HRT is a "must" for symptomatic women within 10 years of menopause is a strong clinical position in a debated area; individual risk assessment still applies.
- The figure that more than 80% of Indian/Asian women are vitamin D deficient and the 800–1,000 mg/day calcium target are her stated figures.
