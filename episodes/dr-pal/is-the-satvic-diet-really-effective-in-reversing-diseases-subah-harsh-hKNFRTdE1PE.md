## Important tidbits
- This is part two of the Satvic Movement conversation; on coffee, Subah and Harsh say they avoid it and suggest going step by step rather than quitting everything at once — if you sweeten coffee, jaggery is closer to nature than white sugar, and a small amount of white sugar is a personal, conscious compromise rather than something to be judged for ("there's nothing wrong or bad" about the person).
- Their dessert approach is to remake favorites the satvic way: rabri, halwa, cakes and even cheesecakes with jaggery or dates instead of white sugar and heavy dairy; fruit itself is "the healthiest dessert".
- Their oil spectrum mirrors their food lists: the purest fats are whole fats — nuts, nut butters, nut milks (almond, coconut), seeds, seed pastes and grated coconut; next are cold-pressed oils (kachi ghani), extracted without chemicals or high heat in small mills; the category they call an "absolute danger" is ultra-refined oil.
- Satvic cooking is not meant to be slow or impractical — preparing a wholesome meal takes about the same or less time than a normal meal once your hand is set; they quote Robin Sharma: "any change is hard at first, messy in the middle and gorgeous in the end".
- Eating out and travel: plan ahead and find places offering clean food; they note South Indian food (idli, sambar) is relatively cleaner, and you can simply ask for less oil — demand for clean food is what will make restaurants supply it.
- On quantity, Dr Pal presses that calories in vs calories out still matters and mentions roughly 500 ml of oil per month per person as a working guideline; Subah and Harsh agree not all calories are the same (nuts vs pizza) while accepting amount matters.
- Their deeper framework: food choices stick when tied to a life purpose. They define purpose as the overlap of (1) your innate strengths — talents you didn't have to work for — and (2) using them to serve others, fellow animals and nature; a pregnant woman checking every decision against her baby's safety is their example of how a strong goal makes temptations easy to refuse.
- The fitness goal should be bigger than a number: rather than fixating on weight loss or fat loss, judge how you feel — energy levels and a sense of lightness are what make you stay with the change.
- Their morning routine uses the "3M" framework — Movement, Mastery (learning/reading to nourish the intellect) and Meditation (nourishing the spiritual body, which could be mantra chanting, scripture study, kirtan or helping people) — equivalently, start the day with three wins: a physical win, a mental win and a spiritual win; they compare the morning routine to an archer pulling the bow back so the day (the arrow) flies far.
- Habit advice: start with just 5–10 minutes of each practice; first establish the habit daily, and perfection follows automatically from repeated consistency — aiming for an hour from day one usually fails.
- Personal practices mentioned: Harsh reads scriptures (Bhagavad Gita; Bible for Christians) in the morning, Subah plays harmonium and sings kirtans/bhajans, and Dr Pal says he will restructure his own routine (workout, joke-writing as mastery, heartfulness meditation) around the 3M framework.
- Their closing line, echoed by Dr Pal: "be patient so that you don't become a patient" — start somewhere with small changes and integrate what works for you rather than adopting everything at once.

## Claims to be careful with
- Despite the episode title's question about reversing diseases, this part-two conversation presents no clinical evidence of disease reversal; it is largely philosophy, food frameworks and lifestyle practice.
- Ultra-refined oils are described as an "absolute danger" based on how they are processed; this is advocacy framing, not trial evidence, and the whole-fats-over-oils hierarchy is the movement's own framework rather than medical consensus.
- Claims that a purpose-driven life makes dietary temptation easy to resist are motivational, not empirical.
- Dr Pal's ~500 ml oil per person per month figure is offered conversationally without a cited source.

## Summary
In part two, Subah and Harsh walk Dr Pal through the practical and philosophical side of satvic living: jaggery or dates instead of white sugar (without moral judgment), a fat hierarchy from whole nuts and seeds through cold-pressed oils to avoided ultra-refined oils, travel and restaurant strategies, and above all a purpose-driven framework — using your innate strengths to serve others — plus a 3M morning routine of Movement, Mastery and Meditation started in 5–10 minute doses, while the disease-reversal question in the title is addressed only through their general philosophy rather than evidence.
