## Important tidbits
- The #1 skincare mistake, per Dr Rajan, is relying only on applications and products instead of lifestyle. Her order of priority: sleep, diet, exercise, then skincare. Sleeping on time beats spending an extra hour on a night routine — the cortisol–melatonin reset and sleeping with the circadian rhythm matter more than any product.
- Strength training is a standard recommendation in every one of her consultations: insulin resistance is "our biggest enemy" — driving acne, hair loss and facial hair growth. She notes 30–40% of her patients now have a workout routine, versus under 10% earlier.
- Beauty has moved away from symmetry and the golden ratio (1:1.6) to the "hero feature" — the one or two features that make a face relatable. Good aesthetic work never alters the hero feature. Indian skin ages "hypertrophically" (the face gets fuller, typically from the late 20s), unlike Caucasian atrophic ageing, so jawline contouring is the most common request; she says Indian women look their best in their early 30s.
- Rinse-off vs leave-on rule: you can be relatively casual about rinse-off products (face wash, shampoo) because contact is brief, but leave-on products (sunscreen, makeup, moisturiser) are absorbed and cumulative — research those carefully.
- She advises avoiding parabens and phthalates completely in leave-on products: they are endocrine disruptors that can bind the same receptors as hormones and create unfavourable signals. She cites a study in which breast-tissue "negative signals" reduced after just one month of stopping products containing them, and says a cross-specialty endocrine-disruption consensus group chose parabens first and phthalates second as the ingredients with the most evidence.
- Ingredients she rates: hyaluronic acid for hydration (more useful in drier places like Bangalore or Kashmir than humid Chennai), salicylic acid as a rinse-off/peel, lactic acid, tranexamic acid for pigmentation and melasma (also used post-microneedling/laser), and antioxidants pycnogenol and astaxanthin, which survive oxidation better than vitamin C.
- Beware "percentage wars": more is not better. Vitamin C works best around 15–17% and can become pro-oxidant at higher percentages, so a 30% serum is not better than a 15% one; similarly, very high zinc percentages in sunscreen cause irritation and poor compliance.
- Hair colour is her biggest worry: chemicals such as paraphenylenediamine (in dyes, and similar sensitisers in perfumes, room sprays, car polishes and even beard-growth products) can trigger lichen planus pigmentosus, an immune reaction that traps pigment in the dermis, often on the sides of the face and neck. She saw 3–4 cases in her entire residency; now she sees 3–4 every consultation day. "Ammonia-free" does not remove this risk. Her rule: colour hair no more than four times a year, and limit straightening/smoothening treatments.
- Do a simple patch test before using a new product — apply it on the forearm or behind the ear and watch for 24 hours — but know it only catches itchy contact dermatitis, not slow cumulative pigmentation.
- "Non-comedogenic" simply means unlikely to clog pores or cause pimples. Coconut oil is comedogenic and can cause acne; she says pimples driven by overnight coconut-oil application can settle within 2–3 weeks of just stopping it.
- For Indian skin, sunscreen is mainly to prevent tanning, pigmentation and photoageing — not skin cancer, which is rare in India (the melanoma Indians get is usually on palms and soles, unrelated to sun). What to buy: a zinc-based, broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 40–50+, PA++++ (UVA drives pigmentation), a critical wavelength above 370 nm (380+ is good), and antioxidants such as pycnogenol, polypodium or astaxanthin that extend protection into visible light and infrared — visible light and even the infrared from cooking flames worsen melasma. SPF 80 is not more protective than SPF 30, just more durable; she applies sunscreen once, before leaving for work, and says an SPF 50 wears down to about SPF 30 after 8 hours. Physical covering beats body sunscreen for everyday commuting.
- Two dangerous social-media trends she calls out: putting sunscreen on the scalp (the Western advice is to wear a hat; sunscreen chemicals and nano-titanium have been picked up from hair follicles and can cause inflammation and hair loss) and "sunscreens cause cancer" reels that resurface every summer.
- Natural is not automatically safe: turmeric applied on skin is a photosensitiser (eat it instead — it is good for skin from inside), henna is also a photosensitiser, and lemon, baking soda, toothpaste and crushed garlic on pimples are plain irritants that burn. Aloe vera gel is roughly comparable to water as a moisturiser — mildly hydrating depending on formulation — and many aloe products add menthol, an irritant.
- Viral "instant acne cure" videos are gimmicks: washing off sebum and gunk creates the before/after illusion (a hydration meter reads ~300% higher right after washing). DIY packs of turmeric, lime and yogurt mostly work through the wash-off effect; skin limits absorption of large molecules — "if everything went in, we would become sponges in a swimming pool."
- Never use steroid creams for skin lightening: beyond 3–4 months they cause "topical steroid damaged face," a problem she calls uniquely Indian; tranexamic acid can reduce that redness within days to weeks.
- The 10-step Korean routine is, in her words, a waste of time: more ingredients mean more sensitisation (her group's published work found more allergen exposure in ingredient-heavy Korean sunscreens), and sheet masks are ecologically wasteful occlusion — apply the product from a bottle and use a reusable occlusive layer instead. "Glass skin" on screen is mostly high-gloss moisturiser, a moisturising base and filters.
- A simple routine for a 9-to-6 office worker: moisturiser at night, sunscreen in the morning, and a cleanser two to three times a day (morning, on returning home — pollution-related pigmentation is a growing problem — and at bedtime). For moisturisers, avoid parabens/phthalates, choose water-based light formulas if acne-prone; she likes MSM (methylsulfonylmethane), hyaluronic acid and peptides. Prefer collagen from food — bone broth simmered 8–12 hours — with supplements (she uses collagen plus pycnogenol/astaxanthin) as a fallback.
- Keep the bedroom at 25–28°C rather than 18–19°C: very cold air-conditioning dries skin and lips (she links Chennai's recent dry-lip problem to heavy AC use), and Chennai's humidity itself helps the skin barrier repair. Her own routine is minimal — cleanser, sunscreen once a day, tinted sunscreen over her melasma patches, an exfoliator two or three times a week, and a retinol product at night.

## Claims to be careful with
- The endocrine-disruptor case against parabens and phthalates (hormone-receptor binding, breast-tissue signals improving after one month off) is presented as settled; the consensus guidelines she describes are still a work in progress and the evidence base is evolving.
- The Sister Study finding she cites — higher breast-cancer risk in women who used hair-straightening/treatment products more than four times — is an observational association in a specific cohort, not proof of causation; evidence linking hair dyes to bladder/renal cancers is limited and mixed.
- Her position that an SPF 50 sunscreen stays effective for ~8 hours and that once-a-day application is enough goes against standard dermatology guidance to reapply every 2–3 hours outdoors; she bases it on immersion tests and UV-camera studies.
- The claim that her clinic found the sunscreen filter octinoxate above threshold levels in blood after face-only application comes from a single in-house study.
- Benefits of oral collagen supplements and of pycnogenol/astaxanthin for skin are described confidently; the clinical evidence is still emerging.
- Statements such as vitamin C becoming pro-oxidant above ~15–17% are formulation-dependent generalisations.

## Summary
Celebrity dermatologist Dr Renita Rajan argues that the biggest skincare mistake is product-first thinking: sleep, diet and exercise — especially strength training against insulin resistance — come before anything in a bottle. She then gives a practical buyer's guide (zinc sunscreen with SPF 40–50+, PA++++ and visible-light protection; avoid parabens and phthalates in leave-on products; ignore percentage wars), warns that hair dyes and other everyday chemicals are driving an epidemic of stubborn pigmentation, and debunks viral trends from scalp sunscreen and 10-step Korean routines to turmeric packs and steroid lightening creams.
