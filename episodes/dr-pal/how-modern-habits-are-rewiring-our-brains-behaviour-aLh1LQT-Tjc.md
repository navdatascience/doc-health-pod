## Important tidbits
- Dr Jayakumar is a psychologist, not a psychiatrist; she spends the first 5–10 minutes of sessions explaining the difference and her role, and stresses confidentiality — she is not a channel who reports the child's disclosures back to the mother.
- Small, everyday stresses add up to childhood trauma — including simply not being appreciated or shown physical affection. Parents who were never allowed to express themselves as children tend to become excessively strict, passing the unhealed trauma unconsciously to the next generation.
- Warning signs parents must not ignore: sudden loss of interest in things the child liked, academic decline, disturbed sleep, losing all friends within a month, isolation and room-withdrawal, school refusal, and not eating properly (bullying can lead to eating disorders). She sees parents who ignored such signs for five years; early-stage mental-health problems are far easier to treat, while entrenched problems like teenage borderline personality disorder can need 45–50 DBT sessions.
- Sadness is not depression: casual labelling of everyday behaviour as depression, anxiety or OCD — often self-diagnosed via ChatGPT or Google — is a major challenge in her practice. She urges people not to use the internet for mental-health diagnosis.
- On Roblox: the game is not fully predefined — adults can enter, create sexually inappropriate games and communicate with small children inside it. She recounts an in-game companion repeatedly prompting a boy to bring his sister to play, then telling him "she doesn't deserve to be your sister" — distorting the child's family cognitions.
- Violent multiplayer games (Free Fire, PUBG) are linked in her casework to real violence: she recounts a Kerala boy who stabbed his mother when his phone's charge ran out, and an 11-year-old girl who broke her mother's head for being stopped from playing; children blur the line between the game and real life.
- Screen addiction diverts children's dopamine-seeking into scrolling; some children she describes go 3–4 days without bathing while watching reels. Her treatment approach: a consistent routine for 66 continuous days, replacing screen dopamine with physical, exciting activities (dance, football, pillow fights) — not with yoga or maths homework.
- Psychology counts 12 types of play, of which electronic play is only one — yet she says today's children spend about 80% of their play time on it. Heavy daily screen use has damaged attention and memory so last-minute exam study no longer works; children need daily study time precisely because screen time is daily.
- Ages 7–14 are the critical window for parental consistency: set ground rules, learn to say no, but explain why (this generation needs the explanation previous generations never got). "Children have friends — they need parents," and grandparents should not override parents, which confuses the child's obedience hierarchy.
- Her own household rules: she raised her daughters with no devices until 14 (only a Sunday movie/series of about 2 hours), no TV while eating and no TV in the dining area; at 14 she allows board-game apps like Ludo but no violent games.
- Phone readiness depends on the child (around 14–15 for some; some 17-year-olds still not ready) and monitoring must start on day one — e.g. if weekly-checked screen time exceeds 45 minutes to 1 hour, the phone is taken away. Children who start sleeping alone (from ~11) should never sleep with a device, at any age; parents should choose their battles so a "no" retains value.
- Online predators' strongest weapon is blackmail with morphed photos, and boys are targeted too: she recounts boys taking ₹4,000–5,000 app loans against an Aadhaar upload to fund game purchases, after which the app sent morphed photos to all their contacts, driving one boy toward self-harm. Children must be able to approach a trusted adult; parents should understand their child's online world without overreacting or spying.
- If children are allowed on social media, disable comments and like counts: negativity and sexualised content earn the engagement, teaching children to expose more. She also warns adults spending 6–8 hours a day on reels/vlogs end up feeling their own lives are insufficient.
- On bullying: never normalise it or tell the child to hit back. Teach the child to speak up firmly — "I like you, you're my friend, but this is paining me; don't do it" — because tears fuel the bully. Schools, where the bullying happens, bear primary responsibility; every school needs a good counsellor, bystander children are also affected, and making the bully help the victim (the Ben Franklin effect) can end it. She distinguishes today's school refusal (won't leave home) from the previous generation's truancy.

## Claims to be careful with
- Her statement that brain-scan images of a child who has played video games continuously for 8–9 hours are "the same" as those of an adult who has used cocaine is a strong equivalence presented without a cited study.
- Screen-addiction treatment being "actually very easy" with a 66-day routine is her clinical framing; addiction severity varies.
- Developmental timelines she states — frontal-cortex decision-making maturing by ~21 years in boys and ~18 in girls — are simplified as presented.
- The crime and exploitation anecdotes (the Kerala stabbing, Dubai blackmail cases, deaths by suicide at ages 7–9 attributed to bullying) are recounted from her casework and commission experience, not independently verified in the episode.

## Summary
Child and educational psychologist Dr Saranya Jayakumar explains how everyday habits — unprocessed parental trauma, casual psychiatric labelling, and above all screens — are rewiring children's brains and behaviour: the warning signs parents ignore for years, the dangers inside games like Roblox and Free Fire, predator blackmail, cyberbullying, and her practical rules for families (critical 7–14 window, monitored and time-limited devices, no devices in bed, dopamine replacement through physical play, and firm, explained boundaries). She closes on bullying: teach children to speak up, and hold schools responsible for counsellors and intervention.
