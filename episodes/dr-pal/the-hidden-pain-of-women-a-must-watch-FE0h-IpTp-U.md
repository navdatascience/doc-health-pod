## Important tidbits
- Dr Shalini calls parenting her actual first profession: "Anybody else can replace me in my job, but nobody else can replace me in my role as a parent." She removed the "patients come first" plaque from her room after having children and now keeps firm boundaries on her availability.
- What children actually want is not gifts or overflowing love but time, attention, presence, eye contact and attunement. The standard she sets is "good enough" parenting — good enough attention, care, words, correction and repair — not being the best possible parent.
- Children carry their parents' voices inside them as their own internal conversation for life. She says most therapy clients are, in effect, searching for the "missing link" of inadequate parenting — she sees clients aged 35 to 60 whose core issues trace back to childhood and adolescence.
- In earlier generations, an absent father was compensated by an elder brother, uncle or grandfather stepping into the role. In today's nuclear families, if the dad is not there, the role is simply missing — and children miss learning authority, tone and body language from him.
- She pushes back on the idea that Western parenting is worse: Western parents are often more involved and intentional precisely because they have no help. In India, she says, adults are quick to point out a child's defects (her example: the comments a child gets on first wearing glasses) — "Culture is lifting people up; here we shame others and feel very good about ourselves."
- The Indian woman is expected to be the best cook, homemaker, decorator and everything else. Her prescription is simplification: the simplest, leanest meals and routines, cutting time-consuming ritual burdens, because "me time" and recharging are essential — "care work is also work," and it is invisible and taken for granted until the woman stops doing it.
- Unaddressed distress in women surfaces in the body: Dr Pal notes homemaker patients with GI complaints who rate their stress 10 out of 10. Dr Shalini adds that stigma, the cost of psychiatrists and poorly equipped government hospitals push women to present psychological problems to other doctors instead — "the body keeps the score" of harassment and being put down, including by in-laws.
- Men, she says, are not trained for relationships and particularly lack repair skills: after saying something in anger they excuse it as "that's how I am" — medicalising a moral error (she references the Kamal Haasan film *Thug Life*, where an affair is excused as "my disease, like diabetes and hypertension"). Men assume earning money fulfils the father's role, but children remember the time, conversations and what their dad taught them — not the money spent.
- Her model for husbands is the bird pair: male birds brood, share the eggs and split the work 50/50. The man's job is to be present, protect, provide, share, and make his wife feel safe, listened to, understood and loved — because the woman is the binding force of the "nest," and a happy wife binds the family together. Simply ignoring a wife — not looking at her, not asking how she feels — "amounts to cruelty," she says.
- Dr Pal confesses his own version of this: after social-media fame he came home at 7:30–8 pm assuming his wife would absorb all the childcare, until the school called him about a pickup. The turning point in his marriage, he says, was learning to have slow, negotiating conversations with his wife.
- Parenting rewards are delayed by decades: no child will say "you're the best dad on earth" — the appreciation typically arrives when the child is 30–35. Because the work is boring and unthanked, adults must support and cheer other adults and parents.
- Her closing advice to housewives: "It's okay to be imperfect. If the house is very well-kept and neat, it means a woman has lost her entire life to it — a well-kept house is a sign of slavery." Be good enough, accept disapproval, and take "one dose of queendom" — because a victim mother raises children who accept being slaves.
- Her advice to couples: parents are the child's prefrontal cortex and template, so be intentional. Her family practises scheduled "feedback time" (she reviews the week, asks what is happening and how she can help) and a "Mom and Me day" — one-on-one time where the child chooses the activity. She recommends family meetings, feedback time and hug time as household structures.
- On adolescents questioning their identity or sexuality: adolescence is an identity-versus-confusion stage, teens test different moulds, and parents should not panic. She says the male prefrontal cortex takes about 27 years to complete its growth, parents are temporary guides rather than owners of their children, and a child who says "maybe I'm gay" should be met with calm — "anyhow you have to study, anyhow you have to take care of yourself."
- She explains her clinical view that the brain starts in a female form and is masculinised by testosterone from the testes; where masculinisation is incomplete, that is simply how that child's brain is — "nobody can change it" — and families need space and acceptance. Parents of LGBTQ children typically move through denial, anger and bargaining, depression, and finally acceptance, and therapy helps them through it.
- She reframes karma psychologically: not a divine ledger but human memory and collective debt — like the *moy* (gift money) remembered and repaid at weddings. Similarly, she says, our image of God tends to reflect our inner self, and religious belief can support mental health by giving suffering order and purpose: the brain fills missing gaps, and "the suffering has meaning, so let me endure it."

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Shalini draws a sharp distinction between people who are biologically gay and boys who were sexually abused or "groomed" by men and imprinted with a preference, saying therapy has helped some men realise they were "not gay but groomed" and "go back to their original versions." This framing is contested and should not be read as settled clinical consensus.
- The statement that the male prefrontal cortex completes growth at 27 years (and related claims about testosterone "vascularising"/masculinising the brain) is her simplified account of a gradual, debated process.
- Her remarks on the Y chromosome "getting deteriorated" and globally falling testosterone and sperm levels are presented as established fact; the scale and implications are debated.
- The historical narrative — ancient India having women rulers, and Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism successively reshaping family life from the sixth century — is her interpretive framing, offered as cultural commentary rather than sourced history.
- The "bionic male" replacing human men is offered half-jokingly as speculation about AI, not a clinical claim.

## Summary
Psychiatrist Dr Shalini delivers a wide-ranging conversation on why so many women hurt quietly inside families: invisible care work, husbands who equate money with fatherhood and never learned relationship repair, and parenting that is either absent or perfectionist. Her remedies are "good enough" parenting built on presence and attunement, men learning to build the "nest" alongside their wives, structured family practices like feedback time and one-on-one days, and calm, non-panicked guidance for adolescents figuring out who they are.
