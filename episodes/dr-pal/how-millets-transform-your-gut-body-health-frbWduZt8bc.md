## Important tidbits
- Dr Vali's core protocol is "fix the gut first": before eating millet food, drink fermented millet porridge (ambali/ambuli) for about 3 weeks — foxtail millet ambali for 2 days, then kodo millet for 2 days, rotating millets — so gut microbes are ready for the high fibre; people who jump straight to millet food complain of bloating because the fibre is not hydrated and their gut is unprepared.
- Ambali recipe as stated: 5 g of a single millet (never mix millets, or lentils, with each other — he says the microbial populations "fight") soaked in 500 ml water in a mud pot, cooked 10 minutes, covered with muslin cloth (not a closed lid, so oxygen enters), fermented 8 hours (10 hours maximum), then drink 100–200 ml per person; 5 g serves 2–3 people.
- Soak millets (and lentils) 6–8 hours before cooking: soaking removes anti-nutrients such as phytic acid and makes the grain lighter on the gut. He recommends cooking in a mud pot for ~10 minutes because its micro-pores release pressure, unlike a steel/pressure vessel where the bottom layers are pressurised.
- He ranks millets by fibre: the five minor millets (kodo, foxtail, barnyard, browntop, little) are highest — grains with 8%+ fibre are "very good," little millet has 9.8 g fibre per 100 g, and a minimum of 3 g fibre per 100 g of grain is his floor. Ragi (finger millet) is lower in fibre; people who are already sick should use only minor millets, healthy people can use all 10 including major millets (kambu/pearl millet, jowar/sorghum), and growing children need major millets for strength.
- Satiety claim: four browntop millet idlis, or a ragi/foxtail meal, can keep a person from feeling hungry until the next day, unlike rice idlis after which hunger returns in ~2 hours — he attributes this to fibre regulating appetite, and says some people complain millet food is "not digesting" simply because they don't feel hungry.
- Fermentation distinction: idli/dosa batter is fermented *then* cooked (microbes die in steaming, leaving beneficial metabolites and short-chain fatty acids), whereas ambali is cooked *then* fermented, so you consume the live microbes themselves — he rates the latter higher. Fermented millet batter keeps only ~10 hours refrigerated before turning too sour.
- Recipes given: millet dosa = 90 g millet + 10 g urad dal, soaked and ground, batter fermented overnight (about 5 hours suffices in warm Chennai; grind late at night in cooler places like Mysuru); millet vada = 80 g browntop millet + 20% chana (Bengal gram) dal, soaked overnight, ground with onion, green chilli and jeera, fried in cold-pressed (bull-driven ghani) coconut oil — but he warns people with gut problems to fix their gut before eating chilli.
- Millet milk: soak ragi overnight, grind in the morning and squeeze through muslin cloth. He says his family feeds babies ragi milk after breast milk, and that pearl-millet (kambu) curd rice was the traditional form because early cows gave only ~0.5–1 litre of milk.
- His own plate rule for every meal: greens/vegetables, a tuber (elephant yam or sweet potato), and millets (as millet rice, idli or roti), plus ambali once a day; he personally eats one meal a day and says greens + tuber + millets make a complete food that makes frequent eating unnecessary, unlike rice/wheat which he says drive eating every 3 hours.
- Water argument: he calculates kodo millet needs about 200 litres of water per kilo from sowing to harvest versus 8,000–10,000 litres for rice (~40x), and claims Punjab's groundwater will last only ~12 more years at current use; he also cites 28,000 litres of water per kilo of sugar from sugarcane.
- Sweets and bread: millet sweets (e.g. millet badusha) are made with palm jaggery (toddy/silver-date/khajoor palm) rather than refined sugar or cane jaggery, which he says spikes glucose more; sourdough is "100%" better than regular bread, but he says nothing comes close to ambali.
- He rejects 30-day diet plans as a "corporate" idea: change the kitchen slowly and steadily — drink ambali for 5–6 weeks before changing the rest of your food — and says millet biryani/pulao (e.g. barnyard millet pulao, kodo millet vegetable biryani) replaces basmati versions that spike glucose and harm gut microbes.
- On the popular claim that millets cause goitre/thyroid problems, he says it is "made up": goitre and thyroid disease are different things, the African women in the old report were eating bread, meat and cassava rather than millets, and he claims millets have actually healed thyroid issues via the gut–hormone axis.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Vali claims grinding in a mixer-grinder ("mixie") makes food "up to 30 to 40%" nutritionally inferior because of heat and pressure — stated without cited evidence.
- He claims ragi milk has "five times more calcium than any milk on this planet" and that babies are fed only ragi milk after breast milk in his family — a strong nutritional claim listeners should verify before applying to infants.
- He claims millets have "healed" thyroid issues and that specific millet fibres act directly on the endocrine system, and that ambali can "wipe out many of the diseases"; these are his assertions, not established clinical findings.
- His cost claim — that ₹100 of millet can "correct 400 guts" and that people on the protocol will "forget to go to doctor" — is advocacy rhetoric, as is his statement that he has not seen a doctor in 45 years.
- His water figures (200 L/kg for kodo millet; 28,000 L per kg of sugar) and the "12 years of Punjab groundwater" figure are his own calculations/estimates as presented in the episode.

## Summary
Padma Shri scientist Dr Khadar Vali, the "Millet Man of India," argues to Dr Pal that millets — especially the five high-fibre minor millets — can rebuild gut health and conserve water, but only if the gut is prepared first: his method is 3 weeks of fermented ambali (5 g millet in 500 ml water, cooked, then fermented 8–10 hours), soaked grains cooked in mud pots, and meals built on greens, tubers and millets, while rejecting polished rice, refined sugar, mixer-grinding and millet-branded junk food.
