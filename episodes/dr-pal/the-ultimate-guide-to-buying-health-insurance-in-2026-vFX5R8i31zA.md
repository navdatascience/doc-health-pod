## Important tidbits
- Medical inflation in India is stated at 14% per year, so treatment costs roughly double in 4–5 years: a knee replacement at ₹3 lakh today becomes ~₹6 lakh, and a bypass that cost ₹4–5 lakh a few years ago now costs ₹7–9 lakh at a mid-size hospital. Banu cites a study that almost 70% of Indian families are one hospitalization away from bankruptcy.
- Buy early: you can insure a baby from 91 days old, premiums are lowest and underwriting easiest while healthy (a 25-year-old can get ₹10 lakh cover for roughly ₹10,000/year), and waiting periods get completed sooner. Insurers charge extra for overweight applicants and, per Banu, will not offer a plan at BMI above ~37–40.
- Pre-existing disease (PED) waiting period is usually 3 years — and it also covers related ailments (e.g. diabetes/hypertension and related kidney problems). A PED-reduction rider can cut it to 1 year for some applicants, and special "ABCD" plans (Asthma, Blood pressure, Cholesterol, Diabetes) can start covering those conditions from day 30, at a higher premium; otherwise only accidents are covered in the first 30 days.
- Non-disclosure is, per Banu, the biggest cause of claim rejection in India: agents may tell buyers to tick "fully healthy," but hospital records (e.g. metformin use for 3 years, or a weight of 100 kg vs 75 kg declared) expose it, and the insurer can cancel the policy and keep the premiums. He says Ditto has handled 15,000–20,000 claims in 3–4 years and has never seen a claim denied when all conditions were properly disclosed.
- Watch for disease-wise sub-limits hidden in the 40–50 page policy document: e.g. a ₹5 lakh policy that pays only ₹1.5 lakh for knee replacement, forcing cheaper implants. Room-rent caps are similarly risky — with a ₹5,000/day room cap, staying in a ₹10,000/day room can get the whole claim proportionately halved — so Banu advises policies with no room-rent limit.
- How much cover: start with at least ₹10–15 lakh. A ₹1 crore family floater for a 40-year-old couple with two children costs about ₹40,000/year (~$400) and, per Banu, covers 95–99% of medical conditions in India. Reference premiums given: ~₹12,000/year for a single 30-year-old at ₹15 lakh cover, ~₹20,000/year as a family floater; for a 55-year-old parent, ~₹45,000/year at ₹15 lakh vs ~₹70,000 at ₹50 lakh, with ₹15 lakh the suggested minimum for parents.
- Look for a restoration benefit: if one family member uses ₹12 lakh of a ₹15 lakh floater, the insurer restores another ₹15 lakh for a different member — or the same person for a different problem — in the same year.
- Buying checklist from Banu: (1) claim settlement ratio of at least 95% (publicly available data); (2) a large network of hospitals where you actually take treatment, and always prefer cashless over reimbursement; (3) no room-rent limit; (4) restoration benefit; (5) no disease-wise sub-limits — buy a "full blanket cover"; (6) fewest regulator-published complaints per 10,000 claims; (7) premium should be the last deciding factor, and buy through an expert/agent who will support you at claim time.
- If a claim is wrongly rejected, you can escalate to the Insurance Ombudsman, where both sides present their case. For dengue-type disputes, Banu says a treating doctor certificate (TDC) explaining why home treatment was not possible (IV fluids, low platelets, comorbidities) is what gets hospitalization claims approved — Dr Pal recounts his own ~₹1.2 lakh dengue claim initially rejected as "outpatient treatment."
- Employer insurance is not enough — buy a separate personal policy (rapid-fire answers). NRIs/OCI holders with an Indian passport or OCI can buy Indian policies; buying after diagnosis to use for "medical tourism" does not work because non-disclosure gets the policy cancelled.
- Indian policies generally do not cover outpatient/preventive care or regular medication: GLP-1 drugs such as Mounjaro (~₹15,000–16,000/month) and routine diabetes medicines are not covered (no hospitalization required), though doctor-mandated bariatric surgery for severe obesity can be. Government schemes (e.g. the CM's ₹25 lakh scheme discussed, and the Andhra Pradesh scheme from ~2004–05 for below-poverty-line families) pay capped rates — e.g. ₹15,000–20,000 for cataract vs ₹70,000–80,000 privately — so Banu says they give bare-minimum treatment and earners should rely on their own insurance.
