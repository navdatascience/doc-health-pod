## Important tidbits
- Dr Kamaraj names fear of judgment in sex — from partner, friends and family — as the most common silent struggle Indian women face, ahead of any single sexual dysfunction; women often cannot voice what they want for fear the partner will question how they know it.
- Her advice: keep sexual problems between the couple or take them to an expert; involving family typically triggers blame and embarrassment (especially in arranged marriages) and complicates the problem.
- Vaginismus — involuntary tightening of the vaginal muscles from fear of penetration — is common and not under the woman's control, she says; delaying help turns a fear into a phobia, while treatment is simple and easier earlier.
- Intimacy fades, she says, because early curiosity is replaced by routine and habit; the fix is deliberate effort — intentionally scheduled quality time together (e.g. every alternate day: relaxed lighting, music, eating or watching something together), whether or not it leads to sex, plus exploring something new about once a month.
- She explains the Basson circular model (2000): for women, sexual contact typically starts from emotional intimacy, trust and safety, then sexual neutrality, arousal and desire in either order, and satisfaction that is about bonding and pleasure, not orgasm alone — unlike the older linear Masters & Johnson and Kaplan models.
- Low desire in women is usually a vicious cycle of unsatisfying experiences, not hormones, she says: satisfying responses restore desire without medical or hormonal help in most cases; rare medical contributors include thyroid problems and very low haemoglobin.
- She says studies show women's libido peaks in the early-to-late 30s, not the 20s, as the HPO (hypothalamo-pituitary-ovarian) axis matures — men who bring in a 32–33-year-old wife assuming a hormonal defect are usually mistaken.
- On frequency, she recommends regularity of at least two to three times a week (roughly once every two days), contrasting it with couples who focus only on 4 ovulation days a month before pregnancy and about once a month after delivery.
- "Sex should never be painful" is her key message: pain commonly comes from vaginismus, inadequate arousal/lubrication and friction, oil-based products that alter vaginal pH and invite fungal infection, or deep endometriosis; if lubricant is used, choose unscented, water-based products. Routine lubricant use is not needed before menopause if arousal/foreplay is adequate, she says.
- Chronic stress suppresses desire, she says: high cortisol and adrenaline lower estrogen and dopamine, breaking the pleasure–reward cycle that motivates further intimacy.
- Male sexual dysfunction is prevalent — she estimates 30–40% of men (erectile dysfunction, premature ejaculation) — but men present late because sexuality is tied to masculinity; early erectile dysfunction is often performance anxiety.
- Postpartum, she says partners must be involved from pregnancy visits through night feeds (breastfeeding roughly every 3 hours); an absent partner is a fast route to postpartum blues and depression, which women often do not voice.
- She recommends pre-marital counselling for both partners (body parts, pleasure, supplements such as folic acid and vitamin D3) and says age is the commonest reason for IVF: ovarian reserve declines from about age 33, so planning pregnancy earlier avoids delay and stress.
- Vasectomy, she says, is far less invasive than tubal ligation and a reasonable alternative to long-term birth control pills, but uptake in India is low because of myths that it lowers libido or performance.
- Sex education should start at home from about age 3 by naming body parts correctly and teaching boundaries and how to say no, she says; for teenagers, keep devices in common areas and teach the difference between use, misuse and abuse.
- After menopause (around 50+), falling estrogen causes vaginal dryness, burning and pain and lowers drive; she says local hormonal/estrogen creams and other treatments can restore comfort, and improving sex life remains worthwhile at that age.

## Claims to be careful with
- The recommended frequency of two to three times a week and the claim that women's libido peaks in their 30s are presented as clinical guidance/study findings by Dr Kamaraj; individual variation is large and these are not universal norms.
- Her 30–40% prevalence figure for male sexual dysfunction is given as a practice-based estimate ("we would say 30 to 40%") without a cited study in the episode.
- The link she draws between low intimacy, high cortisol and downstream gut symptoms (bloating, IBS-type complaints) reflects her and Dr Pal's clinical framing rather than established causal evidence.
- Suggestions that satisfying sexual activity by itself restores desire "without any medical help" apply, in her own account, only after medical causes such as thyroid disease or anaemia are excluded.

## Summary
Gynecologist Dr Nivetha Kamaraj tells Dr Pal that intimacy fades mainly through fear of judgment, routine and lost emotional connection — not hormones. She explains vaginismus and why sex should never be painful, the circular (Basson) model of female desire, how stress and unsatisfying experiences create a low-desire cycle, why couples should schedule intentional time together and seek expert help early instead of involving family, and covers male dysfunction, postpartum support, pre-marital counselling, IVF timing, vasectomy myths, children's sex education and menopause.
