## Important tidbits
- Whey is simply one of the two proteins in milk (the other is casein): it is the protein in the water left behind when cheese/paneer is made, and it is also present in mother's milk and infant formula — which Shashank uses to argue it is a food, not a drug-like "supplement."
- Protein targets discussed: about 1 g per kg of body weight per day for maintenance, and 1.5–2 g per kg if trying to gain muscle; on a vegetarian diet the protein usually comes bundled with many more calories (e.g., paneer), which is why whey can help within a calorie budget.
- Concentrate vs isolate is just concentration: whey dried to ~80% protein is WPC (whey protein concentrate); dried further to ~90% it is isolate. If lactose bothers you (Dr Pal found concentrate made him bloat), choose isolate.
- If you are new to whey, start with half a scoop and build up — jumping from 40–50 g of dietary protein a day to a concentrated 25–30 g scoop can itself cause bloating, acne-like complaints or queasiness that people wrongly blame on the protein.
- A clean whey needs almost nothing else: real cocoa or real coffee for flavour (not "chocolate flavour"), a natural sweetener like stevia or monk fruit if any, a real-food bulking agent like date powder rather than maltodextrin, and at most a trace (<0.1%) of a digestive enzyme such as bromelain. A pack listing ~20 ingredients is a red flag.
- Dr Pal warns that the supplement industry is largely unregulated and he has seen supplement-related liver injury (e.g., a patient whose liver enzymes were in the thousands on turmeric tablets); whey with minimal ingredients is a different category from such products.
- Both argue the real harm of additives is compounding: small daily doses of emulsifiers, artificial flavours and sweeteners across several packaged foods, over years, gradually erode gut-bacterial diversity — an effect long-term human science has not yet been able to measure.
- Label rule 1: ingredients are listed by law in decreasing order of quantity — if sugar is the first ingredient in your chocolate or your child's hazelnut spread, that is mostly what you are eating.
- Label rule 2: ignore the front of the pack entirely ("it's a Tinder profile photo; the back is the Aadhaar card picture"). Words like high protein, low fat, fresh, farm fresh and nature's bounty are often trademarks or relative claims — "high protein" may mean higher than the brand's own previous recipe (e.g., 2 g instead of 1.5 g in a biscuit).
- Label rule 3: verify any claim in the nutrition table against the serving size — check what share of your daily protein target one serving actually delivers, and drop products with ~20 ingredients, unpronounceable chemicals, or additive codes like "INS 961."
- Watch for wording tricks: "100% whole wheat" bread may be only 30–40% whole wheat (the wheat used is 100% whole), a "high fibre" biscuit may literally brand itself "Hi Fibre," green packaging signals "natural" although whole wheat is brown, and a hazelnut spread may contain only hazelnut flavour, no hazelnut.
- On the ICMR protein-supplement advisory, Shashank's reading is that ICMR's actual point was that many protein powders carry too many additives — the fix is to pick one without additives, the same advice that applies to cookies and chips, not to avoid protein.
- On organic food, his take is to buy organic only for the "dirty dozen" produce items if you can afford it, prefer local food otherwise, and not panic — he argues India's farming is less pesticide-driven and organic premiums are largely a privileged concern.
- Shashank's own journey: he yo-yo dieted through three crash cycles (losing ~40 kg, then ~30 kg, then ~25 kg by starving and running, regaining most each time) and drove his basal metabolic rate down to ~1,100–1,200 kcal before learning that fitness has three legs — endurance, strength and flexibility — which he now rotates rather than maximising one.
- Dr Pal's parallel lesson: fasting without lifting weights cost him muscle mass, so protein intake and resistance training must accompany any weight-loss method.

## Claims to be careful with
- The suggestion (via a doctor Shashank quotes) that 3,000–4,000-year-old Ayurvedic texts describe using paneer water to make atta more nutritious is an anecdote, not verified textual evidence.
- The idea that dietary emulsifiers steadily "wipe out" 1–2% of gut bacteria is extrapolated largely from animal studies and reasoning; both speakers acknowledge the long-term human effect is unproven and unmeasured.
- Claims that specific label tricks (particular spreads, biscuits, breads) apply to named categories are illustrative examples from the guest's content work, not audited findings about every such product.
- The "dirty dozen" organic-buying advice is imported from US produce-pesticide rankings; its applicability to Indian produce is the guest's opinion.

## Summary
Dr Pal talks to Shashank Mehta, founder of The Whole Truth, about why whey protein is just a concentrated milk food rather than a scary supplement, how to choose between concentrate and isolate, and why the real problem is the long ingredient lists of additives in most packaged foods. Most of the episode is a practical masterclass in reading labels — ingredient order, serving sizes, trademark and "high protein" tricks — plus both men's hard lessons about losing weight without losing muscle.
