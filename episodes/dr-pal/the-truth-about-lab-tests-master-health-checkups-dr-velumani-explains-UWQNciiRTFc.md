## Important tidbits
- On doctor–lab kickbacks ("cuts"), Dr Velumani says roughly 50% of the medical community is "cut free" and 50% is into that business; he says Thyrocare charged a single B2B rate of ₹100 for a thyroid test for the whole country, with "not even one rupee going back to anyone" — his low price put him in the "non-cut market," while intermediaries could add their own margins on top.
- His advice on trusting results: the top ~10 lab brands in any city will have equal quality because they are NABL/CAP-accredited and use the same high-end automated machines (Siemens, Roche, etc.); errors still occur, maybe 1 in 100 or 1 in 1,000 (air bubbles, low sample aspiration). Pick one good lab for chronic illness monitoring and stick to it, because lab-to-lab variation confuses more than same-lab variation.
- His punchline for conflicting doctor/lab trust: "If you trust the doctor, change the lab. If you trust the lab, change the doctor."
- Not all tests are equally reliable, he stresses: thyroid tests, HbA1c, blood sugar and CBC are ~100% reliable ("truthful" tests), whereas a tuberculosis test can be false-positive/false-negative a large share of the time (he cites ~60%/40% error patterns), and cardiac and cancer tests are not reliable as screens "unless there is a reason to doubt."
- Master health checkups should only screen for disorders that are frequently encountered, he says — not everything. Dr Pal gives the example of CA 19-9, a pancreatic tumour marker included in packages: a healthy 40-year-old with a mildly raised value (80, vs a normal upper limit around 40–50) fell into depression fearing pancreatic cancer, even though studies show CA 19-9 has no diagnostic or prognostic role in a normal, healthy person.
- Vitamin D is his example of a misleading package test: ~90% of people test "abnormal," which he says means the normal range itself is wrongly defined; mild deficiency carries no short-term health risk.
- He admits he created the "many tests for little price" marketing himself (T3/T4 free with TSH; lipid and liver profiles free with a thyroid test; "25 tests for ₹1,000") and calls these "optical strategies of marketing" — per-test prices of ~₹17 make people buy packages they may not need. His request to listeners: when a master checkup is recommended, talk to your doctor about whether you really need those tests.
- Scale is why his prices could be lowest yet ~40% profitable, he says: once he asked "why can't a sample fly?", samples from across India reached one Mumbai lab the same night, replacing the old model of pathologists serving a 20 km radius.
- On his wife's pancreatic cancer: despite running what he calls the world's largest preventive-care testing laboratory, he never screened his own family; she was diagnosed at stage 4 with no prior symptoms and died ~60 days after diagnosis (after a Whipple surgery she chose to undergo). He believes frequent abdominal ultrasounds might have caught it at "peanut size" rather than "lemon size." US (MSK) and Indian teams also gave differing diagnoses (neuroendocrine tumour vs the other reading).
- On cost differences: a TSH costs ~$60 in the US vs ~$6 (₹300–500) in India, which he attributes to per-capita income and manpower costs, not profiteering — "India is cheapest in healthcare in the entire world."
- On regulation, he says India already has rules on paper barring pharma from influencing doctors (unlike the US disclosure system Dr Pal describes, where even a pen must be reported), but favours are still passed unofficially — while adding that dishonesty is declining year after year.
- His work-life view: work-life balance is only an issue if you don't enjoy the job or are driven by fear (job loss, EMIs); he worked 7 am–11 pm for years because he enjoyed it, and exited Thyrocare when the fun stopped and one more zero in net worth made no difference.
- Advice for doctors building businesses: separate procurement from processing (let franchise/agency partners procure, you only process/treat), barcode everything, employ paramedical staff for paramedical work (citing Aravind Eye Hospital, where ~50 of 60 minutes of a cataract workflow is paramedical), and build a training system that creates "duplicates" of yourself — most medical businesses fail, he says, because the doctor believes no one else can do it like them. He also estimates only ~10–12 of 1,000 doctors become entrepreneurs vs ~100 of 1,000 engineers.

## Claims to be careful with
- His "50% of doctors take cuts" figure is his personal estimate from industry experience, not audited data.
- The claim that thyroid/HbA1c/blood sugar/CBC tests are "100% reliable" and that TB testing is wrong 40–60% of the time are his characterisations; real-world accuracy varies by test, lab and clinical context.
- His statement that ~90% of people have abnormal vitamin D because the reference range is wrong is his opinion; mainstream guidance still treats low vitamin D as common and clinically relevant in many groups.
- His regret that screening ultrasounds could have caught his wife's pancreatic cancer earlier is a personal counterfactual — pancreatic cancer screening is not generally recommended for average-risk people, and the episode itself notes the cancer was symptomless and fast-growing.

## Summary
Thyrocare founder Dr Velumani talks candidly about the business and ethics of diagnostics: how scale let him sell a thyroid test for ₹100 nationwide with no kickbacks, why he estimates half the medical community still takes lab "cuts," how to choose a trustworthy lab, and why master health checkups cause harm when they include unreliable tests like tumour markers. Woven through is the personal story of losing his wife to stage-4 pancreatic cancer 60 days after diagnosis — despite running a preventive-testing empire — plus his philosophies on work, money and building scalable medical businesses.
