## Important tidbits
- Dr Pal's framework is the "3 Fs" — fiber, fermented food and fasting — which he says brings the fourth F, fun; Zarish's per-meal rule is 20 g of protein and 10 g of fiber in every meal.
- Practical fiber hacks: stir chopped cucumber, carrot and onion with chaat masala into curd to make a raita (a "raw salad" people actually enjoy, with probiotics included), and cook pulao/fried rice with far more vegetables than rice.
- Grate paneer over a dosa to add protein to a carb-heavy breakfast, and aim for one vegetable and one fruit with every meal — 2–3 fruit servings a day, up to 4 for very active people.
- Glycemic load matters more than glycemic index: watermelon has a GI of ~72 but a big bowl has only ~60 calories, while a small bowl of ice cream has a GI of ~54 yet ~200 calories — so "ice cream is better than watermelon" reels are cherry-picking.
- If you want high-glycemic fruit (banana, mango, custard apple), eat it before a workout, walk or yoga session, or early in the morning/after a fast when insulin sensitivity is higher; an apple and a banana both carry roughly 18–20 g of carbs.
- Guava is a low-glycemic-load fruit he personally favors in winter; rotate fruits by season and mood rather than eating only one kind.
- Dr Pal calls kiwi a "superfood" for constipation patients, crediting its effect on gut bacteria and bowel movement; cucumber and carrot sticks are Zarish's go-to fiber snack.
- Fermented foods are everywhere in India: idli, dosa and dhokla, plus regional options like kanji (fermented black carrot drink from Punjab), enduri pitha (Odisha) and fermented bamboo shoots (Assam); at home he makes sambar thick with plenty of dal rather than the watery restaurant version.
- When ordering food in, choose veggie-loaded, less-processed options (e.g., a multigrain Subway-style sandwich) over mayo-heavy items — he recounts being served a "cheese and vegetable sandwich" that was just mayonnaise between bread slices.
- Weight training is genuinely functional — deadlifts, squats and overhead presses mimic lifting and placing objects in daily life and support joint health — but it is only one method: do the activity you enjoy (yoga, swimming, home workouts) because sustainability is what matters, and he calls the carnivore diet the scariest trend because it has no fiber and is unsustainable for most people.
- Ancestral eating is widely misunderstood: hunter-gatherers like the Hadza eat ~150 g of fiber a day (vs ~15 g in a Western diet), and early humans likely ate meat only about once in 20 days, filling up on tubers, berries and plants the rest of the time.
- Carbs are not inherently fattening: Zarish says he eats ~300 g of carbs a day while losing fat and that clients do too; overeating any macro — fat or even protein — can be converted to fat. Brown rice adds only ~1–2 g more fiber than white rice, so white rice plus plenty of vegetables is the tastier, more sustainable choice.
- On fats: prefer cold-pressed oils over refined where possible, use roughly 2–3 teaspoons of oil per meal per person, and draw fats from multiple sources (ghee, coconut oil, nuts, seeds like flax and pumpkin) for omega-3s and minerals like zinc.
- Calorie awareness beats obsessive calorie counting, which creates food anxiety in many people; simply knowing that one samosa is ~550 kcal changes behavior subconsciously, and he argues restaurants should be required to print calories next to dishes like a 1,500 kcal biryani.

## Claims to be careful with
- Figures quoted from memory in conversation — a samosa at ~550 kcal, a restaurant biryani at ~1,500 kcal, Hadza fiber intake of ~150 g/day — are the speakers' estimates, not verified measurements.
- Dr Pal's description of kiwi as a proven constipation "superfood" rests on limited research he references loosely.
- Zarish's personal 300 g-carb fat-loss experience is anecdotal and individual, not a general prescription.

## Summary
Nutritionist Zarish (Zealocity) joins Dr Pal to make fiber, fermented food and protein practical: build every meal around 20 g protein and 10 g fiber, judge foods by glycemic load rather than index, load pulaos and raitas with vegetables, pick any exercise you will actually sustain, and use calorie awareness rather than fear of carbs or fat to guide eating.
