## Important tidbits
- About 10% of the children Dr Rakesh Kumar sees are overweight or obese, and childhood obesity is underdiagnosed in India because parents cannot assess it at home — height and weight must be plotted on a growth chart by a doctor (Dr Pal's own son was at the 90th percentile before anyone noticed).
- Take children for a wellness visit at least once a year even when they are healthy, so growth can be plotted and small issues picked up early.
- Fatty liver (NAFLD) is now appearing in children: of 100 obese children, roughly 10–15 will have it, he estimates.
- When a child is diagnosed overweight/obese, his first priority is reducing food intake (a calorie deficit planned with a pediatric gastroenterologist or dietitian), plus at least 1 hour a day of real physical play — one boy "played football" on a video game for 3 months and gained 3 kg instead.
- Children need 8–9 hours of sound sleep; a consistent sleep routine keeps the circadian rhythm and melatonin normal, which in turn regulates hunger. Wake the child at least 2 hours before the school bus so mornings are relaxed and breakfast is not skipped.
- Screen-time limits he cites from the Indian Academy of Pediatrics: no screen time at all under age 2; ages 2–5 a maximum of 1 hour a day, split into 30-minute stretches; above 5 years a maximum of 2 hours a day, split up and meant for educational use. Boredom is not a reason to watch TV — boredom is for creativity.
- Screens at mealtimes make children lose interest in the food itself; he recommends screen-free meals, a daily family no-screen period, and no phones/TV in the bedroom.
- Feed on demand — the rule starts with newborns and applies to older children too: a child who can express hunger will ask for food. Overfeeding (insisting the plate be finished) is the bigger problem, and being lean does not mean unhealthy.
- Height is largely set by mid-parental height — for a girl, (mother's height + father's height − 13) / 2, ±8 cm. Extra food does not make a calorie-sufficient child taller, but chronic calorie deficiency does stunt growth.
- Never force-feed: forcing food into a child's throat can create what he calls post-traumatic feeding disorder and a lasting aversion to the dining table. Offer new foods repeatedly on different days without forcing; the "three-bite rule" rarely works.
- Obesity can cause obstructive sleep apnea in children — snoring, waking at night, and sleeping in class the next day. If a teacher reports daytime sleepiness, check the child's weight.
- A child should pass stool at least 3 times a week, without pain or distress. Chronic constipation can cause fecal incontinence (soiling) even in 7–9-year-olds, and recurrent urinary tract infections via bowel-bladder dysfunction.
- Start toilet training at 1.5–2 years: sit the child on the toilet for 10–15 minutes after meals (using the gastrocolic reflex), following a "rule of one" — same toilet, same time, same caregiver, minimal talking, no phones. On a western toilet the feet must rest on a stool, not dangle. Laxatives are only a 4–6-month bridge while the habit is trained.
- He is more worried about high-fructose and trans-fat ultra-processed foods than about MSG: they drive obesity and kill off good gut bacteria (dysbiosis). Eggs — including ordinary broiler eggs — are good for children despite WhatsApp/Facebook myths.
- Button and disc batteries are the most dangerous swallowed objects: they cause caustic injury and later narrowing (stricture) of the food pipe. If a child swallows any object, go straight to a pediatrician/pediatric gastroenterologist and give nothing by mouth (not even banana) beforehand.
- See a gastroenterologist if diarrhea persists beyond 2–3 weeks, especially with blood in stool, unexplained vomiting, or poor growth — inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's, ulcerative colitis) is now seen in Indian children as young as 2.5 years.

## Claims to be careful with
- The suggestion that rising pediatric IBD in India is mainly caused by "western food" and gut bacteria is presented as the doctors' theory; IBD is multifactorial and this is not established causation.
- Specific figures (10% obesity in his practice, 10–15% fatty liver among obese children, infliximab costs of ₹17,000–25,000 per infusion in Tamil Nadu) are one clinician's practice experience, not population data.

## Summary
Pediatric gastroenterologist Dr Rakesh Kumar tells Dr Pal that childhood obesity in India is common but missed, because parents judge by "chubbiness" instead of growth charts. The fixes are unglamorous and interconnected: an annual wellness visit, a calorie-aware plate without force-feeding, 8–9 hours of sleep, strict age-based screen limits with screen-free meals, one hour of real outdoor play, and toilet training built on the post-meal reflex. He also flags fatty liver and IBD in children, constipation's hidden complications, and the danger of swallowed button batteries.
