## Important tidbits
- Dr Priyanka says bloating, excess gas, burping and mild abdominal discomfort are rising in children mainly because of excess junk food, lack of fibre, irregular meal timings and poor sleep — leading to bad digestion and more bad bacteria than good bacteria in the gut, which produces abnormal gas.
- In infants under one year, mild bloating and gas can be normal because the gut, its bacteria and motility are still maturing. Danger signs that need an expert, not dietary guesswork: severe pain with bloating, vomiting, severe diarrhoea, or constipation combined with vomiting and bloating.
- The big missed diagnosis in 1–5 year olds is "hidden constipation": children of 2–3 years withhold stool (busy playing, don't want to go), the rectum dilates, and the sensation threshold for needing to pass stool falls. Signs parents miss: hard stools, very small stool amounts, irregular timing. Her advice: watch your child's stools in the pot every day — and ask maids/caregivers specific questions, since working parents often never see them.
- Once constipation becomes chronic, she says, laxatives may be needed for months to years to clear the stool and restore the rectum's size and sensation — by 5–10 years she sees children not passing stool for 3–4, even 7–10 days. Detecting it early avoids that.
- Consistent stool timing depends on sleep: children need a minimum of 9–10 hours, should be in bed around 8 pm, and should wake at least one hour before school time so there is unhurried time to drink, eat and use the washroom. A rushed morning activates the gut–brain axis, and the child then holds stool at school out of hesitation.
- Screens before bed delay sleep because screen light reaching the retina reduces melatonin secretion; she advises stopping screens and starting to unwind about three hours before bed, with an early dinner and adequate daytime physical activity, so the child sleeps by about 8:30–9 pm.
- Acid reflux is now common in children, she says — she performs endoscopies on kids and finds redness and even stomach ulcers, driven by stress, junk food, irregular meal timings and vitamin/mineral deficiencies that loosen the valve between the food pipe and stomach. Children don't report heartburn; they complain of chest pain/discomfort and tummy pain.
- In pregnancy, bloating and reflux are largely physiological: progesterone relaxes the GI tract's smooth muscles so the same valve loosens, and the growing uterus compresses the stomach and intestines. Heavy, fatty and spicy meals aggravate it.
- On iodine: pink salt and rock salt contain only about 1–2 ppm iodine versus about 15–30 ppm in iodized salt, and their trace minerals are too little to meet daily needs. Requirements she cites: children about 90–150 micrograms/day, adults about 150 micrograms/day, pregnant women about 220 micrograms/day. A vegetarian day of food alone cannot reach the iodine requirement — the reason for India's salt-iodization policy. Her rule: use iodized salt daily; keep pink/rock salt only for occasional fasting use, never as the replacement.
- A home iodine check she describes: slice a potato, squeeze lemon on it, add the salt — iodized salt turns it bluish-grey within about 5 minutes. Iodine test kits (a card and solution that turns blue, intensity indicating ppm) are also available, she says. Dr Pal cites figures of 9 million pregnant women and 8 million newborns in India at increased risk of iodine deficiency disorders, and children losing about 13.5 IQ points when iodine deficient.
- Iron deficiency, she says, may affect 60–70% of women and adolescent girls. Beyond low hemoglobin it causes easy fatigability, poor concentration, learning difficulties, lower IQ, poor immunity and loss of appetite — so a teenager whose school performance and attention suddenly drop and who is always tired should get blood work (CBC, hemoglobin, iron and B12 levels checked). She calls worm infestation the most common cause of iron deficiency in India, ahead of menstrual losses in adolescent girls and women.
- Deworming protocol she prescribes: albendazole as a single dose — 200 mg for children under 2 years, 400 mg above 2 years — repeated once every six months until age 18 (government school programmes do this); adults with anaemia, heavy travel or outside-food exposure and poor hygiene can also be dewormed.
- Iron absorption rules: pair iron-rich foods (spinach, dates, ragi, green leafy vegetables, legumes, jaggery) with vitamin C (orange juice, amla juice, lemon) — but take them after meals, not on an empty stomach, because citrus and iron supplements on an empty stomach cause gastritis, acidity and constipation. Black stools on iron supplements are expected and not a cause for alarm, though she notes GI bleeding can also blacken stools. Dr Pal adds that double-fortified salt (iodine + iron) gives roughly 25% of daily iron needs per tablespoon.
- Allergies are rising, she says, because processed foods (pizza, pasta, burgers, packaged juices, dressings, ketchups) full of preservatives, thickeners and artificial sweeteners change gut bacteria and the gut lining — and 70% of the immune system sits in the gut, so immunity becomes over-reactive. She states food-allergy prevalence has risen from about 10% to 30% over the last two to three decades, with 30–40% of the population now having some allergic problem.
- She explains the hygiene hypothesis: early exposure to soil, street food and pathogens trains a balanced immune system; excessive hygiene delays exposure until after immune maturation (about 7–8 years), producing more allergy and asthma. Peanut anaphylaxis is very common in the US, she says, but she has not seen a single case in her Indian practice.
- Milk allergy versus lactose intolerance: milk allergy is an immune over-reaction — it can cause skin rash, recurrent cough/cold and sinus congestion, vomiting, diarrhoea, blood in stool, and she has seen ulcers on endoscopy even in one-year-olds; most cases are non-IgE mediated with delayed, indirect symptoms (bloating, discomfort, rashes after 2–3 days). Lactose intolerance is lactase-enzyme deficiency: gut-only symptoms (bloating, gas, mild diarrhoea), no skin or respiratory involvement, and often transient after food poisoning or viral infection damages the gut lining. Critically: for suspected milk allergy under one year, do not stop breast milk — remove milk and milk products from the mother's diet instead (with calcium supplementation for the mother); excess top milk in infancy itself causes anaemia and iron deficiency, and no top milk is advised before one year.
- Celiac disease must be separated from wheat allergy and gluten sensitivity. Celiac is immune-mediated and needs strict, lifelong gluten elimination; it can present with only low hemoglobin and tiredness, so she orders the tTG-IgA test almost like a CBC in every low-hemoglobin case. She calls celiac "like an epidemic" in North India — in Rajasthan she saw about 40 paediatric celiac cases a day — typically with stunted growth, anaemia, iron deficiency and poor concentration from malabsorption; missed cases can progress to liver fibrosis, epilepsy, infertility and intestinal lymphoma. Wheat allergy (positive skin prick test) is less strict and may be outgrown; gluten sensitivity has all tests negative with a healthy-looking intestine.
- The "daily mistake" of the title is eating with screens: feeding a child with a mobile, or eating while watching, disturbs the gut–brain axis — the child eats too fast or too little, doesn't chew, salivary digestion is skipped, and satiety signals (stomach distension, glucose levels) are overridden, causing poor digestion, overeating, obesity and fatty liver, which she now sees in children as a paediatric hepatologist. Her closing prescription: eat wholesome food (whole grains, legumes, pulses, nuts, seeds, fruit) mindfully without screens, hydrate, stay physically active — and "live in the present moment."

## Claims to be careful with
- Her prevalence figures — 30–40% of the population allergic, food allergy rising from 10% to 30%, and 60–70% of women and adolescent girls being iron deficient — are stated without sources in the episode and blur allergy, allergic disorders and deficiency definitions.
- A substantial middle segment promotes a specific iodized/double-fortified salt brand and its CSR awareness programme, in which Dr Pal says he participates — a commercial interest behind the (otherwise mainstream) iodized-salt advice. One on-air claim about the brand's iodine content per 100 g appears garbled in the captions and should not be relied on.
- She recounts a patient whose 3–4 years of IBS cramps resolved after a single meditation/counselling session, and describes autoimmune diseases as "basically stress induced" with antibodies developing after long internal conflict — anecdote and oversimplification, not established causation.
- The claim that restaurant frying oil is "boiled 100 times" and loaded with carcinogenic chemicals is rhetorical rather than measured.
- Her deworming schedule (albendazole every six months for all children to age 18, and for many adults) is her practice recommendation; dosing and frequency should follow a treating doctor's advice.
- Broad claims that breast milk prevents future diabetes, heart disease, obesity and fatty liver, and that milk "causes increased phlegm," go beyond what the episode supports with evidence.

## Summary
Paediatric gastroenterologist and lifestyle-medicine consultant Dr Priyanka joins Dr Pal (in Hinglish) to trace gas, acidity and poor digestion back to daily habits: junk food, irregular meals, poor sleep and — the title's daily mistake — eating with screens, which breaks chewing, satiety signals and the gut–brain axis. She covers hidden constipation and stool routines in children, reflux in kids and pregnancy, why iodized salt must not be swapped for pink salt, iron deficiency and six-monthly deworming, the allergy surge and hygiene hypothesis, milk allergy versus lactose intolerance, and celiac disease in North India, closing with mindful eating, counselling/meditation for functional gut problems, and eating wholesome food in the present moment.
