## Important tidbits
- Dry eye is a three-layer problem: the tear film has a mucin layer against the cornea (which is naturally water-repelling), a middle aqueous layer from the lacrimal gland, and an outer lipid layer from the meibomian glands that stops evaporation. Disruption of any one layer causes dry-eye syndrome — and air conditioning accelerates evaporation. Dr Pal's own resident ignored chronic dry eye until he developed a corneal ulcer mid-procedure.
- Blinking is the mechanism that spreads tears, delivers nutrition and washes toxins out — and it collapses on screens: a normal blink rate is 15–20 per minute, but while viewing any screen (phone, laptop, iPad, TV) it drops to 5–10 per minute, roughly 50% less. Make a conscious effort to blink more.
- The screen-time epidemic has two causes, he says: social media (from ~2016) and COVID lockdowns. Consequences go beyond dry eye and red eye to rising myopia and wrecked sleep, because screen blue light blocks melatonin and raises dopamine and body temperature — after late screen use you typically can't sleep for about two hours.
- Blue-light glasses are a "Band-Aid": most block only 30–50% of blue light, and he says you need 90–95% blocking for real impact, asking the optometrist for the percentage. The real fix is timing — stop screens after 8 p.m. so you can sleep by 10 and not miss the circadian window; he sets his own phone to night mode automatically after 6 p.m. Blue-light filters also exist for laptops, phone screen guards and TVs, and using voice-to-text and voice notes cuts typing screen time.
- Reading light is a yin-yang: dim light helps circadian rhythm but strains the eye, so keep ambient light dim but put a bright clip-on light on the book or page itself.
- Two desk habits for digital eye strain: the 20-20-20 rule — every 20 minutes, look at something 20 feet away for 20 seconds — and palming, cupping your hands over closed eyes for 20 seconds after an hour of screen work. A warm compress opens the meibomian glands and restores the lipid layer; a cold compress (the real reason cucumber slices help) improves circulation. For children: incentivise one hour of outdoor sport daily — sunlight and distance viewing are the antidote to eyes locked in near-flexion.
- Why it matters: studies project half the world's population will be myopic by 2050, and about 1.1 billion of 8.1 billion people already have uncorrected refractive error. Chronic dry eye also stagnates the tear film, drops local immunity, and lets infections (e.g. adenovirus) ulcerate the cornea — a chain that can end in transplant or vision loss; his group performs roughly 1,200 corneal transplants a year.
- Corneal transplant facts: a whole eye can never be transplanted (the optic nerve, an extension of the brain, cannot be reconnected) — only the cornea. The cornea has six layers (the sixth was discovered in 2013), and modern lamellar transplants replace only the affected layers, so one donor's two corneas can restore sight for four people. Eyes must be retrieved within about six hours of death; only the corneal "button" is taken now, so the face looks normal afterwards. Around 8 million people worldwide are corneal-blind, and donation in India lags because of cultural and religious taboos — the US exports surplus corneas.
- Refractive errors come in four types: myopia (minus power, near clear, far blurred), hyperopia (plus power), astigmatism (cornea shaped like the back of a spoon, skewing one axis), and presbyopia (age-related, now starting around 38–40). No refractive surgery under 18 because the eye is still growing. LASIK cuts a ~22 mm flap and laser-sculpts the cornea (so it needs adequate corneal thickness); SMILE uses a 4 mm incision to remove a lenticule — less dry eye, better healing, and now 70% of his practice versus 30% LASIK (the US lags because of FDA/insurance approvals). He calls laser vision correction the most successful surgery on the planet at 98–99% success, with failures usually traceable to poor pre-operative judgment.
- Contact lenses are not harmless fashion: everyone eventually becomes intolerant; sleeping in lenses risks corneal infection, including Acanthamoeba from lens solutions growing between lens and cornea overnight; and lenses warp the corneal surface, so stop wearing them at least a week before any LASIK workup. Given a suitable cornea, he recommends LASIK over lenses any day.
- "Madras eye" (pink eye) is NOT spread by looking at someone — that is a complete myth. It spreads by hand-to-face contact, so wash hands with soap every 20 minutes. A true red eye has at least one of watering, redness, pain or itching; stay home about three days. Discharge tells the cause: watery suggests viral (no specific treatment, but antibiotic drops are added to prevent bacterial superinfection), thick yellow suggests bacterial (needs antibiotics), thick white suggests fungal. Pinkish eyes after long screen sessions — dilated vessels — are not red eye.
- Myopia-control glasses for children work on a newly understood mechanism: ordinary lenses focus the centre sharply but land peripheral rays behind the peripheral retina, which signals the eye to elongate and worsens myopia; control lenses curve the focus so that elongation trigger is removed. He is not pushy about chronic atropine drops in children.
- Myths busted: never look at the sun to improve your eyes — it can burn the macula; wear sunglasses outdoors because UV is directly linked to cataract and age-related macular degeneration, but don't wear them indoors, where they fool your circadian rhythm. Rubbing your eyes is harmful and is linked to keratoconus, where the cornea deforms from a dome into a cone and may need cross-linking, rigid or scleral lenses, or surgery. Never put rose water, castor oil, turmeric or Ayurvedic drops into the eye without a prescription — the pH-controlled cornea can turn opaque.
- Kajal belongs outside the lashes only: applied on the inner lid margin it chronically blocks the meibomian gland pores, cutting off the tear film's lipid layer — which is why such patients tear excessively yet have dry eyes (the tears won't stick and roll off) — and stagnant glands form styes that may need incision surgery.
- Food and supplements: vitamin-A-rich food is good, but excess carrots cause carotenemia (yellow skin) with no extra eye benefit. Lutein and zeaxanthin are pigments that sit in the macula, filter blue light, act as antioxidants and help prevent age-related macular degeneration; eat green leafy vegetables, fruits, grains, nuts, seeds and fatty fish (omega-3), and consider supplements — his five are omega-3, lutein, zeaxanthin, zinc and vitamin C — generally after age 45–50, and only on top of a balanced diet, since supplements cannot offset excessive carbs, sugar and sodium.

## Claims to be careful with
- "Half the world will be myopic by 2050" is a published projection he cites from studies, but it is an extrapolation, not a certainty, and no specific study is named in the episode.
- The figure of 1.1 billion people with uncorrected refractive error and 8 million corneal-blind are given conversationally; they are broadly in line with global estimates but should not be quoted as precise.
- His requirement of 90–95% blue-light blocking for glasses to work is his clinical position; the overall evidence base for blue-light glasses is contested, as he partly acknowledges — the episode's stronger, better-supported advice is reducing screen time itself.
- The claim that we touch our face "close to 10,000 times a day" appears inflated relative to observational studies (which suggest far fewer); the underlying hygiene point stands regardless.
- Eye rubbing is presented as a trigger of keratoconus; in the literature rubbing is a risk factor and aggravator alongside genetic predisposition, not a proven sole cause — he does mention genetic causes as well.
- Describing the eye as having "a trillion neurons" is rhetorical emphasis on complexity rather than an anatomical count.

## Summary
Ophthalmologist Dr Ashvin Agarwal warns that the social-media-and-COVID screen era is producing an epidemic of digital eye strain: blink rates halve on screens, tear films break down, melatonin is suppressed, and myopia is climbing toward a projected half of humanity by 2050. His defences are behavioural — no screens after 8 p.m., night mode, the 20-20-20 rule, palming, warm compresses, an hour of outdoor play for children — backed by clear-eyed explainers on dry-eye anatomy, corneal transplants (only the cornea, never the whole eye; one donor can help four people), LASIK versus SMILE, contact-lens risks, pink-eye myths, kajal blocking the lid glands, and lutein/zeaxanthin nutrition after 45.
