## Important tidbits
- Weight gain is multifactorial, says Dr Moodin: genetics, hormonal conditions in women (PCOS, hypothyroidism), insulin resistance (insulin is "prolipogenic" — it accumulates fat), screen time and sedentary jobs, and food that delivery apps make available within minutes. Indians in particular carry central (belly) obesity even when not obese overall.
- Basal metabolic rate (BMR) is the core variable: if your BMR is around 2,000 kcal but you habitually eat more than you burn, the surplus is stored as fat. BMR varies with age, sex, genetics, geography and hormones, falls after age 40, and in his clinic even 150–180 kg patients often have a low measured BMR on body-composition (InBody) scans — which is why simply eating less often fails them.
- He estimates BMR is roughly 40% genetic: parents with a low BMR tend to pass it on. BMR can be nudged up with daily habits — stairs instead of lifts, parking farther away, less rice/sugar/carbs, high-protein food, and making fitness a routine rather than a luxury.
- Alcohol is a major hidden driver: it is very calorie-dense, is converted and stored as fat, and about 30–40% of his obese patients drink significantly. Alcohol plus obesity is a "double whammy" for fatty liver, which progresses grade 1 → 2 → 3 → cirrhosis; grade 1 fatty liver has become so common it is almost treated as normal.
- Visceral fat — the fat attached to the intestines and liver, not the subcutaneous fat under the skin — is the dangerous depot behind diabetes and blood pressure. On body-composition scans it should be under 10 (7–8 is ideal); a reading of 20 is a "danger symbol." Grade 2 or worse fatty liver on ultrasound must be addressed before it reaches cirrhosis.
- The body defends a "fat set point": repeated short, inconsistent dieting attempts derange that set point further, which is why people lose 4–5 kg and regain it within two months. Women with PCOS plus hypothyroidism face a doubly slowed system — high TSH (around 10–15 versus a normal of about 5) slows metabolism, reflexes and mood — and he stresses these patients often need twice the effort and should not simply be blamed.
- Sleep is a weight factor in both directions: sleeping only 4–5 hours is associated with about a 22% increased risk of obesity; in a study Dr Pal cites, patients sleeping under 7 hours had BMRs of only about 800–1,000 kcal. Obesity also causes obstructive sleep apnea — fat blocks the airway, patients sleep propped up at 45 degrees on BiPAP/CPAP machines, and apnea itself can be fatal.
- He recommends five small meals a day (three meals plus a fruit meal and a vegetable meal) for his patients, because large meals produce large insulin spikes and insulin spikes drive fat storage; the alternative pattern discussed is fewer meals with at least 4–5 hours between lunch and dinner and about 12 hours between dinner and the next meal.
- Bariatric (increasingly called metabolic) surgery is laparoscopic: the stomach is reduced and joined to the intestine, so patients eat four to five small meals, hunger falls, satiety comes early, ghrelin (the hunger hormone) drops significantly and GLP-1 release rises. Patients typically lose about 80% of their excess weight over 8–10 months — e.g. about 45 kg of a 60 kg excess.
- Who qualifies: roughly, being 25 kg or more above your ideal weight (height in cm minus 100), i.e. a BMI of about 32.5+; morbid obesity is a BMI of 35+ for Indians (40 in the US criteria) because Indians carry more visceral fat. He operates on patients of 200 kg and above and says delay driven by fear is what turns operable 130 kg patients into high-risk 200+ kg patients.
- After surgery for PCOS, periods typically regularise within six months, excess hair and voice hoarseness reduce, IVF outcomes improve and some women conceive naturally — but conception should wait at least one year after surgery. Subclinical hypothyroidism also often improves, with thyroid doses sometimes reduced or stopped, though he gives no guarantee.
- Regain after surgery is mainly a follow-up failure: he wants reviews every three months, soft food, thorough chewing, spacing between eating, about an hour's walk daily, and avoiding sugar, bakery items, cola/Pepsi and other fizzy drinks, which stretch the reduced stomach.
- On GLP-1 medicines (Ozempic, Mounjaro, Wegovy): they suit the overweight/intermediate group (roughly BMI 25–32), not the morbidly obese, and are temporary — weight returns when the drug stops. His comparison: medicines may remove only about 10% of excess weight versus about 80% with surgery, and he lists side effects including pancreatitis, nausea/vomiting and gastroparesis. Mounjaro costs about ₹4,000 per weekly injection (about ₹15,000/month at the 2.5 mg dose).
- Insurance increasingly covers bariatric surgery because insurers now accept obesity as a disease when it comes with metabolic problems (uncontrolled diabetes, blood pressure, sleep apnea, diabetes-related cardiomyopathy), though policies carry two-to-three-year waiting periods and corporate policies approve more readily than individual ones.
- His inspiring case: a girl who reached 130 kg at 16 with severe PCOS and no periods for two years; after failed medicines and IVF refusal at 138 kg, surgery brought her under 100 kg, she conceived naturally within 9–10 months, and five years on she is about 68–70 kg with two children.
- Rapid-fire verdicts: stop eating sweets; cardio over strength training; the diet myth he hates is that apple cider vinegar causes weight loss; the most common weight-loss mistake is not toning muscle while losing weight; obesity is a disease.

## Claims to be careful with
- The figures he gives — BMR being "roughly 40% genetic," 30–40% of obese patients drinking heavily, 80% excess-weight loss with surgery versus 10% with medicines, and a 22% obesity risk increase with 4–5 hours of sleep — are his clinical estimates stated without cited sources.
- His statement that GLP-1 medicines can cause "blindness" among their side effects is an unusually strong claim presented without context in the episode.
- The claim that surgeons sometimes find a frankly cirrhotic liver at surgery when ultrasound/CT looked relatively normal is presented as frequent clinical experience; imaging can miss advanced disease, but the episode gives no rates.
- Rules of thumb such as "height in cm minus 100, plus 25 kg qualifies you for surgery" and visceral fat cut-offs are simplifications of formal criteria, as he partly acknowledges.

## Summary
Bariatric and metabolic surgeon Dr Moodin explains to Dr Pal why eating less so often fails: a low, partly genetic basal metabolic rate, insulin resistance, PCOS and hypothyroidism, alcohol, poor sleep and a deranged fat set point all defend the higher weight. He walks through how he assesses patients (body-composition BMR and visceral fat, liver ultrasound), when medicines like Mounjaro fit versus when metabolic surgery is appropriate, what the laparoscopic surgery actually changes hormonally (ghrelin down, GLP-1 up, about 80% of excess weight lost), and why follow-up, sleep and stigma-free, individualised treatment matter more than blame.
