## Important tidbits
- Sunscreen is for everyone now, regardless of skin colour: Indians sit around Fitzpatrick type 3 (range 2–5) and melanin gives partial protection, but modern overuse of actives has damaged most people's skin barrier, and a damaged barrier lets sun damage through. Our grandmothers could skip sunscreen because the ozone layer was intact, their food supplied internal antioxidants, and they never experimented on their skin.
- SPF 30–50 is enough — beyond 50 the added protection is negligible (SPF 50 already blocks ~99%). To actually get the labelled SPF you need about two finger-lengths for face and neck, and reapplication every 2–3 hours in the sun; powder sunscreens and roll-on sticks make reapplication practical.
- Application order: nourish → protect → enhance. Serums/essence and moisturiser go first, sunscreen on top of them, makeup last. Moisturiser is the single most important barrier protector — a well-moisturised skin protects itself.
- The commonest barrier disruptors she sees are self-prescribed products: vitamin C and hyaluronic acid (an acid despite the marketing — it burns dry, chapped, sensitive skin), and acne patients stacking salicylic acid, benzoyl peroxide and antibiotic gels non-stop. Cheap products may contain token amounts or poor grades of an ingredient; very potent ingredients are double-edged swords, like ICU drugs.
- Melasma is multifactorial and usually symmetrical (cheeks, jaw, upper lip, brow): it is triggered by steep hormonal changes — birth-control pills, pregnancy, menopause — compounded by sun exposure and barrier insult (e.g., starting retinol, irritation, then pigmentation). Men get it too, and everyday friction/shaving causes "frictional melanosis." Sunglasses help: they stop squinting (fewer frown/under-eye lines) and protect the thin under-eye skin.
- "Internal sunscreen": traditional Indian food supplied daily antioxidants and anti-inflammatories — turmeric/curcumin, pepper (piperine), ginger, tulsi — which modern diets have lost. She personally takes an antioxidant set, an anti-inflammatory set and vitamin C daily as internal protection, alongside topical sunscreen.
- The gut–skin axis: gram-negative Bacteroides produce LPS (lipopolysaccharide); a high-fat diet promotes them, LPS seeps into the blood, and the resulting inflammation (IL-12, TNF-alpha, micro-capillary swelling) dehydrates and damages skin. She has started giving general gut probiotics to atopic dermatitis patients with good results in her practice.
- Weight is a root driver she returns to repeatedly: weight gain underlies PCOS, hormonal imbalance, thyroid problems and acanthosis/insulin resistance. Every patient in her clinic gets a diet sheet of inclusions and exclusions aimed at reducing inflammation and high-glycemic-index food.
- On probiotics, both doctors agree the specific deficient strain for an individual is unknown, so megadoses of one strain are questionable; lower doses with more variety — or simply fermented foods plus a high-fiber diet — make more sense. Routine stool microbiome testing is not recommended for the general population.
- Hygiene hypothesis: over-sanitising (which spiked after COVID) kills protective microbes, and gut-associated lymphoid tissue that is never exposed to dirt/allergens becomes hyperactive and attacks the self — one reason eczema and allergies are rising in over-protected children.
- Eating window: she endorses Dr Pal's sunrise-to-sunset approach and stopping food by ~7–8 pm so night-time repair and growth hormones can work; a minimum ~12-hour fast is the baseline requirement. Autophagy, she says, begins slowly at 18–24 hours and peaks at 2–3 days, so one-day monthly fasts don't meaningfully trigger it — and extremes aren't needed.
- Bloating after 45/menopause: falling estrogen/progesterone slows small-intestine motility; stagnant food (even healthy carbs like chana and rajma) is fermented by bacteria into gas. Lentils and dals are still carbs; paneer is protein.
- GLP-1 weight-loss drugs (Ozempic, Mounjaro) work partly by slowing gut motility, which worries her long-term in ageing patients whose motility is already falling — she recounts an endoscopy patient whose stomach was still full of dinner the next morning despite an overnight fast. Muscle loss on these drugs can be regained, she argues; a gut trained to move slowly is the scarier legacy.
- Acne: treat it as a skin issue, not a confidence catastrophe — but do treat it, because acne is far easier to fix than scars. Textural scars (box, rolling, pitted) cannot be fixed with creams; options are retinoids for slow turnover, microneedling (including RF microneedling, which is colour-blind and safe on brown skin), CO2/erbium lasers (CO2 is tricky on brown skin), subcision for tethered scars, and injectables like PRP/peptides — realistically a year or more of combined work.
- Salicylic acid works by drying sebum so it can't feed bacteria, but stacking it in a face wash + serum + night cream over-dries the skin, breaks the barrier, kills skin probiotics, and triggers rebound oil production. A doctor's regimen is a balanced "orchestra" — don't drop the calming/repair products and keep only the actives.
- Botox practicalities: results are entirely injector-dependent (wrong placement can drop the brow instead of lifting it); onset is 1–7 days and it is repeated about every 3 months. Masseter Botox treats bruxism/TMJ clenching and slims the lower face — she prefers it over jawline filler in Indians, whose jaw area is often bulky from salivary glands and paan/gum chewing. Oily, thicker skin ages more slowly; Indian skin is further protected by melanin and diet.
- Collagen supplements: "vegetable collagen doesn't really work," in her view — bovine and marine collagen are the effective forms, and they work by supplying amino acids, not by becoming collagen directly. She also reports that patients on heavy whey/animal protein show more acne, hair fall, eczema and back folliculitis in her practice, and improve when switched to lighter/plant protein; Dr Pal notes whey promotes Bacteroides while soy/pea protein promotes Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in lab findings.
- Eczema (adult): the biggest mistakes are over-cleansing and using only the steroid/immunomodulator while skipping moisturiser. Her basics: a quick ~2-minute lukewarm bucket bath with oil mixed in (hot water emulsifies and strips skin oils), generous moisturiser — absorption boosted with a cold wet muslin cloth — plus gut care, a food journal or sensitivity test to find personal triggers, and stress management; medication then becomes SOS-only for flares.
- Fungal infections keep recurring in India because of OTC steroid–antifungal combination creams: the steroid silences the itch and the immune response while the fungus survives. Her rule: apply plain antifungal about 2 inches beyond the lesion, 2–3 times a day, massaging ~2 minutes, for a full 21 days even after the lesion disappears; complete any oral course; and wash clothes and bed linen so you don't reinfect yourself.
- Dr Pal's gut prescription for her: 12 hours of gut rest, sleep 3 hours after your last meal, 2 litres of water a day, and a relaxation technique (start with 2 minutes) before bed. Night-time "hunger" is often thirst — drink water and wait 20 minutes, since the brain needs that long to tell hunger from thirst. Her own routine: dinner by 7:30, phone off after dinner, bed ~10 pm, up at 5:45 without an alarm, skincare done at 7 pm on returning home, room AC at 23°C, cold water (never hot) on the face, and morning oil massage left on for an hour.
- CoolSculpting honesty: it only works on pinchable, stubborn fat pockets in people already near their ideal weight, costs at least a couple of lakhs per area in India with an original machine, and cannot touch visceral/intra-abdominal fat — which only diet and lifestyle reduce. She questions whether it is worth the money versus liposuction.

## Claims to be careful with
- Blue light from gadgets and indoor light as a reason to wear sunscreen indoors is presented as settled; the evidence for visible/blue light harm is strongest for pigmentation-prone skin and remains an active research area.
- The mouse study in which LPS from obese mice allegedly made normal-diet mice obese within four weeks is animal research described loosely by Dr Pal; it is not human evidence.
- The whey protein → Bacteroides → skin inflammation chain is explicitly flagged in the episode itself as a lab finding whose clinical translation is unknown; Dr Rashmi's protein-switch observations are practice anecdotes.
- GLP-1 drugs causing lasting gut-motility harm is her hypothesis — she concedes "we don't know" — not an established outcome.
- She sells and promotes her own supplement line (the RA antioxidant/anti-inflammatory/hydration products she recommends throughout) and discloses advisory work with Unilever, L'Oréal and Estée Lauder brands — a commercial interest in the product advice.
- "Vegetable collagen doesn't work," autophagy timelines, and hookworms as an eczema therapy are stated more confidently than the underlying evidence supports.

## Summary
Dermatologist Dr Rashmi argues that skin problems start in the gut: a high-fat, high-sugar diet feeds LPS-producing gut bacteria whose inflammation shows up as pigmentation, eczema, acne and dehydration, while weight gain quietly drives PCOS, thyroid issues and insulin resistance. Around that core she gives a practical skincare manual — moisturiser first in importance, SPF 30–50 sunscreen in the right quantity and order (nourish, protect, enhance), no self-prescribed acid stacking, plain antifungal for a full 21 days instead of steroid combos, and honest limits on Botox, fillers and CoolSculpting — all anchored to sleep, meal timing and consistency rather than products.
