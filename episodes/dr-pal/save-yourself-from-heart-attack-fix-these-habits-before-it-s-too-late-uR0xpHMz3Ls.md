## Important tidbits
- Chest or upper-stomach (epigastric) discomfort that worsens on exertion — climbing stairs, rushing across a station bridge — and eases at rest is angina until proven otherwise, even when it feels like a stomach problem. Discomfort after a heavy meal on minimal exertion (postprandial angina) can signal extensive triple-vessel or left-main disease.
- The standard workup: ECG, 2D echocardiogram, then a treadmill stress test (watching ECG changes, blood-pressure response and reproduction of symptoms), then coronary angiography if indicated. Angiography is a daycare procedure, usually via the wrist, with the patient home in 3–4 hours; blockages around 50–60% can often be managed with medication and lifestyle rather than a stent. Treadmill tests produce many false positives in Indian women in their 50s, he notes.
- A major heart attack means a 100% blocked artery: "time is muscle," and the golden window to open the artery with primary angioplasty is 60–90 minutes, because dead heart muscle does not come back.
- Know the anatomy: the left main splits into the LAD (the "widow maker," supplying ~50% of the heart) and the circumflex; the right coronary artery supplies much of the heart's electrical system. Bypass surgery is preferred over stents for triple-vessel disease, left-main blocks, heavily calcified/diffuse disease and many diabetics, with the choice guided by SYNTAX scoring; stents can re-block (restenosis) if lifestyle is not corrected.
- South Asians develop coronary disease roughly 10 years earlier than Western populations; Dr Anup has performed primary angioplasty on patients aged 18–20. Family history is a first-degree risk: a parent with heart disease at ~45–50 raises your risk, and both parents affected roughly doubles it.
- Hypertension mechanics: chronic high pressure injures the endothelium (the vessel lining), which stiffens, stops relaxing, and accumulates plaque and clots. Don't label hypertension from a single office reading — use home monitoring or 24-hour ambulatory BP; night-time "non-dipping" and the early-morning BP surge are early danger signs, and one reason pills may be timed at night. Keeping BP under 130/80 reduces end-organ damage.
- His stark India statistic: of 100 hypertensive people in the community, only ~30 are diagnosed, only ~30% of those actually take their medication, and only ~30% of those are adequately controlled — roughly 3 in 100. He estimates controlling BP properly could cut the heart-failure burden by ~30%.
- Sodium target: 1,500–2,000 mg per day (about a teaspoon of total salt), counting hidden sodium in packaged food, chips, pickles, papads and restaurant fried rice — not just table salt. The DASH diet (fresh vegetables, legumes, fruit, nuts) is the recommended pattern. Beware the opposite extreme: families who cut salt to zero have brought elderly patients in with low sodium (hyponatremia), especially on diuretics.
- Heart failure develops when the pressure-overloaded heart muscle thickens (hypertrophy) and eventually gives up. Breathlessness on exertion is an early warning; breathlessness lying flat or waking up gasping at night is *advanced* heart failure. Well-controlled hypertensive patients should still review with tests about every 6 months.
- Diabetes is treated as a coronary-disease risk equivalent: high glucose makes the endothelium inflamed, sticky and pro-clotting, producing diffuse, calcified, multi-vessel disease (hence bypass often beats stents). Because diabetes dulls pain sensation, heart attacks are often "silent" — the only signs may be fatigue, mild breathlessness, reduced exercise capacity or upper-stomach pain. His summary: any patient with diabetes should imagine they already have some heart disease, act on any new discomfort, and get an ECG rather than waiting. Higher A1c means higher, more widespread vascular disease.
- Prevention timing: start paying attention to diet in your 30s and early 40s, maintain ideal weight, and eat mindfully (attention on the food, no phone/TV). In his practice, previously non-compliant patients who adopt diet, activity and weight loss can bring A1c down by ~1.5 points; diabetes caught early at A1c ~7–7.5 can sometimes be normalised with weight loss.
- Cholesterol screening should start at age 20 and repeat every 5 years if normal. The Indian dyslipidemia pattern differs from the West: moderately high LDL, strikingly low HDL (he has seen 15–20) and very high triglycerides — 700–800 is common and 1,200–1,400 seen (blood serum can look milky). A triglyceride/HDL ratio above 4 means increased risk; aim below 2. The first treatment for high triglycerides is cutting the carbohydrate load (he suggests a "reverse banana leaf": vegetables first and in bulk, rice last and less). Exercise is the main lever that raises HDL; flaxseed, walnuts, almonds, avocado and oily fish may help slightly. ApoB (all "bad" particles) is a target he follows; new options include monoclonal antibodies and twice-yearly RNA-based therapy.
- Statins: only ~20–25% of his patients manage to lower cholesterol with lifestyle alone within 3 months. Targets: LDL below 100, ideally below 70 in diabetics, below 130 with family history. High-dose statins can nudge A1c up slightly in some patients, but he stresses the LDL benefit is far larger. His rule: try lifestyle for 3 months if you insist, but the numbers must reach target — by medication if needed.
- Smoking — including passive smoking in enclosed spaces — is the single risk factor he would eliminate in young patients; stress is directly tied to hypertension (cortisol/adrenaline/sympathetic surge), and current Indian guidance is that no level of alcohol is safe.
- Sleep is "the best form of meditation" (quoting the Dalai Lama): the body reboots during sleep, and he advises a minimum of 6–7 hours, with gadgets put away after a set time at night.

## Claims to be careful with
- The 30/30/30 hypertension cascade (~3 of 100 adequately controlled) and the "30% of the heart failure epidemic would go down" figure are the guest's practice-based estimates, presented without a cited source.
- The ~1.5% A1c reduction from lifestyle change is his clinical impression in non-compliant patients; he himself notes guidelines suggest closer to 0.5%.
- Triglycerides as the *primary* lipid target (ahead of LDL) reflects the Lipid Association of India position he endorses; Western guidelines still prioritise LDL.
- "Any patient with diabetes — imagine you already have heart disease" is a deliberate awareness-raising simplification, as both speakers acknowledge.
- Familial hypercholesterolemia and genetic high cholesterol are real but, as he says, account for only a small percentage of high-cholesterol cases; the "cholesterol runs in the family — no one runs in the family" line is a joke, not a dismissal.

## Summary
Interventional cardiologist Dr Anup walks through heart disease end to end: how exertional and post-meal discomfort can be angina in disguise, how ECG, echo, treadmill testing and angiography lead to stents or bypass, and why "time is muscle" in a heart attack. The bulk of the episode is prevention — hypertension as a silent, badly undertreated killer in India (home/ambulatory BP checks, <130/80, ~2,000 mg sodium, DASH diet), diabetes as a heart-disease equivalent with silent attacks, the Indian lipid pattern of very high triglycerides and very low HDL, and an unapologetic defence of statins when lifestyle alone doesn't hit target.
