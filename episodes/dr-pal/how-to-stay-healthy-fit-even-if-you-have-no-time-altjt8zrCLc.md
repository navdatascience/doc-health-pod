# How To Stay Healthy & Fit Even If You Have No Time

- **Guest:** Dr Sheela Nambiar, gynecologist and lifestyle/fitness specialist, author of the "Training for Life" method

## Important tidbits
- Dr Nambiar says the invisible reason diets and exercise fail is autopilot living: following internet/neighbour trends instead of listening to your own body, plus unrecognised stress, poor sleep and fad diets that promise magical, quick results instead of a long-term, personalised pattern.
- Intermittent fasting is not for everyone, she says: 12 hours is fine and recommended for most, but an 8-hour feeding window fails many women — especially if they exercise at 6 a.m. and cannot refuel until 9–10 a.m.; shift the window or the workout instead of forcing it.
- On refuelling, she says the old "protein within an hour" rule no longer holds strictly — protein can be spread through the day — but after a long workout you should eat a proper meal reasonably soon, because depleted glycogen otherwise gets drawn from muscle.
- Fasted workouts are fine if you feel good doing them (she prefers them herself), but she warns much exercise research is male-focused: women who cut energy intake too low lose muscle mass, which is especially harmful for Indian women who already have low muscle mass.
- Sarcopenia: Indians carry less muscle and more fat than Caucasian counterparts of the same size, she says; muscle is an endocrine organ releasing myokines that protect brain, bone and gut — one class is nicknamed the "hope molecule" for raising optimism — and muscle loss begins after age 30 if not actively challenged.
- Walking is good but does not build muscle, she says; only progressive resistance (dumbbells, barbells or bodyweight moves like push-ups and squats) recruits more muscle fibres and grows muscle — possible even at age 90, though earlier is better.
- Track fat, not just weight: use a simple inch tape on waist, hips and thighs every two weeks, weigh at most once a week (readings can vary ~500 g within a day), and set controllable SMART goals — e.g. a 12-minute walk test baseline of 1 km improved to 1.2 km, or a 2 kg shoulder press raised to 3 kg in 3–4 weeks.
- Her "no time" prescription: write out your day hour by hour to expose time-wasters (e.g. scrolling), then use "exercise snacking" — 10 minutes three times a day, or 15-minute circuits (squats, lunges, wall push-ups, plank; ~6–7 exercises, 3 circuits) — which she says research finds as good as one continuous hour; WHO's 150 minutes/week is about 30 minutes, 5 days a week.
- Minimum strength dose: train each muscle at least twice a week (e.g. upper body Monday/Thursday, lower body Tuesday/Friday), splitting a 15-minute slot by body part; a full hour can hold 30 minutes cardio + 20 minutes strength + 10 minutes stretching/yoga.
- Strength training improves glucose control because muscle is the biggest glucose user after the brain, she says; she has seen pre-diabetic and diabetic patients reduce medication after building muscle, and lean PCOS patients are ideal candidates since training improves insulin sensitivity without needing weight loss.
- Cycle syncing: track 1–2 cycles first; many women can push harder in the first ~12–14 days, then ease to yoga, Pilates and longer slower walks around days 17+ if PMS, bloating and cravings appear — but she stresses this is individual and some women train hard all month.
- She links force-feeding children ("finish your plate") to overriding the satiety signal and a lifelong dysfunctional relationship with food; food used as reward and punishment feeds emotional eating and, at extremes, eating disorders.
- On mindset, her train-platform analogy: observe thoughts like trains passing rather than boarding them; 5 minutes of daily meditation/mindfulness, CBT-style questioning of thoughts and small wins can rewire the "I can't do this" belief within a couple of weeks, she says.
- Age-specific advice: at 25 build the foundation (food, sleep, exercise, thought habits) and muscle while you can push hard; at 35 add bone/muscle/mental health and stop people-pleasing by setting boundaries; at 45+ protect bone, muscle and brain, prune social connections to those that matter, and keep learning new things.
- Her single instruction for tomorrow: "Listen to their body" and don't let outside influences control how you live. She credits her own fitness at age 56 to exercise, vegetable diversity, hydration and mindset.

## Claims to be careful with
- The statement that 15-minute "exercise snacking" is as good as a continuous hour, and that afternoon (~2 p.m.) training carries fewer injuries due to circadian insulin/muscle rhythms, are presented as research findings without specific citations in the episode.
- The claim that social support reduces breast cancer recurrence, and that close partners' brain waves and physiology "sync up", are cited loosely from research by Dr Nambiar and should not be read as precise effect sizes.
- Reports of diabetic patients reducing medication through strength training are her clinical anecdotes; medication changes require a treating doctor's supervision.
- The "hope molecule" description of myokines is a popularised label used in the conversation, not a clinical term.

## Summary
Gynecologist and fitness specialist Dr Sheela Nambiar tells Dr Pal how busy people — especially women — can stay fit without an hour a day: personalise fasting instead of copying trends, refuel after workouts, treat muscle as a hormone-producing organ that Indians urgently need to build, measure inches rather than obsessing over the scale, and use exercise snacking (10–15 minute bouts found by auditing your day hour by hour). She adds cycle-aware training, mindful thought habits from her train-platform meditation analogy, social connection, boundary-setting, and age-by-age priorities from 25 to 45+, closing with her core rule: listen to your body.
