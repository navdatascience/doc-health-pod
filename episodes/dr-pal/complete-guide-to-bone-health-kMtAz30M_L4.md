## Important tidbits
- For desk workers he recommends the "30-30-30 rule": every 30 minutes, take a ~30-second break, get up, stretch, and look at something 30 feet away to reduce neck/back strain and eye strain; a 45-minute break cycle is an acceptable fallback.
- An ergonomic chair, he says, needs adjustable seat height so the feet rest flat on the ground (hanging feet compress the back of the thigh and the sciatic nerve), a curved back support reproducing the lumbar (and cervical, if there is a headrest) curve, and an adjustable armrest level with the table so the neck/trapezius muscles can relax.
- About 70–75% of back pain lasting less than ~6 weeks is muscular/postural, he says; but severe back pain that blocks daily activities must be seen by a doctor — a common serious cause is an acute disc bulge compressing the cord/nerve, which can cause radiating leg pain (sciatica), numbness and weakness.
- Belly fat causes back pain two ways, he says: extra weight (e.g. 20 kg extra) makes the spine work harder on every bend, and it exaggerates the lumbar lordosis (normal ~15–20 degrees, rising ~35% more with belly fat) — the same mechanism behind back pain in the last 3–4 months of pregnancy.
- Oestrogen protects bone by inhibiting osteoclasts (bone removal) and supporting osteoblasts (bone building); at menopause its sudden loss accelerates removal, causing perimenopausal/postmenopausal osteoporosis — women are far more prone than men, whose testosterone decline is gradual.
- He advises women 45+ to get a DEXA scan roughly once a year (or once in two years) to catch osteopenia early. On the T-score, he says: above −1 is osteopenia territory needing attention, −1.5 onwards needs definite treatment, and beyond −2.5 is osteoporosis; he says ~90% of Indian women around 45 he has scanned fall between −1.5 and −2.5.
- His bone-health mnemonic is "PVC": Protein, Vitamin D (plus K/K2), Calcium. Bone is largely type-2 collagen, and collagen can only be built with adequate protein — 1 g per kg body weight per day as the baseline, up to 1.5 g/kg for building muscle/bone strength.
- Recommended intakes he cites: calcium ~1,000 mg (1 g) per day, especially post-menopause, and vitamin D ~600–800 IU per day. Vitamin D helps absorb calcium from the gut and prevents its loss in urine. He does not routinely prescribe supplements to asymptomatic 40–45-year-olds without first checking blood calcium/vitamin D levels or a DEXA; he does prescribe after 60 and after fracture surgery.
- Milk is not bad for bones, he says — unless you are lactose intolerant or allergic. Milk and dairy (curd, yoghurt, paneer, cheese) supply both protein and calcium; he calls the "milk is bad" claim a social-media myth.
- For osteopenia/early bone loss his prescription is exercise — 20–30 minutes of active cardio daily plus some strength training; walking is best for the 50–60+ group, ideally around 3:30–4 pm to combine it with sunlight exposure — plus protein-rich food.
- Knee osteoarthritis is cartilage wear-and-tear: the ~2 mm cartilage layer is lost faster than it reforms after ~50–55, graded 1–4 on weight-bearing X-rays; most Indian patients, he says, only present at grade 3–4. In the young (<35–40), reversible cartilage softening (chondromalacia) and inflammatory arthritis are more common, and he says ~90% of early arthritis treatment is home-based: rest, weight reduction, exercise, physiotherapy, hot/ice packs, ointments, knee supports, and only 5 days to a week of medication.
- Weight is the single most important modifiable factor, he stresses: every 1 kg of extra body weight adds ~4 kg of stress to the knee, so 15 kg extra means ~60 kg of extra knee strain. "First advice: weight reduction; second: weight reduction; third: weight reduction — only then exercise."
- On robotic (MAKO) knee/hip replacement, he says it personalises the implant to the patient's own pre-arthritic anatomy (with virtual simulation surgeries before the real one), with ~30–40% less blood loss and pain and walking in 2–3 days; he has done ~3,000 joint replacements over ~18–20 years and ~80–85 robotic cases in ~5.5 months. Doing both knees together is usually preferable if the patient is fit, after anaesthetist/cardiologist risk assessment.
- Myth-busting: steroid injections do not cure arthritis — they give a few months' (6–8 months) relief, max about twice a year. Once knees hurt, exercise should not be avoided, but must be done properly and monitored. Losing weight matters more than gaining muscle for arthritic knees.
- His own routine: exercise ~5 days a week (evenings, mostly kettlebell — combining strength, cardio and flexibility — progressing from 8 kg to 24 kg, always learned under a trainer first because kettlebells are injury-prone), and eating everything in moderation, including 4–5 coffees a day but without sugar.

## Claims to be careful with
- He says hip dislocation after manual hip replacement runs at 5–10% (1 in 10–15) but falls to "1 in 1,000" with robotic surgery — a very strong comparative figure from his own practice experience.
- The DEXA T-score thresholds as spoken are slightly garbled (osteopenia described as "more than −1" and treatment from −1.5); the conventional cut-offs are −1 to −2.5 for osteopenia and ≤−2.5 for osteoporosis — verify against the standard definition.
- His estimate that ~90% of Indian women around 45 score −1.5 to −2.5 on DEXA is his clinic experience, not a population study.
