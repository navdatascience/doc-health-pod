## Important tidbits
- Gayathri's core model: beliefs are repeated thoughts with evidence attached, and they work like the root of a tree — plucking rotten fruit (negative thoughts) daily changes nothing until the root belief changes. All beliefs, she says, are self-fulfilling prophecies.
- The best times to use affirmations are immediately after waking and just before sleep, when the brain is moving between alpha and beta states and is most suggestible; repeat the same affirmations at both ends of the day.
- From birth (even the womb) to about age 7, everything a child observes goes into the subconscious as beliefs. Limiting beliefs are not your fault, but changing them is your responsibility.
- The gut connection: Dr Pal notes that many of his irritable bowel syndrome patients get better with reassurance alone once tests are normal (a placebo effect), and his hospital refers such patients to a GI psychologist whose 6–9 months of work is essentially belief change.
- You recreate your childhood in relationships: Gayathri's parents showed love through fights, so she fought with her husband — whose parents modelled calm love — until she realised her body was "addicted" to the familiar trigger. The work is to process triggers and do inner-child work rather than blame the partner.
- Never fighting is the other unhealthy extreme: she argues suppressed emotions eventually show up in the body, so couples need healthy ways to fight and communicate, plus clear non-negotiables (in her marriage, the "D word" is never used — problems get fixed instead).
- Commitment-phobia is usually fear of being hurt, not fear of commitment. Validation-seeking is like filling a bucket with a hole in the bottom: if you don't love yourself, no amount of outside love stays. Facades attract people to a mask, and the confusion when the mask drops is what breaks relationships.
- On divorce becoming normalised: leaving a relationship without healing yourself means attracting the same pattern with the next partner — she describes clients with two or three divorces repeating identical dynamics. Exit, if at all, only after genuinely giving 100%.
- "Scrolling is the new smoking": short-form video trains the brain to expect a switch every 30 seconds, so a 10-minute video feels like hard work. Her "mental diet" mirrors Dr Pal's food fasting — cap social media at about 30 minutes a day and unfollow every profile that doesn't benefit you.
- Her own 30-day mental diet: the SAVERS morning routine from "The Miracle Morning" (Silence, Affirmations, Visualization, Exercise, Reading, Scribing — 45–60 minutes), 1–2 hours of high-value content, an evening workout, and "night tapes" — her own recorded affirmations looping for ~4 hours while she sleeps. Slipping up meant restarting from day one; withdrawal-like cravings are normal.
- Belief-change timeline: the older claim was 21 days; she cites 66 days without a break to change a subconscious belief. The catch is the other 15 waking hours — her "alarm technique" sets 5–6 alarms a day that replay recorded affirmations so the old thinking doesn't undo the morning hour.
- Social media comparison is a mental-health hazard: every "perfect" picture is edited and colour-corrected, and comparing your behind-the-scenes with someone else's highlights fuels anxiety, depression and body-image issues in teenagers.
- True self-love is not spa days or eating burgers and pizza "because I deserve it" — that is emotional eating to avoid processing feelings. Real self-love is being able to sit with yourself without distraction, accepting yourself while improving; the strongest self-esteem builder is simply doing what you told yourself you would do, balancing kindness with accountability.
- "Value stacking" beats willpower: motivation fades, so link a new habit to your top three life priorities. She made fitness stick by asking how being fit would serve her work/clients, her wealth, and her family — then decisions aligned naturally. It took her 1–2 years of practice to say no without guilt; guilt, she says, is just comparing your actions with others' expectations.

## Claims to be careful with
- The Masaru Emoto water-crystal experiment (kind words make beautiful crystals, hateful words make ugly ones) is cited approvingly, though Gayathri herself notes it is "very debated" — it is not accepted science.
- Claims that "all our cells are constantly listening" to your self-talk and fall sick accordingly, that suppressed emotions manifest as physical disease, that affirmations played during sleep program the subconscious, and the law-of-attraction/law-of-vibration manifestation framework are coaching beliefs, not established medical evidence.
- The "66 days to change a subconscious belief" figure is a popularised claim; habit-formation research shows wide individual variation.
- Dr Pal's suggestion that rising psychiatric illness in the US (bipolar, schizophrenia) is driven by parental divorce and childhood trauma is a broad oversimplification of multifactorial conditions.

## Summary
Mindset coach Gayathri joins Dr Pal to explain why people chase red flags, validation and chaos: childhood-formed subconscious beliefs make the familiar feel like love, so adults recreate their parents' dynamics until they deliberately rewire them. The tools she prescribes are morning and bedtime affirmations, a 30-day "mental diet" with the SAVERS routine and night tapes, daytime affirmation alarms, strict social-media limits, and value stacking to make habits stick — all tied back to Dr Pal's world through the gut-brain axis and the reassurance effect he sees in his IBS patients.
