## Important tidbits
- Consistency, not knowledge, is the biggest challenge: Shiny says clients push hard for an appointment, then vanish from follow-ups within two weeks. Her clinic uses a readiness questionnaire, WhatsApp food-photo monitoring, and age-matched psychologists who use cognitive behavioural therapy — because stress eating is often learned in childhood (one film-industry client was fed by her mother every time she cried, and as an adult binges on biryani, pasta and noodles after every rejection).
- She strongly warns against quick-fix weight loss — losing 10 kg in a month on liquids and protein shakes. The repercussions she lists: gut dysbiosis and leaky gut, severe hair loss (clients have gone bald and needed hair transplants), sagging skin, flares of conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, and regaining more than was lost. Dr Pal adds a case: a patient who lost ~15 kg in a month on meal-replacement shakes arrived with severe micronutrient deficiencies, the worst stomach mucositis he had seen in ~10,000 endoscopies, and a gut microbiome resembling a newborn baby's.
- Focus on fat loss, not weight loss — and stop weighing yourself daily, because day-to-day gram fluctuations can't be explained and only demotivate. Use body composition analysis (BCA) or DEXA (about ₹3,000–3,500) instead; gym BIA machines carry a 3–5% error, so measure in the morning between meals, avoid the week around periods, and never with a pacemaker or implant. The simplest tool is a tailor's tape: waist-to-hip ratio. Dr Pal says men above ~20–22% body fat fall into the overweight/obese category (women have leeway to ~25–26%), while Shiny sees 35–55% body fat in her women clients. Dr Pal's own wake-up call: time-restricted feeding made him lean but cost him muscle — he was "skinny fat" at 35% body fat.
- Per ICMR figures she cites, 73% of Indians do not get adequate protein, and fibre is the other big gap — the target is about 25 g of fibre a day. Bowel movements do not have to be daily; what matters is a change from your own normal frequency or consistency (Bristol stool types 3–4 are normal).
- How to fix upma: replace white rava/suji with broken wheat, quinoa or millet vermicelli, make it about 70% vegetables (the general target is 2–3 cups, roughly 300–400 g of vegetables a day), and add protein — crumbled paneer or tofu, peanuts, or a coconut chutney made with roasted Bengal gram or peanuts.
- Liquid calories are "pretty dangerous": a 250–300 ml glass of fresh juice takes 5–6 oranges you would never eat in one sitting, delivers almost no fibre (about 0.1 g in a typical ABC juice versus 3.5 g if you ate the same produce), and without chewing there is no satiety signal. People with insulin resistance, PCOS or fatty liver should avoid juice and go easy on very sweet fruits (grapes, big bowls of watermelon, jackfruit, mango) — though juice is fine for a highly active sports-playing child. Advice is always individual.
- She cites 136 million Indians living with prediabetes/insulin resistance, and says a government official urged focus on 10–19 year olds: they walk too little, sleep late, are on gadgets constantly, and food is available 24/7 — habits that worsened through the pandemic.
- Coffee maths: an Indian-style milk coffee with four or five sugar packets can be ~300 calories, and many professionals drink it three or four times a day. Studies Dr Pal cites suggest 4–5 cups of black coffee a day can benefit fatty liver — but not the milky, sugary version. Shiny's nutrigenomics test showed she is a slow caffeine metaboliser (and gluten/lactose intolerant): coffee after 5–6 pm gives her insomnia, so she now takes just 30 ml of filter coffee in the morning. Her habit for clients: five soaked almonds and five walnuts before coffee or tea, so it does not hit an empty gastric lining.
- Omega-3: she recommends supplements because few people eat fish regularly. Look for EPA + DHA totalling at least 1,000 mg; typical dosing is around three capsules a day (three to four for athletes recovering from injury), and the bottle must be refrigerated because the oil is fragile. She uses it for food cravings, high triglycerides and inflammation. Vegetarian sources — walnuts (they turn rancid fast), flaxseed, chia/sabja seeds, fenugreek — give much smaller amounts; almonds are the more practical everyday nut.
- Bread: her pick is sourdough, fermented 3–4 days, which breaks down gluten. She says non-celiac gluten sensitivity is common in her practice and shows up as bloating, psoriasis, dermatitis or eczema — and was the hidden reason some clients' fat loss stalled despite perfect calorie counting. Use single-grain breads and a food journal to identify culprits (multigrain hides them), or scoop the bread out, Subway-style, to fit more filling.
- Rice does not make you fat — quantity does. Her plate rule: 1 cup of rice with 2–3 cups of vegetables across two or three varieties, plus a protein portion, and eat more of the curry pieces. Her standout case: a 170 cm client who came in at 142 kg ate idli, dosa, rice and a Sunday tandoori platter on measured portions (with buttermilk, salads and guava/pomegranate snacks) and reached 98 kg in 8 months alongside strength training.
- Worst snacks: anything from a local bakery — loaded with trans fats and refined flour. Roadside grilled meats are risky (dripping fat, high-heat carcinogens); grill at home instead. She warns that delivered food may come from cloud kitchens — frozen long ago, thawed and reheated quickly, and sent in plastic containers — and advises paying a good cook instead; frozen vegetables you microwave at home are a different, acceptable thing.
- On eating disorders: orthorexia (obsessing over eating perfectly) can tip family members into anorexia or bulimia, so her approach is moderation — eat the biryani but load up on the meat pieces, choose tikkas over deep-fried starters, add extra vegetable and protein toppings to pizza. Dr Pal's own trick: order a to-go box with his biryani, split half into it immediately, and eat that half the next day.
- Batch cooking for busy weeks: freeze chopped vegetables in ziplocs or glass boxes, make cutlets and chutneys on the weekend (store chutney in small single-serving boxes to avoid contamination), and boil lentils/beans in bulk for quick sundal or sambar across the week. Dosa batter keeps about a week refrigerated in Indian conditions, longer in the US.
- Celebrity transformations are a team production, not a reel: for actors like Dhanush and Sivakarthikeyan it takes a year to 18 months, a travelling chef, trainers, a medical team, gram-level tracking of chicken and eggs, no sugar, minimal oil, and eating the same food repeatedly — and the camera makes you look about two and a half times bigger, so they must get leaner than seems natural. Do not copy their routines from social media.
- Her two closing basics: finish dinner as early as possible, and hydrate — she has met clients who have survived for years on a single glass of water (200–250 ml) a day. Thirst is routinely misread as hunger, driving headaches and overeating; Dr Pal recommends a minimum of 2–2.5 litres a day, starting on waking and sipping roughly every hour.

## Claims to be careful with
- The meal-replacement-shake case (worst mucositis in ~10,000 endoscopies, "newborn-like" microbiome) is a single-patient anecdote; it does not prove the shakes caused the damage in everyone.
- Omega-3 dosing around 3,000 mg/day and its benefits for cravings and triglycerides are her practice recommendations; high-dose omega-3 is not appropriate for everyone and the episode does not discuss risks or interactions.
- The claim that 4–5 cups of black coffee a day are "good for fatty liver" is Dr Pal's summary of studies, stated without specifics.
- Non-celiac gluten sensitivity as the hidden cause of stalled fat loss and skin conditions reflects her clinical experience; it remains a contested diagnosis.
- The "136 million" prediabetes/insulin resistance figure and the 73% protein-inadequacy figure are quoted from memory of official statements/ICMR data rather than shown on screen.
- Nutrigenomics testing (e.g. for caffeine metabolism and food intolerances) is presented as straightforwardly actionable; the clinical utility of such consumer tests is debated.

## Summary
Celebrity dietitian Shiny Surendran's "diet fix" is unglamorous: fix consistency before food. She and Dr Pal dismantle quick-fix shake diets (with a horror-story case of gut damage), argue for fat loss over weight loss using body-composition numbers, and work through everyday Indian food — protein-and-vegetable-loaded upma, portioned rice, sourdough over regular bread, the hidden calories of juice and milky coffee, refrigerated omega-3s, batch cooking — ending on the two basics people ignore: eat dinner early and drink enough water.
