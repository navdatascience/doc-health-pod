## Important tidbits
- The "sum insured" is a myth, Dr Raj says: a ₹10 lakh policy does not mean the hospital is paid up to ₹10 lakh for your treatment. Insurers negotiate fixed packages per procedure with hospitals — his example: an appendectomy that costs ~₹1–1.5 lakh as a cash case may be signed at only ₹75,000 (and can be ₹30,000–50,000 at smaller hospitals), regardless of your cover.
- Because packages are fixed, hospitals earn LESS from an insurance patient than a cash patient, he says — the opposite of the common belief that hospitals over-bill insured patients. The hospital cannot legally take the difference from the patient on a packaged procedure, even if the patient offers to pay for better care.
- Fixed packages push hospitals toward cost-effective (not state-of-the-art) choices: his example is the harmonic scalpel, a ~₹40,000 single-use instrument for bloodless cutting/sealing, which a package price may not cover — similarly the grade of hernia mesh, stent or cataract lens used is limited by what fits inside the package.
- The insurer pays the same package whether a senior or junior doctor operates, so the most experienced surgeon may not be the one operating on insured cases at smaller centres; GEM says it runs one undifferentiated OT list for cash and insurance patients because its volume lets it absorb the difference.
- Ask before buying, he advises: "Have you negotiated packages with hospitals?" If yes, avoid that insurer if you can — insurers that go with hospital pricing treat insured and cash patients the same. Most, but not all, companies negotiate packages.
- Cashless is never 100% cashless: consumables (gloves, IV cannula/venflon, catheter, drains) and hospital diet are NOT covered under Indian policy rules, so on an open-billing (non-package) case — e.g. a ₹5.5 lakh bill — the patient can still pay ₹50,000–₹1 lakh out of pocket despite a ₹10 lakh policy.
- Complicated cases trigger disputes: for a gangrenous (rotten, pus-filled) appendix, insurers routinely query the claim, demand doctor's letters and audits, and discharge can be delayed for hours; he cites a case approved at ₹1.25 lakh where the insurer finally paid only the ₹75,000 package, leaving the gap with the hospital.
- Pre-existing disease (PED) is the biggest rejection lever: anything directly or indirectly linked to a condition you had before the policy — diabetic foot ulcer/retinopathy from pre-existing diabetes, even a hernia linked to a childhood appendectomy you forgot to declare — can be rejected. His advice: disclose EVERYTHING when buying, or the policy can be cancelled outright.
- Insurers will not sell a policy at all if BMI is over 40, he says, and bariatric surgery claims get rejected as "obesity" even though IRDAI guidelines now cover bariatric surgery — he calls both points legally fightable.
- Only ~25% of Indians have private health insurance; the rest of coverage is government schemes (PMJAY, state Chief Minister's schemes, CGHS, ESI, ECHS). Predominantly only inpatient (IP) care is covered in India — outpatient (OPD) cover is rare/minuscule.
- Government scheme pricing is "absolutely unscientific," he says: PMJAY pays ₹35,000 for a Whipple's procedure (pancreatic cancer surgery) when the harmonic scalpel alone costs ₹40,000, and lists fibroadenoma of the right breast at ₹5,000 but the left breast at ₹6,000. He filed an RTI asking for the costing breakup and only received the same package list back.
- The GIPSA bloc — the four public-sector insurers (United, National, New India, Oriental) — negotiates with hospitals jointly, which he calls an indirect monopoly; when hospitals threatened to de-panel three insurers (via AHPI, ~15,000 hospitals), the insurers "bent down" and settled.
- Direct access to super-specialists makes Indian care feel expensive: for ~₹500 anyone can see a neurologist who must rule out the 1-in-1,000 tumour and order an ₹8,000–10,000 CT, whereas NHS-style systems filter through primary care first. He recommends starting with a primary-care doctor/GP.
- Medical inflation in India runs ~14% a year vs 6–8% general inflation, yet many insurer package rates have not been revised in 10 years, he says — even so, he maintains Indian treatment costs are among the cheapest in the world, cheaper than Sri Lanka, Bangladesh or Pakistan.
- His closing position: insurance is not bad, but if you can self-fund or keep a dedicated healthcare-emergency investment, paying cash avoids the cashless/package/consumable headaches and lets you choose any doctor and hospital with transparent pricing — there is "no third man in between."

## Claims to be careful with
- The claim that insurers refuse all policies above BMI 40, and that bariatric rejections contradict IRDAI guidelines, is Dr Raj's account from his practice — individual insurer underwriting rules vary and should be checked against the actual policy wording.
- His characterisation of government scheme rates (Whipple's ₹35,000; right vs left fibroadenoma ₹5,000/₹6,000) and of GIPSA as a monopoly is presented as his professional opinion as an AHPI insurance coordinator; he explicitly declines to go further on the politics.
- The suggestion that a person who can self-fund may be better off without insurance is his personal view, framed for the Indian cost context — it is not general financial advice and he still says insurance is worthwhile if a major bill would not be affordable.

## Summary
Dr Pal interviews his batchmate Dr Praveen Raj, the surgical gastroenterologist who runs seven GEM hospitals, on how health insurance really works in India: insurers negotiate below-cost fixed packages with hospitals, so a large sum insured does not buy state-of-the-art care, consumables and diet are never covered, complicated cases and pre-existing conditions become rejection battlegrounds, and government scheme rates are set without any costing logic. His practical advice is to disclose everything, ask whether an insurer negotiates hospital packages before buying, start care with a primary-care doctor — and, if you can genuinely afford to self-fund, to consider whether the policy is worth the friction at all.
