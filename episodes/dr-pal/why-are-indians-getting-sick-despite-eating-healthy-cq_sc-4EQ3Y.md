# Why Are Indians Getting Sick Despite Eating Healthy?

## Important tidbits
- Grover says the "same food" is not the same: citing ICAR's own study, he says common Indian foods have lost up to 50% of their nutrition in 50 years — the zinc a roti provides fell from 56% of the daily RDA to about 25%, with iron on the same downward trend.
- He defines "hidden hunger" as the body being full while the cells starve: meals satisfy satiety but fall short on protein, zinc, iron, micronutrients and fiber — invoking the protein leverage hypothesis, he says the body keeps craving food until its nutrient need is met, which is why obese people can be malnourished.
- Three drivers of the nutrient decline, he says: modern high-yield crop varieties bred for productivity rather than micronutrients; degraded soil; and ultra-processing that strips fiber and micronutrients leaving hyper-palatable empty calories.
- On soil: he says India's soil organic carbon is about 0.5% against an ideal of 5%, one-third of soils are degraded, and a crop grown in nutrient-deficient soil can carry up to 80% fewer nutrients than the same crop in healthy soil — "you are not what you eat; you are what your food eats," so consumers should ask where and how food (and livestock feed) was produced.
- On adulteration and labels: he says the soya chaap eaten across North India contains no soya — it is made from maida batter — and claims 2.3 crore litres of milk made from detergents were found in Maharashtra the previous month; until willful adulteration is tackled by law, he says, labels claiming "2x/3x nutrition" cannot be 100% trusted because testing is minimal.
- He contrasts front-of-pack nutrition ratings in Europe and Singapore with India's proposed Indian Nutrition Rating, which he says still does not account for micronutrients.
- Today's environment compounds the problem: an "obesogenic environment" of food-delivery algorithms, grazing all day, skipped regular meals, 2 am snacking and chronic digital stress (raised cortisol) — and the metabolic "bill" for youthful excess typically arrives around age 40 and is often not reversible.
- Biofortification differs from fortification: fortification adds a premix at the factory (recurring cost, shelf-life problems — he says India's fortified rice in the public distribution system was discontinued because fortified rice disintegrated in 3-year buffer stocks), while biofortification breeds the nutrient into the seed through conventional breeding — explicitly not GMO — as a one-time cost that stays in the food system for years.
- HarvestPlus focuses on staples and the big three deficiencies — iron, zinc, vitamin A: in India, zinc-rich wheat, zinc-rich rice, iron-rich pearl millet (bajra) and calcium-rich finger millet (ragi); in Africa, vitamin A maize and cassava and iron beans. Biofortified products carry a biofortification mark so consumers can identify them, and he says two biofortified ragi rotis can provide more calcium than a glass of milk, making ragi a strong option for people avoiding dairy.
- The gender nutrition gap: women need almost double the iron men need, and nearly triple in pregnancy, while crop iron keeps falling — he links this to nearly 60% of Indian women being anemic, and says iron pearl millet and iron-rich beans can help close it.
- Scale and price: he says about 623 million people worldwide already have access to biofortified foods, and biofortified seed costs the same as regular seed with no change in farming practice, so the food should cost the same — any premium charged is for traceability, not the biofortification itself.
- On where the trajectory leads, he cites sperm counts falling from 101 million to 49 million (approaching the ~40 million clinical infertility zone) and testosterone declining about 1% per year over five decades, alongside gut damage from high-protein diets (he gives 150 g protein as an example) pursued without fiber or micronutrients.
- Slow chewing is his "natural Ozempic": chewing properly (he suggests ~20 chews; Dr Pal says 32) and making a meal last about 20 minutes gives leptin and PYY time to signal fullness via the GLP-1 pathway, whereas a 5-minute lunch eaten while scrolling tricks the brain into hunger again within minutes; he says slowing his own eating reduced his bloating, acidity and heartburn.
- "Sonic seasoning": citing Oxford research by Dr Charles Spence, he says sound changes taste — high-pitched sounds (temple bells, wind chimes) make food taste sweeter, low-pitched bass makes it bitter, and airline cabin white noise makes food taste bland; calm music at meals can also slow children's chewing, and temple food tastes better partly because bells plus a rest-and-digest state aid enjoyment and digestion.
- Cooking unlocks plant nutrition: ferment foods (breaks down anti-nutrient phytates and provides probiotics), soak and sprout (sprouting even creates new vitamins), avoid over-polishing rice and over-refining flour, and rotate dals, millets and grains daily instead of eating one favourite food — variety feeds microbiome diversity.
- Through its Nutri Pathshala programme, HarvestPlus teaches nutrition in gamified, age-appropriate modules to primary-school children — he cites 12 crore primary-school kids in India — using Gen Z "nutri buddy" volunteers and a newspaper, arguing nutrition education currently reaches only adults on Instagram, "like teaching someone driving after the accident."

## Claims to be careful with
- The headline figures — up to 50% nutrient loss in common foods, up to 80% fewer nutrients from deficient soil, and 2.3 crore litres of detergent-based milk found in Maharashtra — are stated by the guest (a biofortification advocate) without the underlying studies or reports being shown; verify independently before quoting.
- The claim that two biofortified ragi rotis contain more calcium than a glass of milk, and the ~623 million people reached figure, come from his own organization.
- The sperm-count (101M → 49M) and testosterone-decline figures are cited loosely in conversation and should be checked against primary sources.
- Sonic seasoning is presented as settled Oxford-backed science; effect sizes and real-world impact are not quantified in the episode.

## Summary
Ravinder Grover of HarvestPlus argues Indians are getting sick on seemingly healthy diets because the food itself has changed: citing ICAR data, he says staples have lost up to half their micronutrients in 50 years through high-yield breeding, degraded soil and ultra-processing, producing "hidden hunger" in people who eat enough calories. He explains how conventional-breeding biofortification (zinc wheat and rice, iron pearl millet, calcium ragi) can restore nutrients at no extra cost, and adds practical levers — slow chewing as a "natural Ozempic," sonic seasoning, fermentation, sprouting and daily food rotation — plus his Nutri Pathshala push to teach nutrition to schoolchildren.
