## Important tidbits
- Sleep is the episode's central prescription: Dr Prabash says migraine patients in particular need about 9 hours. Sleep runs in roughly 90-minute cycles of deep (non-REM) and light (REM) sleep, and getting the full 6 cycles takes about 8½–9 hours; fixed bed and wake times also reduce migraine, since disturbed sleep is one of its most important triggers.
- During sleep the brain clears its toxins through a lymphatic-like system (the glymphatic system), and sleep also handles memory consolidation and energy restoration. Chronic short sleep lets proteins misfold and beta-amyloid accumulate, raising the risk of Alzheimer's and other dementias later in life — younger people can partly catch up, but after about 40–45 it becomes much harder.
- Weekend catch-up sleep is mostly a bad idea: it disturbs the circadian rhythm and the timed secretion of hormones (he compares it to shift-work disorder). It is acceptable only for people who genuinely cannot sleep enough on weekdays.
- Screen light is the saboteur: blue light from phones is picked up by the retina like sunlight, signals the pineal gland that it is still daytime, and stops melatonin secretion — so binge-watching reels at night directly steals sleep.
- His patients' average sleep is only about 5–6 hours. Chronic sleep loss plus stress feeds blood pressure, glucose problems, obesity and sleep apnea — he calls the cluster a "mother of all diseases" — and he worries most about IT staff, creative-industry workers and doctors who work late nights.
- Dementia is far more than memory loss: early signs include executive dysfunction (his example: a homemaker who made perfect sambar for years progressively losing the taste, then the ability to make it at all, or no longer being able to drape a sari), calculation difficulty, getting lost in familiar places, failing to recognise faces, forgetting names, and outright personality change. It usually appears above 65 but is now seen at 45–50.
- Several dementias are treatable, so early evaluation matters: vitamin B12 deficiency (he sees many vegetarians present with dementia from it), hypothyroidism, "pseudo-depression" that mimics dementia, normal pressure hydrocephalus, and autoimmune dementia. Starting early can at least slow progression.
- Anyone can screen at home with the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination): a 30-point set of simple questions — date, place, state, remembering three items after a delay. He warns that intelligent people can mask dementia in conversation yet score only 16–17 out of 30.
- Brain exercise builds reserve: reading, Sudoku and calculations grow your "fund of information," which slows dementia — his teacher's analogy: a man with ₹100 can lend ₹10; a man with ₹10 cannot. His own routine is 45 minutes of running daily, about 20 minutes of swimming, and 3–4 hours of cycling on weekends; exercise creates new connections in the brain.
- Headache rules: up to about 2 headaches a month is not a concern, but more than 3 per month needs a consultation — and so does even one disabling headache. A first-ever severe headache after age 50, especially with high blood pressure, is a red flag. Tension headache is usually bilateral and comes in the evening under deadline stress; migraine is usually one-sided, and the treatments differ.
- On everyday remedies: caffeine does help a headache, but only before about 4:30 PM — caffeine's half-life is 6–8 hours, so a later coffee disturbs sleep and can trigger the next headache. Amrutanjan-type balms have no scientific evidence. And on mobile phones and brain tumours: research so far says no increased risk, though he still advises keeping the phone away at night because of screen time.
- Vertigo versus fainting can be separated by history alone: if during the episode you could hear and try to communicate, it is dizziness/vertigo; if you could not hear what was happening, it is fainting. Come at the first vertigo attack — it can be a stroke symptom that can be reversed if caught early, and vestibular neuritis treated with a dexamethasone injection in the first day or two can be cured completely, whereas after about three weeks recovery is poorer and it can become a lifelong problem.
- Seizure first aid: get the person onto a flat, safe space; turn them onto their LEFT side so saliva drains out of the mouth instead of into the lungs; loosen tight clothing; put something soft under the head; do not restrain them. Do NOT put keys or metal in the hand (a superstition — seizures settle on their own in 2–3 minutes, which is why the myth seems to work) and give absolutely nothing by mouth, not even water — he has seen a patient die because a bystander gave water during a seizure. A seizure lasting more than 3–5 minutes is a medical emergency: rush to hospital.
- Everyday forgetfulness is normal — walking into a room and forgetting why is a registration/multitasking failure, not dementia; only persistent, progressive problems need evaluation. For Gen Z, his diagnosis is an inability to tolerate boredom: if you cannot stop yourself checking your phone at a traffic signal or in a queue, "you have a problem" — the brain must be trained to handle stillness, and walks in nature help.
- Diet for the brain: the Mediterranean diet — fibre-rich, everything in moderation. His own day: ABC juice (apple, beetroot, carrot) with two dosas and a fruit in the morning, cucumber around 11, a small amount of rice with lots of vegetables at lunch, almonds and nuts in the evening, a small dinner finished by about 8 PM, and the next day's first bite at 8 AM — a 12-hour overnight fast.
- His closing three-part formula for brain health: 9 hours of sleep at consistent times; 40–50 minutes of brisk walking daily (interval training and weight training are even better); and read a lot — the larger your fund of information, the lower your dementia risk. He adds that choosing what you read matters too, since negative news does affect the brain.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Prabash states that when sleep is disturbed "even for few days, there can be some effects which cannot be reversed" — a strong claim presented without a cited study; the amyloid–sleep-loss link is an active research area, not a proven one-to-one cause.
- The 9-hour figure is his recommendation framed around migraine patients and complete sleep cycles; individual sleep needs vary, and he presents cycle timings (90 minutes, then 60) as averages.
- The UCSF fecal-transplant research for dementia that Dr Pal describes — including MRI areas that "glow" — is preliminary, pre-clinical-stage work by his account, not a treatment.
- The link between early screen exposure and an uptick in autism, including "virtual autism" that reverses when screens are removed, is something Dr Prabash says he learned recently at a meeting; he explicitly notes he is an adult neurologist, not a paediatric one.
- Two recently approved dementia drugs "coming to India" are mentioned without names or details.

## Summary
Neurologist Dr Prabash Prabhakaran makes sleep the master key to brain health: 9 hours in full cycles lets the brain clear toxins, while chronic 5–6 hour nights, late screens and weekend catch-up sleep raise the long-term risk of dementia — a condition he shows is much more than forgetfulness, and sometimes treatable if caught early (B12 deficiency, thyroid, NPH). Along the way he gives crisp rules for headaches (more than 3 a month, see a doctor; no coffee after 4:30 PM), first-attack vertigo, and seizure first aid — turn the person on their left side, and never give water or keys in the hand.
