## Important tidbits
- Madan Gowri says he sleeps only about 6 hours and usually goes to bed around 3:00 a.m.; Dr Pal explains sleep repair happens in cycles of ~90 minutes, and you need 4–5 cycles (roughly 6–7.5 hours) — feeling groggy on waking is a sign you are not in the small minority who thrive on less.
- Peak melatonin secretion is between 1:00 and 2:00 a.m., so you should already be in bed before that window, per Dr Pal.
- Fix a broken sleep schedule gradually — shift bedtime by only 10–15 minutes at a time; circadian rhythm realignment takes about 2–3 months, but it is malleable and changeable.
- Morning sunlight anchors the circadian rhythm: Madan's first act on waking is going to the balcony for bright sunlight. Dr Pal notes digestive hormones peak around 1 p.m. and wind down after sunset (~6 p.m.), so eating while the sun is up fits the body's rhythm — and a broken rhythm is why people crave things like 2 a.m. biryani.
- Time-restricted eating: even if you cannot control portion size or how healthy the food is, at least control the time window in which you eat.
- Stop eating when you feel about 80% full to avoid the heavy post-meal slump.
- Madan once went 8 days on water alone while making videos, but never posted the vlog because, as he says, it is not medically advisable. Dr Pal notes fasting traditions exist across religions (Ramadan, Lent, Hindu fasts) — "divided by religions, united by fasting."
- One in four Chennai software engineers has fatty liver, according to a study Dr Pal cites, and most will not know it because there are no symptoms; measuring your belly with an inch tape is a simple way to check risk.
- Fatty liver can progress until the liver shuts down and needs a transplant; Dr Pal says in the US, non-alcoholic fatty liver now accounts for about as many liver transplants as alcohol-related liver disease.
- Chronic stress secretes cortisol, which mobilises energy to escape a threat — but at a desk that energy is never spent, and stress eating adds more calories that get stored as fat.
- Dr Pal describes Indians as "skinny fat": fewer fat-storage cells, so excess fat deposits in organs and heart vessels — which is how a skinny 23-year-old Tesla employee he mentions ended up in the ICU, and how Madan's 29-year-old IT colleague died of a heart attack the day after Madan saw him.
- Madan says his mental health has improved "100 times" over his 6–7 years of daily content creation; he ignores blind hatred but takes fact-check corrections seriously and fixes mistakes.
- Dr Pal limits himself to about 20 patients a day, spending 15–20 minutes with each, and notes India's doctor–patient ratio has improved to roughly 1:836 — patients in between "knowing nothing" and "knowing everything" (from Google) take the most time to convince.
- Dr Pal observes that Indians abroad often can't open up to insurance-covered therapists in the US who don't understand the Indian family and emotional context, whereas Indian patients typically arrive with 10–15 family members involved in their care.

## Claims to be careful with
- The "one in four Chennai software engineers has fatty liver" figure is a study cited conversationally by Dr Pal; it was not independently verified in the episode.
- The 8-day water-only fast is the guest's personal experiment, explicitly described as not medically advisable — not a recommendation.
- Conversational claims such as "nine of the top ten successful entrepreneur companies are Indian-led" and "more Indians own houses in London than whites" are unverified assertions made in passing.
- The fixed 1–2 a.m. melatonin peak for everyone is a simplification; individual circadian timing varies.

## Summary
Dr Pal talks with Tamil YouTuber Madan Gowri about the hidden health costs of the creator/Gen Z lifestyle: sleeping at 3 a.m. on ~6 hours of screen-heavy sleep, stress, and metabolic risk. The conversation covers circadian rhythm repair (gradual shifts, morning sunlight, time-restricted eating), why symptomless fatty liver is hitting young IT workers, how cortisol and the Indian "skinny fat" pattern drive heart attacks in the 20s, and the erosion of doctor–patient trust. Madan also reflects on how his mental health improved over his career and why he takes fact-checking seriously while ignoring hate.
