## Important tidbits
- Type 2 diabetes is mainly a disease of insulin resistance, not insulin shortage: obesity stops the available insulin from working, the pancreas compensates by secreting more, and rising insulin and weight form a vicious cycle. Insulin deficiency typically appears only late, after years of tablets have exhausted the pancreas.
- Pre-diabetes can be diagnosed from any single abnormal test: fasting glucose 100–125 mg/dL, 2-hour glucose tolerance value 140–199 mg/dL, or HbA1c 5.7–6.4% — even if the other tests are normal. A 2-hour value of 200 or more is diabetes. A fasting test needs at least 8 hours of fasting (water is allowed); eating at 3 am and testing at 7 am doesn't count.
- Type 2 diabetes is often completely asymptomatic — Dr Kumar sees people discover glucose levels of 450–550 on a casual check prompted by a diabetic spouse. Type 1 usually presents below age 20, often unmasked by infection or surgical stress, with weight loss, excessive urination, or life-threatening diabetic ketoacidosis.
- An unusual early warning sign in men: balanitis — infection of the foreskin with itching, discharge, or burning — is now so closely associated that Dr Kumar says he almost concludes diabetes when he sees it in South India.
- Who should screen, and how often: everyone above 35; earlier and more often with diabetic parents/siblings, hypertension, obesity, or diabetes-causing drugs like steroids. If normal, retest every 3–5 years (do fasting, post-glucose, and HbA1c together so no criterion is missed); if pre-diabetic, retest every year. Women who had gestational diabetes should repeat a glucose tolerance test at least every 3 years for life, because risk keeps rising with age. Genetics loads the gun, but children's lifestyle can postpone or prevent the disease.
- Common glucometer mistake: checking with unwashed hands after handling food, which falsely raises the reading. Home checking isn't needed for stable pre-diabetics — a lab check after about 3 months is enough; frequency rises only when sugars are unstable or insulin doses are being adjusted.
- Continuous glucose monitors are not for healthy people: a glucose spike after eating idli is normal physiology that healthy insulin handles. CGM is reserved for unstable diabetes where the pattern — such as night-time lows — is unclear.
- When insulin is considered: HbA1c around 10% or above, glucose persistently above ~300 mg/dL despite tablets, or weight loss from uncontrolled diabetes. But injecting more insulin into an obese patient without addressing weight and diet just meets more resistance plus side effects; Dr Kumar estimates only about 5–10% of his patients truly internalise that disease control depends on what goes in the mouth.
- The commonest dietary error is carb volume — eating until the stomach feels full, in large single loads, instead of divided portions of complex carbs. Dr Pal cites South Indian diets averaging 250–300 g of carbs a day against a tolerance of roughly 150–160 g for insulin-resistant people (from the MASALA study); Dr Kumar advises around 150–200 g of complex carbs a day, e.g., replacing about half the idli rice with millets (thinai/foxtail, ragi, kambu), whose low GI avoids insulin surges.
- Exercise prescription: both, not either/or — roughly 30–45 minutes of aerobic exercise a day plus resistance/strength training, which independently improves glycemic control.
- Long fasts are risky in diabetes: 24-hour fasts "won't work" for diabetics because insulin and insulin-stimulating tablets can drive dangerous lows. Metformin and SGLT2 inhibitors act through insulin-independent mechanisms and combine more safely with time-restricted eating plans.
- Annual complication screening is essential: a retina examination by an ophthalmologist (not just an optometrist's glasses test), because early diabetic retinopathy has no symptoms — Dr Pal cites film legend Thyagaraja Bhagavathar, who died blind, likely from retinal damage. Feet should be checked yearly with monofilament and tuning-fork vibration tests for neuropathy, and heart attacks in diabetics can be "silent" — presenting only as breathlessness, sweating, or a strange arm feeling, because damaged nerves don't carry chest pain.
- Reversibility: early kidney involvement (microalbuminuria) can reverse with tight sugar and blood-pressure control; heavy protein leakage (macroalbuminuria) cannot. Retinopathy can only be slowed or stopped from progressing, while early neuropathy may reverse.
- Stress measurably worsens diabetes: cortisol, glucagon, and adrenaline rise, increasing insulin resistance and releasing stored glucose from the liver; Dr Kumar sees patients needing more insulin units during family crises, worsened by stress eating. Sleep of at least 7 hours (ideally 8) lowers these hormones and improves control.
- Brown sugar versus white sugar: "I don't think there is any difference — we all [get] glucose at the end of the day," says Dr Kumar. He also avoids recommending artificial sweeteners because long-term safety reports keep flip-flopping; Dr Pal notes evidence they disturb gut bacteria and suggests using them only as a 1–2 month bridge while cutting sugar.
- Don't mix systems of medicine: Dr Kumar tells patients to choose allopathy or Ayurveda/Siddha/homeopathy and stick to one, because of interaction risks — he declines to endorse remedies like hibiscus tea, fenugreek, or the viral "insulin leaf." Gene therapy for diabetes is still at the research stage, focused mainly on type 1.

## Claims to be careful with
- The attribution of Thyagaraja Bhagavathar's blindness to diabetic retinopathy is Dr Pal's stated belief, not a documented diagnosis.
- The MASALA study figures (250–300 g average carb intake; ~150–160 g tolerance for insulin-resistant people) are quoted from memory in conversation rather than from the paper itself.
- Dr Pal's position that even one sip of alcohol damages the liver and gut bacteria is stricter than Dr Kumar's, who allows that moderate social drinking within limits can be acceptable — though both agree avoiding alcohol is best.

## Summary
Endocrinologist Dr Kumar of Kauvery Hospital, Chennai, gives a complete tour of diabetes for urban Indians: how insulin resistance (not insulin shortage) drives type 2, the exact fasting/2-hour/HbA1c cut-offs that define pre-diabetes from any one test, screening intervals by risk group, why CGMs and 24-hour fasts are wrong for most people, when insulin is genuinely needed, the carb-volume mistake in South Indian diets and the millet fix, and the annual eye, foot, kidney, and heart checks that prevent blindness, amputation, dialysis, and silent heart attacks.
