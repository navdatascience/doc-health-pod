## Important tidbits
- Dr Khadar Valli's central line: "When food is right, no medicine is needed. When your food is wrong, no medicine works." He says doctors and nutritionists need to unlearn and relearn disease prevention through food choice.
- He classifies food by fibre type, which he attributes to ancestral knowledge: greens and vegetables carry "nascent" fibre, roots and tubers carry "immature" fibre, and true grains — millets — carry lignans, an aromatic "regulatory" fibre. Dr Pal cites daily fibre targets of 25 g for women and 38 g for men.
- The key distinction he draws: in millets the fibre is physically associated with the grain's carbohydrate, protein and fat, so digestion must deal with the fibre first and nutrient release is regulated. Added fibre (e.g. isabgol/psyllium husk sprinkled on rice) does not regulate release, he says. White rice has about 0.2 g of fibre with no regulatory capacity.
- His glucose example: eating 100 g of rice pumps almost 35 g of glucose into the blood within about 20 minutes, while whole-body blood glucose homeostasis is about 5 g — forcing the endocrine system to convert the excess into cholesterol, triglycerides and other products. He says BP and diabetes "are the result of the food that you have chosen."
- He says millets are C4 grasses that make glucose via C4 oxaloacetic acid and can grow largely on atmospheric moisture, whereas rice and wheat are water-dependent C3 grasses — which is why millet grains have fibre incorporated. Major millets he names: pearl millet, finger millet (ragi) and great millet (jowar); he counts desi corn/maize as a C4 millet too, excluding genetically modified sweet corn.
- Fibre figures he gives per 100 g: finger millet (ragi) 3.6 g, foxtail millet 8 g, kodo millet 9%, and barnyard/brown top millet 12.5% — the last two, he says, make especially efficient ambli for the gut. He says there were 240 millet types historically and more than 200 have been lost; foxtail alone once had 108 varieties.
- His core prescription is ambli — fermented millet porridge (kambu koozh in Tamil Nadu, basi in North India). Protocol as stated: soak 1 g of millet in 300 ml water for 8 hours, boil 10 minutes, and ferment in a mud pot tied with khaddar (muslin/cotton) cloth. The mud pot's micro-pores allow aerobic fermentation without pressure build-up; he says 26 °C is the best fermentation temperature (the reason basi was traditionally buried 2 feet deep in bamboo bottles).
- How to take ambli: drink it first, on an empty stomach, about 5 minutes before eating — never after food — so it reaches the colon region first. Once a day if healthy, three times a day if sick, he says.
- On probiotic supplements, asked directly, he says: "Absolutely not needed. It is only business." He claims millet-based fermentation produces far more diverse aerobic microbes than buttermilk's lactobacillus/bifidobacteria — "18,000 times more" for one millet, and probiotic quality from mature millet fibre "1 lakh times better" than from fermented greens — against a gut population he puts at 100 trillion microbes.
- On gluten, he says all humans struggle to deal with gluten and that sensitivity is a consequence of lost gut microbes: "If your gut microbe is good, you will have no celiac problems." He goes further: "all diseases are celiac only," and says mental illnesses can also be reversed if gut microbes are good, via the gut–brain axis.
- He argues rice and wheat dominance is a 60–70 year corporate story: World War II ammonium nitrate bomb material converted to fertilizer, dam-building and monoculture (now about 800 million tons each of rice and wheat grown globally), with millets stigmatized as "primitive food, coarse grains, bird feed."
- On protein, he says all amino acids originate in plants — legumes (Fabaceae) fix nitrogen via root microbes — and animals only rearrange plant amino acids. Millets plus lentils are a "perfect match," and millets, he says, contain all amino acids.
- On vitamin B12, he says no animal makes B12: cobalt sits at the centre of the corrin ring (as iron sits in heme), cobalt comes from soil, and pearl, brown top and foxtail millets absorb it — fish, in his account, get cobalt/B12 ultimately from plants via water. Eating millets, he says, covers the cobalt need directly.
- He warns that commercial millet pizza, noodles and biscuits usually mix in wheat (sometimes only ~20% millet) for stickiness and texture. His alternative binder is tuber stickiness — sweet potato, elephant yam, cassava/tapioca — and he says wheat-free millet biscuits, rotis and pooris can be made this way; recipes are in his free book "Pakasiri" (no patent or copyright, downloadable via QR code).
- For patients who bloat on millet fibre, his fix is to start with ambli liquid only: within 3 days to 3 weeks, he says, they can handle regular millet food. He gives tissue renewal times as context: gut lining about 3 days, liver and bones about 6 weeks, hemoglobin about 3 months, neurons and nephrons about 7 years.
- On antioxidants, he says the peanut (groundnut) skin has eight times more resveratrol than red wine and groundnut oil is "10 times better" than red wine, while millet lignans — built from many aromatic rings that absorb and stabilize free radicals — are, he says, 100 to 1,000 times more capable than resveratrol/polyphenols. He defines a complete meal as greens and vegetables + roots and tubers + regulatory-fibre grains.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Khadar Valli claims diabetes can be reversed in 6 weeks and dialysis reversed in 1 year on a millet/ambli regimen — extraordinary claims presented without clinical trial evidence in the episode.
- His case of a 190 kg person drinking ambli and reaching/losing "81 kilos" within 3 months is an anecdote with ambiguous wording and no documentation offered.
- He states rising type 1 diabetes is "not at all genetic" and is caused by food over 7–8 generations — this contradicts the established autoimmune/genetic understanding of type 1 diabetes.
- The claim that "all diseases are celiac only" and that mental illness can be reversed by restoring gut microbes is a sweeping personal theory, not established medicine.
- The quantitative microbe claims (probiotics "1 lakh times better," one millet giving "18,000 times" the microbes of buttermilk, supplying "billion billion" microbes per millet) are unverified figures.
- His B12 argument — that eating millets directly supplies adequate vitamin B12 via cobalt absorption — is not a recognized substitute for B12 assessment/supplementation, especially on fully plant-based diets.
- The fertilizer/bomb-material history of the Green Revolution is his framing of a more complex agricultural history.

## Summary
Padma Shri millet scientist Dr Khadar Valli tells Dr Pal that modern rice-and-wheat diets destroyed the gut microbiome by removing "regulatory" fibre — the lignan fibre built into millet grains that controls glucose and nutrient release and feeds diverse gut microbes. He prescribes daily fermented millet porridge (ambli) made in a mud pot, dismisses probiotic supplements as business, argues gluten problems, diabetes and even mental illness trace back to lost gut microbes, and explains his plant-protein and B12 positions, millet varieties and fibre contents, and how to make genuinely wheat-free millet foods using tubers.
