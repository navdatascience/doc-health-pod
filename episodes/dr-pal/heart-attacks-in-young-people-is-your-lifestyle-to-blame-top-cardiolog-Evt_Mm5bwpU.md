## Important tidbits
- Heart attacks in the young: risk factors can be present from about age 18–20. One mechanism in aggressive gym-goers and marathoners is a small dissection (tear) in the innermost lining of a heart artery; platelets plug the tear and a sudden clot causes the attack. This affects only a small, often genetically predisposed minority — the message is not "don't exercise," but train progressively, hydrate, and stop if you feel discomfort. At the Mumbai marathon, roughly 5–10 runners fall sick each year and 1–2 die, per Dr Anup.
- The single most important preventable factor in young heart attacks is smoking: more than 90% of the under-35 patients Dr Anup performs primary angioplasty on are smokers. "Smoking kills" — and he notes patients often wrongly believe smoking only causes cancer, not heart attacks.
- A rarer cause in young patients: B12 deficiency (common in vegetarians/vegans) raises homocysteine, which promotes aggressive clotting (hyperhomocysteinemia) — worth supplementing B12.
- Endothelial dysfunction is the root process, and erectile dysfunction is its visible early warning: "ED precedes endothelial dysfunction" is reversed in the saying he quotes — erectile dysfunction signals the same damaged vessel lining that later affects the heart.
- South Asians get coronary disease about 10 years earlier than Western populations. From the MASALA study (Metabolic syndrome in South Asians Living in America): South Asian babies have higher body fat at the same birth weight ("thin-fat Indian baby" / TOFI — thin outside, fat inside), and the South Asian abdominal compartment is smaller, so fat spills sooner into visceral fat — the seat of insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. Diabetes risk is about 20% higher than Western counterparts on the same diet.
- Dr Pal recounts his 36-year-old neighbor collapsing with chest pain in a grocery store: no pulse, bystander CPR plus the store's AED restored the pulse, and door-to-balloon time was 40 minutes for a 100% blocked left artery — he walked out of hospital in about 3 days. The lesson: CPR and defibrillation, not the stent, are what save the patient.
- Everyone should learn CPR, and defibrillators should be on every floor of malls, gyms, housing societies, and railway stations — Dr Anup suggests donating an AED to a local mall as charity. The brain tolerates only about 5 minutes without blood flow (versus the heart's 60–90 minute "golden hour"), so CPR's real job is keeping the brain perfused; many patients whose hearts are saved still lose brain function when CPR is delayed.
- On "emergency heart attack kits" (aspirin/statin/sorbitrate kept at home): Dr Anup is not a fan — sorbitrate can worsen a patient whose blood pressure is low. In an ER/ambulance, aspirin and clopidogrel are loaded on suspicion of heart attack, but self-medicating at home is not a general recommendation.
- Night shifts, chronic sleep loss, and stress are major drivers: sleep deprivation is "an epidemic," worsened by phones and OTT streaming in bed; it drives inflammation and metabolic problems. Dr Anup's own prevention ranking: diet first (mindful eating; roughly 80% of weight outcomes are diet), then physical activity, then sleep (aim 6–7 hours), then stress relaxation (breathing, meditation, yoga).
- Master health checkups: basics (lipids, sugars, CBC, thyroid) at least once a year; ECG, 2D echo, and treadmill stress test periodically — after 40 for men and 45 for women, every ~2 years, more often with family history. But stress tests are screening tools with false positives and negatives: a normal treadmill last week does not rule out a heart attack next week.
- CT coronary calcium score: zero is reassuring; a high score (400+) indicates plaque burden and may warrant angiography, but calcium can sit in the outer vessel wall without narrowing the lumen — a high score does not automatically mean bypass or angioplasty. "We treat the patient, not the treadmill."
- Belly fat matters more than weight: 70–80% or more of Dr Anup's heart attack patients have abdominal obesity, often at normal total weight — judge risk by waist circumference and waist-hip ratio, not the scale. The typical pattern: moderately-high LDL, very low HDL, very high triglycerides.
- After a stent: never miss blood thinners, especially in the first 6–9 months, and take statins religiously. In-stent clot risk is about 1–2%, and in-stent restenosis roughly 10% over 10 years even with the best drug-eluting stents — higher if medications and lifestyle lapse.
- Doctors themselves are a high-risk group: a small survey he cites found healthcare professionals develop cardiac problems 5–10 years earlier and have shorter lifespans; he advises young doctors to treat their career as a marathon, not a sprint. His own routine: blood tests every 6 months, occasional treadmill test, never skips breakfast, phone kept aside on off days, family time protected.

## Claims to be careful with
- The figures quoted — over 90% of young angioplasty patients being smokers, 5–10 marathon casualties a year in Mumbai, 70–80%+ of heart attack patients having belly fat — are the guest's clinical impressions, not published statistics.
- The claim that doctors die 5–10 years earlier than the general population comes from a small survey the guest himself cautions "may not hold true" generally.
- The dissection-from-intense-exercise mechanism applies to a "minuscule" minority of young heart attacks, as the guest himself stresses; it should not be read as a reason to avoid vigorous exercise.
- The suggestion that a calcium score of 400+ warrants angiography is his personal practice position; guidelines generally treat calcium scoring as a risk-stratification tool, not a direct trigger for invasive testing.

## Summary
Cardiologist Dr Anup explains why young Indians are having heart attacks — smoking above all, plus visceral "thin-fat" physiology, sleep loss, night-shift stress, and rare causes like exercise-induced arterial dissection and B12-deficiency clotting — and walks through what actually saves lives (CPR, defibrillators, fast door-to-balloon times), what health checkups and calcium scores can and cannot tell you, and how he protects his own heart.
