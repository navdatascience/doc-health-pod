## Important tidbits
- Myopia (short-sight) means light focuses in front of the retina — because the cornea is too curved or the eyeball too long — and is corrected with minus glasses; hyperopia is the reverse (focus behind the retina, plus glasses). Presbyopia is a separate, near-universal ageing change around age 40, when the accommodation muscle weakens and reading glasses become necessary.
- He cites studies projecting that by 2050 almost half the world's population will need glasses; rising childhood myopia is driven by genetics plus excessive near work/screens and too little outdoor activity — a couple of hours of outdoor activity with indirect sunlight exposure reduced myopia progression in children in studies from eastern countries.
- Children's eye screening can start at age 3–4 and should be annual, he says, mainly to catch "lazy eye" (amblyopia): if an uncorrected refractive error or squint makes the brain ignore the weaker eye during development, vision loss can become permanent. Treated at 5–6 with patching the stronger eye it can be corrected; by age 25, he says, there is not much that can be done.
- Adults also need annual eye checks because glaucoma is usually silent: raised eye pressure damages the optic nerve and peripheral vision first, and the damage is irreversible, so early diagnosis is everything. High blood pressure and diabetes (including sudden BP dips/spikes) raise glaucoma risk.
- Screen-time rules he gives: under age 2, avoid screens; ages 2–5, limit to about 1 hour a day in total (school, iPad, TV included); after 5, limit to what schoolwork requires and avoid leisure screen time as far as possible. For eye comfort: moderate screen brightness, larger font and larger screens over phones, room light coming from behind you, and frequent breaks.
- Dry eye from screens is mainly evaporation, not under-production: blink rate falls during screen use, the tear film evaporates faster, and AC/heater air blown at the face makes it worse. Watery eyes can paradoxically be dry eye — "reflex tearing," where the brain orders extra tears once the tear layer has evaporated. A warm compress for 5–10 minutes before sleep helps by opening the meibomian glands that make the tear film's oil layer.
- On blue light, he says the evidence is not conclusive either way: turning on a device's blue-light filter does no harm, but blue-light glasses are not required for children and are not proven to prevent myopia progression.
- Refractive surgery options: LASIK creates a corneal flap, reshapes the cornea with a laser, and replaces the flap; lenticule extraction (SILK/SMILE/CLEAR) removes a disc of tissue through a 3–5 mm incision with no flap; PRK removes the surface layer, which regrows in 3–4 days with slower visual recovery. Flapless procedures suit contact-sport/combat professions (no flap to displace) and cause less dry eye; functional vision is usually good within a week, with precautions for about a month.
- Eligibility for laser surgery: age over 18–21 with a stable number, adequate corneal thickness/shape on scans, no severe dry eye, and no diabetes, collagen-vascular or autoimmune disease; the retina must also be checked. For very high powers (typically beyond −9/−10), he prefers ICL — an implantable contact lens — rather than reshaping the cornea. Surgery is contraindicated in children because the eyeball is still growing.
- Post-surgery: complication and infection rates are "extremely low" but never zero; dry eye for a few months and night glare are the common temporary effects. The number rarely comes back (minor regression can be laser-touched-up or corrected with glasses), but presbyopia still arrives after 40 — and annual eye check-ups remain non-negotiable for life because myopes keep a higher lifetime risk of retinal problems.
- Contact lenses are not inherently bad — they are the main treatment for keratoconus — but he warns: never sleep in them, never swim in them (risk of Acanthamoeba infection), because keratitis can permanently damage the cornea.
- Home remedies are a firm no: sun gazing can cause a permanent retinal burn; cucumber, green-tea bags, coffee, onion juice and urine eye washes risk infection or injury — use a clean cold compress (for allergic eyes) instead. Kajal/mascara on the eyelid should be avoided, especially with dry eye, as it blocks the oil glands, and must be fully removed. Sunglasses with UV protection do help — against retinal degeneration, early cataract and pterygium.
- Eye-drop technique: open the bottle using the spike inside its own cap (never a pin or toothpick, which contaminates it); pull the lower lid down, instil without the nozzle touching the lid or cornea, leave 1–2 minutes between different drops, dab rather than wipe, and discard an opened bottle within a month.
- Diet cannot prevent or reverse myopia, he says — it is a structural problem; carrots/vitamin A mattered for deficiency-related corneal disease, which is now much rarer in India. Fish oil/omega-3 may have some role in dry eye but is not proven, and no food substitutes for reduced screen time and outdoor time. He also recounts a child whose firecracker (Diwali) injury ruptured the cornea and damaged the optic nerve — two hours of suturing could not restore proper eyesight — urging extreme care with crackers.

## Claims to be careful with
- The "half the world will need glasses by 2050" figure and the outdoor-activity effect on myopia are studies he cites in conversation, not data presented in the episode.
- His view that blue-light filters are harmless but unproven, and that omega-3 "may" help dry eye, are explicitly hedged in the episode — the evidence either way is inconclusive.
