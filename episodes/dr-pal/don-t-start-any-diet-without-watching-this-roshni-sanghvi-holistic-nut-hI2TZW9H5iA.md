## Important tidbits
- Carbs come in three forms — sugars, fiber, and resistant starch. When people say "low carb," Roshni argues it should mean low *sugar*, not low fiber or low resistant starch; fiber exists only in plants, so a low-carb diet almost automatically becomes a low-fiber one.
- Fiber targets: she aims for ~50 g/day with her clients; the stated minimums are 25 g/day for women and ~32–35 g/day for men, while the average Indian intake is only ~15 g/day. Increase fiber gradually over weeks so the gut can acclimate.
- Her practical framework: 3 servings of whole grains + 3 servings of lentils/pulses per day (a serving is roughly a fistful, ~3–5 g fiber). Fruits and vegetables alone won't get you there because of their high water content — two bowls of broccoli give only ~6–7 g fiber.
- "Plant points": aim for at least 10 different plant foods per day (70–100 per week) — nuts, seeds, fruits, legumes, lentils, vegetables all count; variety matters because different plants feed different gut bacteria.
- Eat a fruit before your meal as a standing lifestyle habit: it blunts the glucose spike, adds fiber and hydration, satisfies sweet cravings, and reduces how much biscuit/snack food you eat afterwards. A cup of berries before a meal controlled blood sugar better in the studies she cites.
- Whole fruit is not fruit juice: juice strips out fiber and polyphenols and delivers a large fructose load that is metabolised in the liver; with a sedentary lifestyle and low muscle mass, that excess fructose is converted to fat and drives fatty liver. Five to six fruits go into one glass of juice versus the one fruit you'd actually eat.
- Fruit timing myths are dismissed: eat fruit whenever, including with meals — food-combining rules from the 1950s–60s were disproven, and the digestive system is far more powerful than people assume.
- Diabetes and fruit: pre-diabetes is HbA1c 5.7–6.4%. In pre-diabetes or controlled diabetes, include fruits freely; in uncontrolled or type 1 diabetes, work with a nutritionist and favour low-GI fruits — apples, oranges, berries, guava, plums, cherries, papaya. High-GI fruits (mango, banana, jackfruit) are still nutrient-rich and fine in moderation when A1c is around 6.5–7, per both speakers.
- Meal timing matters: the body's ability to handle carbohydrates declines towards the evening (shown even with a constant IV glucose drip), and insulin is most active around midday–2–3 pm, so heavy meals like biryani are best eaten near noon. If you're awake late you'll inevitably snack — fix the sleep time rather than relying on willpower.
- Ghee: 1 tablespoon = ~120 calories and 10–12 g of fat, high in saturated fat; households typically pour 4–5 tablespoons into a single dish plus tadka and roti topping. Her advice: avoid cooking with ghee or use it very sparingly. She frames the ~2,000 kcal daily budget like $2,000 — every calorie should return maximum nutrients.
- Nuts and seeds: prefer raw, unsalted, unroasted (roasting increases advanced glycation end products, per Roshni). Add 1–2 tablespoons of nuts/seeds *with meals* (cashew paste in curry, tahini in hummus) to absorb fat-soluble nutrients. Soaking to remove phytic acid is optional — worthwhile for a weak gut or absorption problems, but forgetting to soak should never become a reason to skip nuts entirely.
- Protein: requirement is 0.8–1 g per **kilogram** of body weight (not per pound) — e.g., 55 g/day at 55 kg; slightly more when genuinely training hard. If you eat enough calories from whole plant foods, protein takes care of itself; the real widespread deficiencies are fiber, B12, vitamin D3, iodine and selenium, not protein.
- Soy is encouraged (~2 servings/day): it is a complete protein, and its isoflavones are described as protective against breast and prostate cancers and against menopausal symptoms when eaten in the 30s–40s. Prefer tofu, soy milk, whole soybeans and tempeh over soy chunks and processed plant-meat alternatives. Soy does not cause "man boobs," and isoflavone interference with thyroxine absorption matters only when iodine is deficient; cooking destroys most goitrogens.
- Paneer is a good protein source but fat-rich — 100 g is roughly 300–400 calories, so portion is critical (she recounts a 19-year-old eating 400–500 g paneer/day who developed high cholesterol). Tofu gives ~10–15 g protein per ~150 g serving at far fewer calories.
- The "second meal effect" (lentil effect): pulses at one meal blunt the blood-glucose spike at the *next* meal too, which is why diabetics are advised to include some lentils at breakfast, lunch and dinner. Lentil fiber is prebiotic; gut bacteria convert it to short-chain fatty acids (notably butyrate), which strengthen the gut–brain axis and support weight loss.
- Whole grains keep the bran and germ (refined grains keep only the starchy endosperm). Swap millets (e.g., foxtail) into bisibele bath, pongal, idli/dosa — taste buds adapt within a few tries; introduce one new recipe a week into your usual rotation of ~15 home recipes.
- Cooking mostly oil-free is presented as feasible: pressure-cook grains and dal without oil, keep only the shared family curry with a little oil (60–70% of the meal oil-free), and follow an 80/20 pattern — eat enjoyably when out, eat nutrient-dense at home.
- Parents modelling health is framed as the loudest way to tell your kids you love them: children who watch parents take care of their health rarely need to be sent to a gym.

## Claims to be careful with
- "Fiber is the only food group which cannot be converted to fat" and "you will not gain weight eating whole plant foods no matter how much" are simplifications stated as absolutes.
- The rat study (rats stayed lean on unlimited pellets but gained weight on unlimited cheesecake) and a study in which participants allegedly ate 20 cups of fruit a day and lost weight are cited loosely, without references.
- The claim that people on a calorie deficit may lose nothing because of defective mitochondria/insulin resistance, and that weight loss should be pursued "without calorie counting," is her framework, not settled consensus.
- Roasting nuts raising AGEs, with AGEs described as a root cause of diseases "from Alzheimer's to PCOS," overstates the evidence.
- Dr Pal's fecal-transplant anecdote — a C. difficile patient whose weight rose from 60 kg to 130 kg over three years, attributed to an obese stool donor — is a single unverified anecdote.
- "The treatment for hemorrhoids is fiber, not surgery" (Dr Pal) is broadly reasonable for constipation-driven cases but stated without qualification.
- Specific isoflavone figures (3,000 mg/kg in Canadian/US soy vs ~1,000 mg/kg in Indian soy) and the breast MRI study Dr Pal describes are presented without citations.

## Summary
Nutritionist Roshni Sanghvi makes the case that diets fail when they are built on restriction and calorie counting, and that the real gaps in the Indian diet are fiber and plant diversity, not protein: aim for ~50 g of fiber, 3 servings each of whole grains and lentils, and 10 plant points a day. She defends whole fruit (but not juice), soy, and high-GI fruits in moderation, warns about ghee and paneer portions, explains the lentil second-meal effect on blood sugar, and argues that environment design — fruit on the counter, workout clothes ready, a fixed sleep time — beats willpower every time.
