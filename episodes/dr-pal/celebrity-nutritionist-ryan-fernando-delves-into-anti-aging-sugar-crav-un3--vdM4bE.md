# Celebrity Nutritionist Ryan Fernando Delves into Anti-Aging, Sugar Craving&Restaurant Food Concerns

- **Guest:** Ryan Fernando, celebrity nutritionist (Bangalore), author of "Weightless", nutrition coach to athletes including Virat Kohli

## Important tidbits
- Ryan Fernando (age 50, says he looks 30) frames anti-aging as an attitude, not just a diet: "Your body is the most expensive real estate you own" — live in your body as a landlord, not a tenant; tenants mistreat their body and eventually get "evicted" by disease.
- His own turning point: he discovered he was gluten intolerant about 10 years ago (he had adult acne despite taking protein powders and training sports stars), which pushed him to study food scientifically; he wrote the book "Weightless".
- For looking younger he tracks visceral fat, subcutaneous fat and the gut microbiome rather than body weight alone.
- Case he cites: a 72-year-old client lost about 11 kg of body fat in a year and brought visceral fat down by five points.
- Eating out drives visceral fat: no portion control (a chef once served him 500 g of biryani), no control over contents, erratic eating times, and eating in "fast mode or stress mode"; he says humans today eat more than they require and use food as a "therapeutic bomb for stress".
- Habit change takes far longer than 21 days: citing habit research, he says the slowest people took roughly 250 days to form a habit, so he asks clients to follow a nutrition plan for about 254 days; food habits are governed by "motherly love" and culture, which usually beats science in a head-on battle.
- He personalizes plans with blood tests, genetic tests, omega-3 testing and microbiome testing, which he says improves client compliance because the plan becomes "my diet plan".
- He recommends a bioimpedance weighing scale that reports segmental fat (trunk, arms, legs), subcutaneous and visceral fat, and water percentage — good ones cost roughly ₹10,000 / $100 and above.
- Portion control starts at home: weigh cooked rotis and rice (have everyone serve themselves, then weigh the plates) until you can eyeball 100 g of rice accurately; calories are the first thing to master because desk workers ("desk jockeys") burn only about one meal's worth of calories through daily movement.
- Protein guidance: the baseline requirement is 0.8 g per kg body weight per day, but shoot for 1 g/kg; aim for roughly 20 g protein at each of breakfast, lunch and dinner, plus 15–20 g protein post-workout for people who exercise and have no gut issues.
- His favorite supplement is glutamine (about 5–10 g stirred into a blended vegetable or fruit juice), but he personally avoids protein supplements — after 15 years working for supplement companies and having a "delicate darling gut", he prefers to hunt down protein from food; he does not give protein supplements to clients with gut issues.
- Fruit sugar vs added sugar: natural fruit sugars are fine; the problem is man-made added sugar in packaged foods (a flight's mango juice had six teaspoons of added sugar). Juicing concentrates sugar without fiber — squeezing orange juice uses about eight oranges — so heavier or non-athletic people should eat one whole orange rather than drink juice.
- Coke vs Diet Coke: for an occasional cheat (once or twice a month) he prefers regular Coke in a smaller/shared quantity (about 250 ml, halved with a friend) over daily Diet Coke, citing research that chemical sweeteners affect the gut microbiome, insulin resistance and sugar cravings; he estimates a daily cola habit adds roughly 9 kg of weight gain a year.
- For a blood pressure patient he would add neither excess salt nor excess sugar — both BP and sugar problems arise when taste buds get desensitized and need more salt/sugar to feel satisfied, so the taste buds must be retrained; alternatives he suggests are celery salt for saltiness and stevia or monk fruit for sweetness (agave and honey are still high in sugar).
- Favorite fermented/probiotic foods: dosa batter fermented overnight, kanji (rice with buttermilk and onions, fermented, from Odisha), kimchi, amla pickled in apple cider vinegar and salt, and fermented garlic; Dr Pal adds that he advises every GI patient on two F's — fiber and fermentation.
- His top two Indian superfoods are amla and moringa (drumstick) — he grows three moringa trees in a large pot on his Bangalore terrace; his go-to travel snack is a ziplock of dates, pistachios or figs plus an organic, non-wax-coated apple.
- Two healthy-eating rules: "eat to your heart's content" — your heart is fist-sized, so eat about a fist-sized amount; and eat as close to sundown as possible.

## Claims to be careful with
- "Diabetes is reversible" is stated categorically, including a claim that he has reversed HbA1c in a patient on insulin through diet — individual results vary and changing diabetes medication without medical supervision is dangerous.
- The claim that chemical sweeteners in diet drinks cause insulin resistance and microbiome damage is presented as settled research; the evidence is more contested than stated.
- The weight-gain figures (a cola a day ≈ 9 kg/year; sugary tea/coffee ≈ 5.5 kg) are rough estimates, not measured outcomes.
- Glutamine is promoted as a favorite supplement (with the claim it makes up 60% of skeletal muscle); benefits for the general population are not established, and this sits oddly with his caution about supplements.
- Moringa is praised partly for its "vibrational element", which is not a scientific claim.

## Summary
Celebrity nutritionist Ryan Fernando tells Dr Pal that looking young at 50 comes from treating your body as your most expensive real estate: measure portions, hit protein targets (shoot for 1 g/kg, ~20 g per meal), watch visceral fat on a bioimpedance scale, prefer whole fruit over juice and occasional real Coke over daily Diet Coke, retrain desensitized taste buds instead of adding salt or sugar, and lean on fermented foods like kanji, kimchi and overnight dosa batter — while insisting diabetes can be reversed through diet and that habits need ~250 days, not 21, to stick.
