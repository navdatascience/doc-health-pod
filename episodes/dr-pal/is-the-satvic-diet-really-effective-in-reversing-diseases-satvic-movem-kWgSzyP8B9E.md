## Important tidbits
- Subah's story: throughout childhood she suffered PCOD, thyroid imbalance, hair fall, acne and being overweight, with life revolving around school and doctor visits; by age 17 she was taking six different pills a day, and she says the helplessness "shattered my confidence".
- Subah says that after learning food and lifestyle practices from teachers, within three to four months her TSH levels came completely normal, her PCOD was "completely reversed", menstrual cycles became regular, hair fall stopped and lost hair grew back, and her skin became vibrant — she later dropped out of school in class 11 to pursue this way of life.
- Harsh's story: from about age 7–8 to 18 he had chronic sinusitis and skin problems (psoriasis/eczema-like), used inhalers (Seroflo, Ventolin) and nasal sprays (Flixonase, Otrivin), and took roughly one 5-day antibiotic course every month.
- Harsh says his turning point was experimenting with stopping milk and milk products while studying in America: his skin improved and he could sleep without nasal sprays, and he later recognized the clean food that helped him as satvic food — no inhalers, sprays or steroids needed to breathe easily.
- The two met when Subah's workshop at Vedari filled 100 people on both Saturday and Sunday; Harsh was doing similar one-on-one clinic work, and Satvic Movement grew out of sharing practical, applicable wisdom "without complicating things".
- Their core principle: "Follow Mother Nature — she's the best nutritionist, the best doctor, the best everything"; they connect gut bacteria to health ("studies have shown you are what your gut bacteria is") and describe disease in terms of accumulated waste within the intestine, believing most diseases can be healed when the gut heals.
- Their processed-food framework has three lists: green list = raw ingredients even if they come in a packet (a bag of nuts, a box of dates or cashews); yellow list = processed but without additives, preservatives, refined sugar or refined oils (e.g., a clean choco bar made only of nuts, dates and raisins, or spiced roasted peanuts); red list = ultra-processed foods loaded with additives, preservatives, synthetic colors, sugars and refined oils (namkeen, bhujia, most biscuits and chips, frozen pizza).
- On frozen vegetables: not ideal, but far better than a frozen pizza — everything is a spectrum of closeness to nature; preparation also matters, because over-spicing, over-salting or frying a vegetable destroys its value, and a dish should taste of the vegetable itself (their teacher's saying: torai ki sabzi should taste of torai, not just of ghee and spices).
- Their sugar spectrum: completely unrefined sweeteners (dates, figs, raisins, fresh fruits, honey), then minimally refined (jaggery, maple syrup), then highly refined (white sugar and brown sugar); the guiding question is "how close is it to its natural form?"
- Their dessert philosophy is "replace, don't remove": swap refined industrial sugar for natural sweeteners so a dessert feels good before, during and after eating, not just during.
- On stevia and monk fruit: because a single compound is heavily extracted from the plant, they place plant-based extracted sweeteners somewhere between the yellow and red lists; synthetic sweeteners (sucralose, aspartame) are red list.
- Dr Pal offers a clinical counterpoint: he sometimes uses artificial sweeteners tactically — a morbidly obese patient (250 lbs) who couldn't quit Coke was switched to Diet Coke for one month, lost 10 lbs, and then became motivated enough to give up artificial sweeteners entirely; Dr Pal stresses individual tailoring over umbrella rules.

## Claims to be careful with
- Subah's claim that her PCOD was completely reversed and TSH normalized within 3–4 months through diet and lifestyle alone is a personal anecdote, not clinical evidence; stopping prescribed medication (she was on six pills a day) without medical supervision can be dangerous.
- The claim that sugar is "much more addictive than cocaine", supported in the episode by a mouse study, is a frequently overstated extrapolation from animal research.
- The idea that most diseases arise from accumulated waste in the intestine and can be healed by healing the gut is the movement's philosophy, not established medical science.
- Harsh's recovery after eliminating dairy is anecdotal; no tests or controlled evidence are presented.
- Despite the episode title, no clinical data on reversing diseases is presented — the case rests on the founders' personal stories.

## Summary
In part one of the Satvic Movement conversation, founders Subah and Harsh tell Dr Pal how their own childhood illnesses — her PCOD, thyroid imbalance, acne and hair fall on six pills a day; his chronic sinusitis and skin problems on inhalers and monthly antibiotics — led them to food-based healing and to building Satvic Movement around one principle: stay as close to Mother Nature as possible, using green/yellow/red food lists and a "replace, don't remove" approach to sugar, while Dr Pal pushes back with a pragmatic clinician's case for individual tailoring, including tactical use of artificial sweeteners.
