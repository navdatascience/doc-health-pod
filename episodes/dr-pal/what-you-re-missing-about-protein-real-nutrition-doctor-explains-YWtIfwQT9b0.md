## Important tidbits
- Dr Sundar's prescription is a *whole-food* plant-predominant diet: fill most of the plate with plants and shrink the meat portion ("swap it"), eat food in minimally processed form, and bring in colour diversity — each colour carries different phytonutrients and feeds different gut bacteria. She does not count calories on whole food, and says no plant causes inflammation unless it is processed; ultra-processed foods (fries, ice cream, deep-fried chicken) drive oxidative stress and free-radical damage behind diabetes and heart disease.
- Chew, don't drink, your fruit: juicing raises sugars and cuts fibre, and even blending breaks down fibre quality and releases sugars. A green smoothie is acceptable only if sipped/chewed very slowly and not loaded — just 3–4 nuts, not 10–15, and prefer chia/flax seeds (flax lignans, she says, have cancer-protective effects); she would rather add counted nuts to a salad.
- Rice: she eats unpolished native varieties (favourites: Navara red rice, Bakul Phool from West Bengal; also iron-rich Raktashali) bought in small quantities because unpolished/organic grain goes rancid and attracts worms within a month or two — long shelf life itself signals preservatives. She eats only ~2–3 tablespoons of rice because her plate is filled with sundal (legumes), vegetables and a green (keerai). Transition via semi-polished rice; Kerala matta can also be polished, so check.
- Millets are "not a superfood": healthier complex carbs, but don't replace everything with them or eat them in white-rice quantities — overconsumption of certain millets can be goitrogenic and dehydrating, most market millets are polished, and millet nachos, pizzas and ragi cakes are still junk formats. Rotate grains across the week instead.
- Dosa/idli reinvention: rotate batters weekly — black rice dosa (anthocyanins), millet dosa (proso/kodo in the US), quinoa dosa, or a pure lentil dosa (mixed masoor, toor, chana, moong and urad dals soaked and blended, no rice at all). Her idli uses 1 cup whole urad/moong dal to *less than a quarter cup* of rice, with vegetables grated in. She also keeps a sourdough starter and adds it to idli batter and rotis; fermentation breaks down carbs and makes food easier on the gut.
- Evidence she cites for plant-based eating: the Adventist Health Study, Nurses' Health Study and Health Professionals Follow-up Study (hundreds of thousands to millions of people) link plant-based and lacto-ovo vegetarian diets to lower all-cause mortality, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease and stroke; the latest Adventist analysis found ~24% lower cancer incidence in vegans and ~10–12% in lacto-ovo vegetarians, especially colorectal and hormonal cancers.
- Red meat and keto: a 2025 meta-analysis of 60 prospective studies with 2–3 million participants links more red meat to colorectal cancer; a 2025 Metabolism study (~200,000 people, median 28-year follow-up) links even unprocessed red meat to ~25–30% higher type 2 diabetes risk. Guidelines are tightening from 500 g/week toward ~300 g/week, and EAT-Lancet allows ~14 g/day. Keto may cut weight and sugar for ~6 months but has no proper long-term safety data, she says.
- Protein without meat: Brazilian and UK studies matching plant vs animal protein at 1.2 g/kg (3 days/week strength training) and 1.6 g/kg (5 days/week resistance training) found very similar muscle gains — provided intake is planned and diverse. Complete plant proteins she names: soy, hemp and quinoa. Dal caveat: dal has more carbs than protein, watery dal (e.g. thin Gujarati dal) is mostly water — eat it thick, 2–3 cups, and cut the rice instead; she eats dal by the bowl with vegetables as a main dish.
- Protein powders (she uses them herself as an endurance athlete in menopause): choose plant-based powders with at least 70–75% protein per scoop, no additives or flavouring agents, third-party tested; pea isolate can bloat, and the gut adapts over a couple of months if you start slow. She mentions fermented yeast protein as digesting comparably to whey, and peanut protein as good but very expensive (~10 kg peanuts per 1 kg powder).
- B12 and vitamin D: plant-based diets are B12-deficient, but she sees values under 100 in meat-eaters too, because of poor gut health and depleted soil — "everybody needs supplementation," with dose set by blood values, plus maintenance for life. Vitamin D deficiency is likewise across the spectrum (she has seen values of 6–7 in meat-eaters), so supplement regardless of diet.
- Milk: she stops dairy in her practice — she says when she removes milk, patients' sugar levels improve and allergic disorders disappear. Her arguments: humans are the only mammals drinking milk past infancy; mass milk production dates only to the 1970s White Revolution; continuously impregnated cows produce hormone-rich milk; milk protein raises IGF-1 and casein is linked in her account to allergies and some cancers. Paneer has more fat than protein — she directs people to tofu instead (protein, some fibre, unsaturated fat).
- Fats and oil-free cooking: saturated fat (solid at room temperature — ghee, coconut oil, palm oil, meat) should be under 10% of calories per WHO — on 2,000 kcal that is ~200 kcal ≈ 20 g/day, and one tablespoon of ghee alone is ~120 kcal and 14–15 g, before hotel dosas and pongal "lathered" in ghee. She cooks with *no oil at home*: tadka works on a dry pan heated until sprinkled water beads into one bubble, then mustard seeds splutter — flavour comes from hing, mustard, curry leaves, not oil. If oil is used, max ~1 tablespoon/day as a salad dressing, cold-pressed sesame or sunflower (better omega-3:omega-6 ratio); skip refined oils and olive oil (not native, expensive, low smoke point for Indian cooking). Steaming replaces oil for okra and brinjal; air-fryers should stay around 180°C and not exceed ~220–230°C because high heat on high-protein food creates AGEs.
- Eggs: she advises not crossing one egg a day (one to two a week is her comfort zone) — eat the whole egg if you eat it, since the yolk holds choline and other nutrients and egg-white-only eating misses amino acids. She cites meta-analyses linking ~half an egg a day to 30–40% higher diabetes risk and studies linking >3 eggs/week to cancer risk, and explains the choline→TMAO pathway: omnivores' gut bacteria convert choline to inflammatory TMAO, which she says plant-based guts lack the bacteria to do.
- Her personal story: went vegan in 2014 after obesity driven by ice-cream addiction, whole-food plant-based in 2017, and lost ~20 kg in 6 months post-menopause, keeping it off with body-fat percentage down ~10–15% on DEXA. Her method for cravings was not to fight them — eat the rasmalai mindfully, never stock it at home, and let the craving fade organically. "French fries are vegan" — vegan is not automatically healthy.
- Whole-food label literacy and children: per FSSAI, "wheat flour" on a label means maida unless it says *whole* wheat flour; eat fruit/vegetable skins where possible (pumpkin skin included); steel-cut oats > rolled oats > instant oats (instant is processed and sugary). She warns against childhood antibiotic overuse and polypharmacy eroding gut diversity in the first 5–10 years, and against exposing children early to jam, soda and ketchup dependence.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Sundar's clinical claim that simply stopping milk makes patients' blood sugar "plummet" and allergies disappear is her practice experience as stated in the episode, not trial evidence.
- The egg claims (>3 eggs/week increasing cancer risk; ~half an egg/day raising diabetes risk 30–40%) and the claim that egg white "takes in all your environmental toxins" and causes allergic disorders are her presentations of selected studies/assertions; she herself notes there is no conclusive data on eggs and cardiovascular disease.
- The claim that omnivores' gut bacteria convert choline to TMAO while plant-based eaters lack those bacteria entirely is a simplification as stated; TMAO production varies and is an active research area.
- Her statements that traditional rice varieties do not absorb arsenic the way brown rice does, that a named rice variety (lacha) protects against prostate cancer per a government study, and that flax lignans are cancer-protective are presented without the studies being shown in the episode.
- Her report of dropping 20 kg in 6 months post-menopause is a personal anecdote, not a typical-result claim.

## Summary
Lifestyle medicine physician Dr Lakshmi Sundar tells Dr Pal that "real nutrition" is a whole-food, plant-predominant plate built on diversity — native unpolished rices, rotating grain and lentil batters, thick dal and varied legumes for protein (which matched animal protein for muscle in the studies she cites at 1.2–1.6 g/kg) — while cutting dairy, limiting red meat and eggs, cooking without oil, and supplementing B12 and vitamin D whatever your diet. Protein, she argues, is a planning problem, not a meat problem.
