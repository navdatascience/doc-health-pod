## Important tidbits
- Water footprint comparison from Dr Sivaraman: producing 1 kg of rice takes roughly 3,000–3,500 litres of water, while 1 kg of millet needs only about 200–300 litres — millets are rain-fed crops ("manavari payir") needing no heavy fertiliser or pesticide.
- Know your millets: foxtail millet is thinai, little millet is saamai, kodo millet is varagu, proso millet is panivaragu, and ragi is finger millet. His personal favourites are ragi and thinai.
- Form matters for blood sugar: whole-grain millet has a glycemic index around 55–60, but grinding it into a smooth batter (idli/dosa) pushes the GI up to about 65–70 — so diabetics should prefer whole-grain preparations like pongal or vegetable rice.
- Soaking and sprouting reduce anti-nutrients by at least ~50%, and fermenting batter adds probiotics — which is why soaked, fermented ragi dosa is a good way to eat it.
- Ragi is calcium-rich and the traditional first weaning food (soaked ragi milk/kanji after 6 months of exclusive breastfeeding), though ragi's GI is slightly higher than foxtail or proso millet. Millets still must be eaten in moderation — they are carbohydrates too.
- Processed millet biscuits and noodles are a better option than maida junk, but processing reduces nutritional value; he notes Indian maida is refined down to ~60% extraction — essentially pure starch — unlike the limits in Europe/Canada.
- Dr Pal cites a study in which a group eating all-purpose flour saw stool gut-microbiome diversity drop by ~50%, while a high-fibre alternate-grain group's blood glucose and cholesterol improved within 3–4 months.
- His own breakfast pattern: fruits, sprouts or sundal (green gram/moong) 3–4 days a week, 10–12 soaked almonds daily, millet-based food about 5 days a week, and red rice (Mappillai Samba) idli/dosa. Black rice (Karuppu Kavuni) needs 2–3 hours of soaking and 6–7 cooker whistles.
- Siddha dietary advice (pathyam) is taste-based: asthma and sinusitis patients are told to avoid sweets (sweet = earth + water, producing more mucus), while osteoarthritis patients avoid tamarind and astringent/sour foods that increase dryness in already-dry joints.
- Fenugreek protocol: for diabetes, soak 1 teaspoon overnight and drink the water along with the seeds (soluble fibre leaches into the water); for sinusitis or throat irritation, dry-roast, powder, and take ½ teaspoon morning and afternoon. He also uses it for grade 1 fatty liver and estimates fenugreek gives roughly 4–6 g of fibre per teaspoon.
- Kadukkai (Indian chestnut/chebulic myrobalan) is, in Siddha, the only herb containing all six tastes: remove the inner nut, powder the shell, and take ½–1 teaspoon at night for a mild laxative effect. The traditional daily formula is ginger in the morning, sukku (dry ginger) powder in the afternoon, and kadukkai in the evening.
- Jeera (cumin) water is a prebiotic that aids digestion — add jeera to hot water and close the lid to preserve its volatile oils, and drink it warm after meals.
- Turmeric taken plain is only ~5% absorbed; it should be taken with fat (milk or ghee — the original "golden milk") and pepper raises curcumin bioavailability further. Food sources first: supplements are only for what the kitchen can't provide.
- Herb–drug interactions are real: he cites a paper finding turmeric reduces the effect of tamoxifen in breast-cancer survivors, suggesting separating them (turmeric in the morning, tamoxifen in the evening) — and argues integrative practice needs formal drug-interaction protocols.
- Dr Pal's cautionary case: a healthy 26-year-old who took an ashwagandha supplement to "optimise" her health developed complete liver failure and needed a liver transplant — his core argument against unregulated supplements.
- Both doctors endorse "reverse pharmacology" (a term he credits to Dr Ashok Vaidya): start from traditional experiential leads and work backwards to mechanism and safety with modern methods — the path that led Tu Youyou from a Chinese decoction to artemisinin and a Nobel Prize. His own model: for ~10 years he co-practised with his allopathic mentor Dr C. N. Deivanayagam (FRCP), who prescribed drugs like amlodipine/telmisartan while Dr Sivaraman added diet, Sitali pranayama and hibiscus tea on the same prescription for HIV, TB, diabetes and hypertension patients.
- His closing rule: "One person's benefit is not another person's prescription" — take social-media health tips to your own physician before using them, and never stop prescribed medication (including diabetes medicines) expecting a miracle cure.

## Claims to be careful with
- Social-media claims that black jeera (karunjeeragam) can prevent or cure cancer are explicitly called out by Dr Sivaraman himself as dangerous misuse of his edited clips — including a mother who wanted to give it to her 8–9-month-old baby.
- Kadukkai as "one of the best immune modulators" and the only herb with all six tastes are traditional Siddha claims, not trial-verified findings.
- The fenugreek fibre estimate (~4–6 g per teaspoon) and the millet/rice water figures are as stated by the guest; standard references may differ.
- The ~50% gut-microbiome drop on an all-purpose-flour diet is a study Dr Pal describes without an on-screen citation.
- The turmeric–tamoxifen timing workaround comes from a single paper the guest read; cancer patients should only act on such advice with their oncologist.
- The ashwagandha liver-failure case is a single patient anecdote from Dr Pal; it shows supplements can be risky, not that ashwagandha always harms the liver (the guest notes the preparation, extract type, or contamination could be responsible).

## Summary
Siddha physician Dr Sivaraman and Dr Pal have an unusually frank allopathy-vs-Siddha conversation: why the fight exists, what evidence each side can fairly demand, and how "reverse pharmacology" and integrated practice (which he did for a decade alongside an FRCP allopath) could bridge the gap. Along the way he gives a detailed case for millets over rice — water use, glycemic index, whole-grain vs batter — and practical Siddha kitchen protocols for fenugreek, kadukkai, jeera water and turmeric, while both men warn against unregulated supplements, miracle-cure fraudsters, and stopping prescribed medicines.
