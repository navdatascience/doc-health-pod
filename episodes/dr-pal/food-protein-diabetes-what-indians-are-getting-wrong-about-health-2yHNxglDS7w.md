## Important tidbits
- Dr Subramaniam tells patients to include vegetables at every meal — ideally about half a cup to one cup per meal — because a typical South Indian breakfast and dinner ("tiffin") contain no vegetables at all, and even lunch is mostly grain with very little vegetable.
- She emphasises local, economical vegetables (bottle gourd, snake gourd, ridge gourd, pumpkin) over exotic or overhyped ones, and eating a "rainbow" of different-coloured vegetables each day, because each vegetable carries different minerals and vitamins.
- Her own household is largely grain-free: rice and roti are not essential, sweet potato serves as the complex carbohydrate, and more than half her plate is vegetables plus a portion of dal. She says the need to pair every meal with rice is a mindset/habit, not a nutritional requirement.
- She explains the NOVA classification: group 1 is whole foods eaten as they are (apple, carrot); group 2 is minimally processed (rice, bread); group 3 is processed food with added salt/sugar (e.g. carrot becomes processed once made into carrot halwa); group 4 is ultra-processed food with additives (biscuits, packaged snacks, bhujia, namkeen). She prefers whole foods as much as possible.
- On plant diversity, she cites evidence for eating 30 different varieties of plants per week for gut health; Dr Pal says he teaches patients five to six "plant points" per day across nuts, seeds, vegetables and fruits, without repeating the same item.
- Saturated fat should be under 10% of total calories — on a 2,000 kcal diet that is 200 kcal, and she stresses this is a maximum ceiling, not a target ("even if you don't have that, it's even better"). Fat provides 9 kcal per gram versus 4 kcal per gram for carbohydrate.
- On ghee: she advises patients who insist on it to keep it to no more than one teaspoon for the entire day, says replacing oil with ghee "doesn't make sense," and states saturated fat increases the risk of heart disease and diabetes, with WHO and AHA recommending reduced saturated fat intake.
- On dairy, she personally avoids milk, butter, cheese and ghee, but allows curd and buttermilk in moderation for patients because curd is a probiotic integral to the Indian diet. Calcium and protein can come from foods like chana (sundal), which also provides fibre and protein, whereas milk (about 8–9 g protein per cup) has fat and no fibre.
- She cooks essentially oil-free — sautéing garlic and onion in water, measuring any oil with a teaspoon (cold-pressed sesame seed oil if used) — and considers the seed-oil / omega-6 panic overhyped relative to the stronger evidence against saturated fat.
- Eggs are a good source of protein and vitamins B12 and D, and she does not tell patients to stop eating them; however, the egg-and-cholesterol link is not clearly settled, and a daily egg plus ghee can easily push a person past the recommended saturated fat limit.
- On meal frequency, she says three meals plus two snacks a day is too much: gastric emptying takes about three hours, the stomach needs rest, and people should eat when actually hungry. For many of her patients breakfast is the first meal to go, with genuine hunger arriving around 12–1 pm; she also notes the Indian evening snack is often a full meal in itself.
- She promotes an early, light dinner around 7 pm: when she ran evening OPD while pregnant she carried a packed dinner and ate it at 7 pm, and now finishes dinner by about 6:30 pm.
- She cites Dean Ornish's study of about 48 heart patients: the experimental group on a plant-based diet plus exercise, stress management and social support saw the diameter of coronary occlusion regress by about 2.2% at one year (and more at five years), while the control group progressed and had more adverse events despite medication.
- A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) changed one elderly patient's behaviour: seeing rava upma spike her sugar "right up to 300," she modified her own diet — Dr Subramaniam says lifestyle medicine works when patients see the effect themselves.
- She notes South Asians carry an inherent risk for diabetes and heart disease (listed as a risk factor in guidelines such as NICE) and that these diseases present about a decade earlier in Indians, so prevention should start before any diagnosis.
- Practical portion rule she uses when eating out: grains should be about one fist; she weighs her food at home, and eats outside food about once a week, mostly salads.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Pal's statement that "a person can live without food for 72 days" because of stored fat was offered as a rhetorical point about overeating, not as fasting guidance.
- The claim that milk is pro-inflammatory because of its omega-6 content: Dr Subramaniam herself concedes there is no clear-cut evidence that milk is definitely pro-inflammatory, and notes dairy (especially curd/yogurt) appears protective in some conditions.
- Her suggestion that the nutrient content of vegetables has fallen substantially since the 1970s due to soil contamination, pesticides and nitrogen fixation disrupting the soil microbiome is presented from a book she read ("What Your Food Ate"); treat the magnitude as unverified.
- The Ornish reversal findings come from a small, intensive, multi-component lifestyle intervention (about 48 patients) — the regression cannot be attributed to the plant-based diet alone.

## Summary
Dr Pal talks with lifestyle-medicine physician Dr Nithya Subramaniam about what Indians get wrong about food: meals built almost entirely on rice and roti with barely any vegetables, ghee and dairy used freely, and constant eating across three meals and two snacks. She lays out her whole-food, predominantly plant-based approach — vegetables at every meal, 30 plant varieties a week, saturated fat under 10% of calories, minimal or no oil, early light dinners, and eating only when hungry — and explains the NOVA food classification, the dairy debate, and the evidence she uses with her diabetes and PCOS patients.

आई आल्सो वाज़ वैरी इट वाज वेरी इम्पोर्टेंट फॉर मी टू बी हेल्दी बिकॉज़ बोथ माय अह किड्स माय बॉयज दे लाइक टू गो आउटडोर्स अ लॉट। सो वी डू अ लॉट ऑफ़ ट्रेकिंग। वी डू अ लॉट ऑफ़ हकिंग। एंड आई वांट टू बी एबल टू डू एव्रीथिंग विथ देम। आई डोंट वांट टू बी सिटींग हियर सेइंग ओह माय नी इज हर्टिंग। इट्स नॉट पॉसिबल फॉर मी। आई वांट टू डू एव्रीथिंग विथ देम। सो, इफ दे आर क्लाइंबिंग, इफ दे आर हाइकिंग, आय वांट टू बी राइट देर विथ देम। सो, दैट वाज़ अ ह्यूज मोटिवेटिंग फैक्टर दैट आय कांट हैव एनी काइंड ऑफ़ फिजिकल डिसेबिलिटी वेयर इट लिमिटस माई एबिलिटी टू बी विथ माई किड्स व्हेन दे आर डूइंग समथिंग। वाओ। एंड यू हैव इनकर्पोरेट देयर इंटू योर प्रैक्टिस एंड हाउ सैटिसफाइंग इट इज नाउ। इट इज वैरी सेटिसफाइंग फॉर मी। आई डोंट नो। आई थिंक यू शुड आस्क माय पेशेंस दे हैप्पी अबाउट। इन टर्म्स ऑफ़ द रिकवरी दैट दे हैव हैविंग। या। आई मीन, देयर आर अ लॉट ऑफ़ पेशेंट्स हु अह आर डूइंग सो मच बेटर वंस आई हैव स्टार्टेड। एंड आई रियली लाइक इट व्हेन अह स्लाइटली सीनियर पेशेंट्स गेट मोर एक्टिव। इट्स रियली नाइस टू सी दैट दे आर मूविंग अबाउट मोर एंड दे कैन नोटिस दैट इट्स इम्पोर्टेन्ट। इट्स चेंज देयर लाइफ। सो। राइट यू नो आई हैव बीन डूइंग दिस न्यू मी प्रोग्राम फॉर द लास्ट लाइक टू इयर्स। एंड आई एम आई कैन क्लियरली से दैट दी अमाउंट ऑफ़ सैटिस्फैक्शन आई गेट विथ दिस डिजीज रिवर्सल इज़ मच मोर देन आई ट्रीट अ वैरिसियल ब्लीडर। याह। ऑर एन इंडिविजुअल पेशेंट व्हाइल आई वाज डूइंग ए गैस्ट्रोलॉजी। एंड माय कॉलिंग्स आर थिंकिंग दैट आई हैव गॉन नट्स। बट दे आर वैरी हैप्पी दैट आई एम डूइंग दिस बिकॉज़ दे रेफरी गॉन देयर पेशेंट्स। पेशेंट्स टू यू। सो आई होप दैट आर पर्सनल इन दिस पॉडकास्ट। अह सी हाउ मच एज अ प्रोफेशनल मेडिकल प्रोफेशनल यू हैव चेंज्ड एंड आई एम होपिंग दैट यू विल बी एन इंस्पिरेशन टू मेनी पीपल।
