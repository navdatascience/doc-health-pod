## Important tidbits
- Diets fight evolution, Dr Kumar says: human hormones are built to STORE fat because scarcity, not surplus, was the historical problem — India has had food surplus only for ~50 years, so the genes that once ensured survival (an obese person outlives a lean one in a 5–7 day famine) now drive obesity, and cravings are hormonal, not a willpower failure.
- Insulin resistance explained: cells stop responding to insulin, glucose cannot enter cells so blood sugar rises, the pancreas over-secretes insulin, and the excess insulin converts sugar to fat — producing fatty liver (triglyceride deposition), obesity, PCOS in the ovaries, and gout via the kidney/uric-acid pathway. There is also central insulin resistance in the hypothalamus, which drives appetite.
- Diseases are arriving a generation earlier: a father developing diabetes at 65, his son at 35–40, and grandchildren aged 10–12 already showing acanthosis nigricans (black neck patches), high fasting insulin and pre-diabetic HbA1c — type 2 diabetes is now seen in children, not just autoimmune type 1.
- Barker's hypothesis / "thin-fat Indian baby": low-birth-weight babies who are pushed to gain weight develop more diabetes, hypertension and metabolic syndrome (citing a CMC Vellore cohort followed since the 1970s); Indian babies carry more belly fat at the same birth weight as American babies. His advice: do not push a healthy lean child to become chubby.
- To beat cravings, change composition, not just quantity: increasing food volume with fibre suppresses ghrelin, while replacing carbs with protein and healthy fats lowers circulating insulin and restores satiety signalling; ketones produced on a low-carb diet themselves suppress appetite, so the food controls hunger instead of mental effort.
- Protein has a thermic effect: of 100 kcal of protein only ~60–70 kcal is actually derived, because 30–40% is spent digesting/metabolising it — versus ~90–95% for carbs and ~100% for fat — so a nominal 1,800 kcal high-protein diet may deliver only ~1,300–1,400 kcal.
- He uses TCR (therapeutic carbohydrate restriction) rather than strict keto: Indian intake is typically 300–400 g carbs/day (~150 g is "relatively normal"); even a 50% cut helps — e.g. 2 dosas + 2 eggs instead of 4 dosas, or 150 g rice + 150 g chicken instead of 300 g rice. Keto is defined as under ~20 g carbs/day, and a 100–150 kg person typically needs the strict phase only 1–2 years before moderating.
- "PCOS is not an ovarian disease at all" — it is the ovarian manifestation of systemic insulin resistance, he says; IVF/IUI treats it by externally stimulating the ovary while the cause is internal. He reports patients conceiving after 5–6 months of strict low carb following 20–30 IUI cycles and 3–4 failed IVF cycles, and argues diet should come first (or alongside) for PCOS-related infertility.
- Sugar is sugar: the body cannot tell whether a sugar molecule came from white sugar, jaggery, palm sugar, honey, dates or fruit — only the carbohydrate/sugar LOAD matters. White sugar, honey and high-fructose corn syrup at equal loads produced equally bad insulin-resistance and triglyceride outcomes in the study he cites; jaggery/palm sugar is only ~80–88% sucrose vs 100% for refined sugar, a 10–15% difference people erase by adding more for the same sweetness.
- Artificial sweeteners (stevia, monk fruit, aspartame, sucralose) carry no calories but raised INSULIN levels in their own testing while blood sugar stayed flat — a problem, he says, when insulin is already high, because the extra insulin converts calories to fat.
- Glycemic index (GI) misleads; glycemic LOAD is what matters: watermelon GI is >80 vs ice cream ~40–45, but watermelon has only ~8 g carbs per 100 g so its load is low. White sugar's GI (~55–60) is lower than rice/idli/dosa/chapati (~70–80); egg GI is zero (a diabetic at 150 mg/dL before four eggs is still ~150 after), nuts ~5–10.
- The main Indian lipid problem is high TRIGLYCERIDES, not LDL: triglycerides of 800–1,000 that drugs could not move fell to ~200 in 3 months on low carb, because triglycerides come from excess carbs being converted, not from eating fat. LDL responds poorly to diet (~20–30 points at most); genetically high LDL (~180–200) needs medication.
- Cooling cooked rice in the fridge for ~12 hours converts some digestible starch to resistant starch — the Sri Lankan study claimed 40–50%, replications found more like 10–15%; refrigerated bread may cut the spike ~10% in some people. CGM is a useful tool for DIABETICS to learn their personal responses, but pointless for a healthy insulin-sensitive person, whose sugar will not cross ~140 even after a huge carb load; a simple pre/post glucometer works too.
- Palm oil is "another villain that isn't": palmolein is ~55% MUFA (olive oil is 70–80% MUFA) and ~30–35% saturated fat, similar to groundnut/sesame/mustard oils (40–45% MUFA). Coconut oil is 92% saturated fat yet lowers triglycerides and raises HDL. He prefers traditional oils over PUFA-rich sunflower/rice-bran oils (omega-6, pro-inflammatory), and stresses fat quantity is a sliding scale set by your goal — 1 g fat = 9 kcal — with only ~3–5 tsp of oil used a day anyway.
- Soaking nuts does NOT remove phytic acid — studies comparing raw, soaked and sliced-soaked nuts found not even a 5–10% reduction — and 2–3 token nuts do nothing: in a low-carb plan nuts are used as a MEAL (50–70 g) replacing a carb meal; 10–15 nuts ≈ 100–150 kcal ≈ one dosa, so the swap only works if the dosa is actually dropped. Almond milk is just a vegan alternative (~30–40 kcal/cup), not nutritionally superior — real milk by definition contains lactose.
- Eggs and protein sources: 1–2 eggs/day is fine for most people, 3–4 (even 5) for most without familial dyslipidaemia — only ~5% see LDL shoot to ~180–190 and need monitoring. Animal foods provide all 9 essential amino acids (soy is the partial vegetarian exception); vegetarian protein comes with carb baggage (100 g chana ≈ 15–18 g protein but 50–55 g carbs), which is why cereal+pulse combinations (idli, pongal, rice+dal) exist. For omega-3, 100–150 g/week of fish such as sardines/anchovies beats capsules; plant ALA (flax, walnuts, fenugreek) converts at only ~5–10%.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Kumar calls the studies reporting harm from long-term keto (10+ years) "bogus/humbug," arguing they classify diet from a single survey day — this is his methodological critique, not a settled consensus, and long-term keto safety remains debated.
- The claim that a Cochrane review found no evidence that higher saturated fat worsens heart outcomes (and possibly fewer strokes) is his reading of that review; mainstream cardiology guidance still advises limiting saturated fat.
- His clinical reports — PCOS patients conceiving on low carb after repeated IVF failure, and triglycerides falling from ~1,000 to ~200 in 3 months — are practice anecdotes, not trial results, and the low-carb approach he advocates is stronger than standard dietary guidance for diabetes and PCOS.

## Summary
Pediatrician and nutrition educator Dr Arun Kumar explains to Dr Pal why weight loss is a hormonal problem, not a calorie-counting one: insulin resistance — in muscle, liver, ovaries and even the brain's appetite centre — drives cravings, fatty liver, PCOS and ever-earlier diabetes, and is best attacked by cutting the carbohydrate load (therapeutic carbohydrate restriction) while raising protein and traditional fats. Along the way he dismantles popular beliefs: jaggery and dates are still sugar, sweeteners raise insulin, glycemic load beats glycemic index, soaking nuts is useless, palm oil is not a villain, and triglycerides — India's real lipid problem — come from carbs, not fat.
