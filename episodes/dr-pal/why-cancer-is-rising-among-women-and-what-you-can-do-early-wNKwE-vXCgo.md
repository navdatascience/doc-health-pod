## Important tidbits
- In India, more than 50% of women affected by breast cancer are under 50 years of age and more than 25% are under 40 — roughly a decade earlier than in Western populations, which Dr Subramanyam attributes largely to lifestyle factors: obesity, inactivity, stress, late childbearing and not breastfeeding.
- Postmenopausal obesity raises breast cancer risk because peripheral fat produces estrogen; she cites a waist circumference above about 100 cm (measured at the belly button) as a marker of increased risk of inflammatory conditions including breast and endometrial cancer.
- "Unopposed estrogen" is the recurring mechanism: early menarche, late first childbirth, not breastfeeding, late menopause, long-term estrogen-containing contraceptive pills and hormone replacement therapy all increase exposure, while pregnancy and breastfeeding are progesterone-rich states that let breast cells fully mature and lower risk. Having the first child before age 30 reduces risk.
- Breastfeeding does not make a woman "safe" — it only reduces risk — and men can also get breast cancer, rarely, especially men with BRCA mutations.
- Only about 10% of breast cancers are due to genetic mutations, and 90% of women who get breast cancer have no family history; genetic testing is recommended for the affected patient when there is a strong family history of breast/ovarian cancer at young ages, and relatives are tested if she is positive.
- The most common symptom is a painless lump — she calls the belief that a dangerous lump should hurt "a very dangerous myth" and says many women present late because a painless lump felt harmless. Other warning signs: nipple ulceration or pulling in, and spontaneous, persistent, one-sided bloody or clear nipple discharge (a drop or two on squeezing is usually normal).
- Self breast examination should start at age 20, done monthly within the first week (roughly the first 7–10 days) after a period when the breast is softest: inspect in a mirror for visible changes, then feel all quadrants and the armpits with the pads of the middle three fingers, since breast tissue extends into the armpit and cancers can spread to axillary lymph nodes.
- Evaluation is age-based: under 30, ultrasound first; over 40, mammogram first; between 30 and 40 it depends on how suspicious the lump is. 80–90% of breast lumps turn out benign, and a recommended biopsy is not a cancer diagnosis — benign biopsies are accepted to avoid missing an early cancer.
- Screening mammograms should be done every year from age 40 even with no symptoms or family history: self-examination only finds lumps at about 2–3 cm, while mammography can catch stage 1 cancers under 2 cm and precancerous changes. With a family history, screening starts 10 years before the youngest affected relative (with MRI, not mammogram, for younger dense breasts; BRCA-positive women can start MRI screening at 25).
- She counters the two common mammogram myths: compression causes brief discomfort, not real pain (schedule it in the first 10 days of the cycle), and its radiation dose is minimal — about equivalent to seven weeks of natural background radiation, far less than a CT. Fewer than 10% of eligible Indian women get screening mammograms, and in India the cost (around ₹2,500) is out of pocket.
- Early detection changes treatment: stage 0–1 cancers have an almost 100% cure rate per Dr Subramanyam, may need only lumpectomy (breast-conserving surgery, always followed by radiotherapy to kill microscopic cells) instead of mastectomy, and may not need chemotherapy at all. Chemotherapy, when used, typically runs about three to four months and hair loss is temporary.
- On lactation: colostrum in the first three days is small in quantity but rich in antibodies and fully adequate; formula top-ups reduce demand and therefore supply. Oxytocin (released during feeding) falls when the mother is stressed or distracted — cortisol suppresses it — so she advises minimising phone use while feeding. A persistent lump after feeding, redness, skin thickening or fever can signal infection, and mothers should keep feeding through an infection rather than stop, to avoid milk stasis and abscess.
- Cervical cancer is the second most common cancer in Indian women (typically ages 40–50), over 90% of it caused by HPV (mainly strains 16 and 18). The HPV vaccine is ideally given at 9–15 years (two doses, at 0 and 6 months), is recommended up to age 45 (three doses after 15: 0, 1 and 6 months), works best before sexual exposure, and is advised for boys too; the nine-strain vaccine costs around ₹13,000 per dose, while the government provides the two-strain (16/18) vaccine.
- Pap smears from age 20 to 65, once every three years, detect precancerous cervical changes; precancer is treated by excising the affected area, early invasive cancer by hysterectomy, and later stages mainly by radiotherapy and chemotherapy.
- Endometrial (uterine lining) cancer mostly affects postmenopausal women over 50; any bleeding more than a year after periods stop — or irregular/intermenstrual or heavy bleeding before menopause — should be checked, starting with an ultrasound of the endometrial lining, and because it usually presents early it is most often completely cured.

## Claims to be careful with
- The "almost 100%" cure rate quoted for stage 0–1 breast cancer is the guest's rounded figure for early-stage disease generally; actual survival varies by subtype and stage.
- The waist-circumference figure of "more than 100 cm" as an increased-risk threshold is higher than commonly used cut-offs for women (often 80–88 cm); Dr Subramanyam presents it as a general marker from a study she recalls.
- Statements that breastfeeding and early pregnancy "reduce" breast cancer risk are population-level associations; she herself stresses they do not protect any individual woman completely.
- The recommendation that Indian screening should start earlier than 40 is her clinical opinion based on younger Indian case ages; the episode's formal recommendation remains annual mammograms from 40.

## Summary
Breast imaging specialist Dr Prema Subramanyam explains why breast cancer is appearing a decade earlier in Indian women — more than a quarter of cases under 40 — and walks through prevention and early detection in detail: unopposed estrogen and obesity as drivers, breastfeeding and early childbirth as partial protection, monthly self-examination from age 20, annual mammograms from 40, and why a painless lump must never be ignored even though 80–90% of lumps are benign. The conversation also covers lactation myths, premenstrual breast pain, HPV vaccination and Pap smears for cervical cancer, and postmenopausal bleeding as the key warning sign of endometrial cancer.
