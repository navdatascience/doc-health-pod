## Important tidbits
- This is part 2 of Dr Pal's childhood-trauma conversation with Riri Trivedi. She says about 70% of her clients are women and 30% men: society permits women to be vulnerable and seek help, while boys are taught from childhood to handle things alone, so men discuss cricket, careers and investments — never what happened at home or how they feel.
- On suicide, she says India's share of global suicides has risen from 1 in 5 to 1 in 4, suicide is a leading killer of young Indians (discussed for ages ~15–39) and the biggest cause of death for young Indian women; she names loneliness and depression as the biggest contributors, rooted in childhood trauma — specifically emotional abuse, emotional neglect and peer isolation, i.e. covert abuse, not necessarily sexual or physical abuse. Covering or removing ceiling fans in hostels, she says, treats only the symptom.
- Peer isolation hits two kinds of children hardest, she says: very introverted or low-self-esteem children whom others exclude, and very smart teacher's-favourite children the whole class resents; both can carry a felt sense of "nobody cares about me" into adulthood — even inside joint families or a room of 50 people. But introversion is not loneliness: an introverted child with 2–3 close friends can be confident and deeply connected, while an extrovert with 50 friends can be shut down inside, so parents should not assume an introverted child is lonely.
- Comparison parenting is now driven by the parents' own self-esteem, she says: parents who grew up compared push children to top every class and activity to prove themselves good parents, so the child performs to keep the parent happy, links failure with shame instead of learning, and develops anxiety and panic attacks — while mothers take a child's exam marks (her example: 1 out of 20) as proof they themselves are failures.
- Dr Pal recounts a teenage patient with panic attacks and gut complaints whose endoscopy, colonoscopy and CT were all normal — a psychosomatic presentation; Trivedi says parents of 16–17-year-olds must educate and open a dialogue rather than dismissing symptoms as "normal teenage problems," especially since social media now adds self-imposed pressure (body image, and even peer pressure in some countries to take ADHD or anti-anxiety medication because classmates use it for focus).
- Connection is built before the teens: if communication in ages 0–14 was not overly critical, abusive, neglectful or helicopter-style, teenagers will return after the turbulent years. Connection means presence without constant advice, interrogation or lecturing — sitting together, walking, asking about their music or a show, not judging their friends — combined with firm boundaries on a few clear rules. Parents must also hold the difficult conversations themselves: she spoke to her own children around age 14, her daughter about saying no, consent and safe sex, her son about respecting that "no means no" and about sexual orientation, so children know these topics can be raised at home.
- Her book's 80/20 rule (from the Pareto principle): if there are 10 things you want to improve in your child, pick only 2 — criticize all 10 and the child will listen to none; let the other 8 go and the chosen 2 will likely be honoured.
- Parental absence is itself a form of neglect, she says — a parent sitting beside a playing child while absorbed in their phone misses the child's emotional signals (even a changed tone of voice), teaching the child not to recognize emotions and making the relationship transactional; a fully present parent, even for half an hour (sitting, eating or cooking together), teaches the child far more. Ice cream outings, movies, mall trips, shopping, buying the next iPhone and parent-organized playdates are not connection, and over-structured, over-protected childhoods produce social anxiety: children kept to fixed people, times and activities panic among strangers and crowds, so parents should still take children to family gatherings and social events — without forcing them to mingle — and turn their observations into conversation.
- Children learn emotional resilience by imitation, not instruction: watching a father discuss a work difficulty, or a mother take breathing exercises after an argument and come back calmer — not from resilience videos or courses. A hobby or passion outside work and studies also builds resilience and social support: children raised as performance machines (toppers with no hobby) struggle more later with depression, loneliness and breakdowns than those who can channel energy into singing, running, painting or similar pursuits.
- Trivedi's own contrast: her daughter, cared for by a nanny while she worked, developed an anxious attachment style, while her son, with her full-time in Japan, is calmer and more balanced; she later told both children she had made mistakes and sent both to therapy. Her guidance: arrange for at least one parent to be present in the formative years (birth to ~4–5), and give at least 30 minutes to 1 hour of quality time daily between ages 5 and 15 — but a resentful, frustrated mother forcing herself to stay home all day is worse than a working mother, so parents must first understand themselves; she notes many young adults now take therapy specifically to heal before planning a child.
- Before planning a pregnancy, she advises assessing your mental health on a 1–10 scale: if it is above 7, take time to heal even at the cost of delaying conception. IVF is psychologically traumatic, she says, and chronic stress works against the body conceiving; she prepares IVF patients with yoga nidra and relaxation so the nervous system settles first.
- Every family has at least one person who needs help, she says; people mask pain, anger, grief and shame with drinking, smoking, pornography or work. Workaholism is an addiction like alcoholism — an escape that numbs feelings — which is why, in COVID, men who had worked 7 days a week to avoid home collapsed with anxiety when forced to stay home. Her book's self-test questions (from its 12 hidden outcomes of trauma, which include overworking and over-caring) include: "What are you escaping from? If you were not working, what do you fear?"
- Asked what harms a child more long-term — being shouted at or being emotionally ignored — she answers emotional neglect: a shouted-at child eventually becomes "selectively deaf," but neglect damages the child's core sense of self ("I am not wanted, not important, have no value"), leading to depression, loneliness and low self-esteem. She is explicit that she has no research data behind this comparison; it is her clinical opinion. Hyper-independence can be the related trauma response: a child repeatedly pushed away when seeking parents learns survival means depending on no one — she gives her own example of being praised as "so independent" for sleeping alone from 3 months and letting herself into an empty home at age 5, and only recognizing the underlying abandonment and loneliness in her own inner-child therapy as an adult.
- Over-apologizing is learned in childhood to keep harmony at any cost and avoid conflict (and was often praised as being a "good child"); in adult relationships, apologizing when you have done nothing wrong buries the problem, signals weak boundaries and builds resentment — an apology should be genuine, she says.
- Punitive silence is, in her words, the most toxic pattern — worse than shouting: a parent who does not speak to a child for a week over bad marks leaves the child in constant guessing, toxic shame ("because of me, my father is silent") and severe anxiety. A temporary, agreed withdrawal to cool down and then discuss — between spouses or with a child — is different and healthy.
- Parents often parent their own inner child's unmet need rather than their actual child: Trivedi was obsessive about being home with a hot snack when her children returned from school because she had missed that herself — her children later said they never needed it. Her question for parents: "Is this my child's need, or mine?" Dr Pal recognizes the same pattern in his own over-presence with his children, rooted in his father's unavailability. She also argues Indian voices are missing from trauma literature — the field cites Gabor Maté, Bessel van der Kolk (The Body Keeps the Score) and Bruce Perry — so her book integrates the Indian Pancha Kosha framework for how trauma affects a person and how healing can be structured, and she says two-thirds of the book is solutions rather than problems.

## Claims to be careful with
- The suicide statistics (India's share rising from 1 in 5 to 1 in 4 global suicides; suicide as the biggest cause of death for young Indian women) are stated conversationally without a cited source in the episode; verify against current official data before quoting.
- Her strongest framings are opinions she presents in absolute terms: parental absence "100%" equals abuse, punitive silence is "the most toxic" and "worse than abuse," and a fully present parent teaches a child "ten times" more. She explicitly acknowledges having no research data for the neglect-versus-shouting comparison.
- Her claim that the body does not distinguish real from imagined experience (offered as the basis for inner-child visualization/reparenting work) is a therapeutic framing, not an established physiological fact.
- The peer-pressure anecdote about teenagers in some developed countries taking ADHD medication for focus is a ~20-year-old second-hand account from Singapore, retold in the episode.

## Summary
In part 2 of their childhood-trauma conversation, mental-health practitioner and author Riri Trivedi tells Dr Pal that emotional neglect — not just shouting or physical abuse — is what quietly damages children's core sense of worth, feeding the loneliness and depression behind India's youth suicide numbers. She covers peer isolation, comparison parenting driven by parents' own unhealed trauma, phone-distracted parental absence, the 80/20 rule for picking battles, workaholism as an addiction of escape, hyper-independence and over-apologizing as trauma responses, and why punitive silence may be the most toxic pattern of all — closing with her push for an Indian, Pancha Kosha-based voice in trauma literature.
