## Important tidbits
- Dr Tanaya defines a normal menstrual cycle as regular bleeding every 21–35 days, lasting about 2–7 days, with only mild cramping; a one-off irregular cycle after stress, weight change or travel can be normal, but persistent change should be investigated — she calls menstrual health a monthly "report card" and an extra vital sign.
- Heavy bleeding red flags: soaking through a pad or period product in less than 2 hours (see a doctor right away), changing more than 4 pads a day, or passing clots larger than a coin; bleeding under a day or beyond ~10 days also warrants a visit.
- Period blood colour does not diagnose hormones: bright red is fresh blood, darker red is older, and brown/black blood is simply old, low-flow blood oxidized in the vagina's acidic environment — common at the start and end of a period; grey-looking blood is a reason to see a doctor.
- Exercise-related amenorrhea is common, she says: severe undereating or over-exercising (including marathon training) disrupts brain-to-ovary hormonal signalling because the body conserves resources, and periods can stop entirely; she also names orthorexia and social-media-driven body dysmorphia as growing problems among Indian teenagers.
- Vaginal discharge is the vagina's normal self-cleaning: it ranges from clear, egg-white-like to creamy, and can look yellowish after exposure to air. See a doctor if it is obviously bright yellow or green, frothy, has a strong off smell, or comes with itching, burning, pain or skin changes — causes range from fungal infection to STIs.
- Never clean inside the vagina — with soap, scented washes, douching or even plain water: the vagina is an acidic, pH-sensitive mucous membrane whose protective bacteria keep bad bacteria out, and disturbing it causes dryness, micro-cracks and more infections. Only the vulva (outside) may be cleaned, ideally with water alone or a gentle, fragrance-free soap.
- Menstrual cups: a medical-grade silicone cup sits below the cervix and collects blood at the source; one cup can last up to 10 years, costs roughly ₹300 for basic ones and ₹1,800–2,500 for the ones she recommends in India, and she says studies have not shown an increased infection risk when used properly — wash hands before and after handling, and sterilize by boiling for 10 minutes at the end of the period.
- A cup can be worn for up to 12 hours, but every other period product — pads, tampons, cloth pads and period panties — should be changed within 8 hours because blood is a good medium for bacterial growth; she advises a backup panty liner for the first 2–3 cycles while learning when your cup fills (for some people 6 hours, for others 12), squatting and relaxing the pelvic floor to insert, and pinching the base to break the vacuum to remove.
- The hymen is a stretchable membrane with openings in it, not a seal ("not a tetra pack"); virginity is a social construct. A completely closed hymen (imperforate hymen) is a medical problem that can trap months of blood and cause severe pain in teenagers who have never had a period. Her youngest cup user is a 14-year-old sportsperson whose mother is informed.
- Period-delay pills work by prolonging progesterone, and she insists they must only be taken under medical supervision: clinicians must check for liver disease and personal/family clotting risk first, because unsupervised use has led to deadly blood clots.
- Endometriosis — endometrial tissue growing outside the uterus — traps blood with no exit, causing severe pain, painful sex, pain when peeing or pooping, and impaired fertility; Dr Pal recounts a patient whose endometriosis in the rectosigmoid colon presented as constipation that worsened around periods. "Get married" or "have kids" is not a cure, Dr Tanaya says.
- PMDD (premenstrual dysphoric disorder) is a severe depressive episode — up to suicidal ideation — that recurs right before periods and resolves within 2–3 days of bleeding starting; she advises seeing a psychiatrist and gynecologist together, and notes she never learned about PMDD in medical school.
- For PMS, progesterone drives cravings for simple carbs; she recommends tryptophan-rich foods (tryptophan supports serotonin synthesis) and relays Dr Pal's three habits — complex carbs such as sweet potato, rajma and channa, some dark chocolate, and raising water intake from 1–2 litres to about 3 litres a day — plus keeping a menstrual/trigger diary.
- Recurrent UTIs after menopause are often caused by estrogen loss (genitourinary syndrome of menopause) drying the urethra-vagina area, not poor hygiene; she says she starts menopausal patients on vaginal estrogen cream, which she links to fewer hospitalizations and urosepsis. More broadly, both doctors recommend a trigger diary — food, exercise, sex, water in the days before symptoms — noting a field worker's "public toilet UTIs" were actually dehydration.
- HPV vaccination: HPV has ~200 strains; the 4-strain and 9-strain vaccines available in India both cover the highest-risk cervical-cancer strains. She recommends it for everyone aged 9–45, including men (HPV also causes anal, penile and head-and-neck cancers); under 15 needs 2 doses, over 15 needs 3 doses (at 0, 2 and 6 months); it should not be given during pregnancy. Pap smears remain the second line of protection, roughly every 3 years between ages 25 and 65.

## Claims to be careful with
- Her Scotland/UK figures — a ~90% reduction in cervical cancer after routine HPV vaccination and "not a single case" in about 10 years — are cited from memory; she herself says she has not fully read the latest study.
- The claim that vaginal estrogen has a "direct relationship" with reducing hospitalization and urosepsis in menopausal women is stated strongly and should be checked against current evidence and individual suitability.
- She mentions a push in the gynaecology community to reclassify endometriosis "as a cancer" because it establishes its own blood supply and colonizes other sites — this is a framing she reports, not a formal reclassification.
- Menstrual-cup safety and the 12-hour wear time assume correct insertion, hand hygiene and sterilization as described in the episode.
