## Important tidbits
- Know your skin type first, she says — it is the basis of any routine. Home test: wash with a mild, pH-balanced gentle cleanser, wait 30–60 minutes; stretched/tight feeling = dry skin, shiny = oily, no change = normal, shiny T-zone with dry cheeks = combination. Sensitive skin is a temporary state, not a type. Skin type changes with age, food, climate and place.
- The three pillars for everyone are cleanser, moisturiser and sunscreen (add an anti-ageing serum only if ageing is a concern). Normal skin needs nothing more — "do not spoil something that is already good." Beginners should get one dermatologist consultation to learn their skin basics before experimenting with trending products.
- The routine can be minimal: dry skin can skip a morning cleanser (just cold water), and a hydrating/nourishing sunscreen can replace moisturiser, making mornings a 1–2 step routine.
- Sunscreen picks: SPF 50 is the "sweet spot" (SPF 30 is also acceptable; above 50 is largely marketing with marginal extra benefit), with PA+++ minimum and PA++++ preferred. Look for labels mentioning blue-light / critical-wavelength protection, especially for indoor screen workers — she says she sees many IT patients with pigmentation driven mainly by blue light, poor diet, smoking and sedentary lifestyle rather than sun.
- On "safe" filters: she tries to avoid oxybenzone, avobenzone and octocrylene-type filters (benzophenones) flagged as potential endocrine disruptors, preferring newer-generation filters; octocrylene is acceptable only within certified permissible limits. Newer long-wear sunscreens may last a full day, but since labels don't reliably say so, reapply every 3–4 hours if you have continuous sun or blue-light exposure; otherwise once a day is enough.
- How much: the two-finger rule — two full finger-lengths of sunscreen for face and neck; three finger-lengths if you also cover the ears and back of the neck, which matters more for men because their ears and neck are exposed. Under-applying because sunscreen is expensive is, she says, the biggest mistake people make.
- Don't touch your face unnecessarily: hands and phones transfer dirt and bacteria, picking acne worsens pigmentation, and friction itself causes pigmentation (friction melanosis) on Indian skin. Mascara/kajal on the eyelid should generally be avoided — especially with dry eye — and must be fully removed.
- Facials are overhyped, she says: relaxation, blood circulation and a temporary glow only, with no long-term benefit; "party peels" give a glow in 5–10 minutes, and peels/lasers that boost collagen do the deeper work. On collagen supplements she was long unconvinced (early studies were company-sponsored) but now prescribes marine/bovine collagen peptides based on newer third-party studies — bovine slightly better, marine preferred by many for religious reasons — and stresses there is no such thing as "vegan collagen"; it only works alongside a healthy diet.
- Bone broth is her food source of collagen: bones simmered 18–24 hours until the peptides seep out and the bones go soft enough to chew; make a week's batch and store it. She also notes a Stanford animal study she was involved in linking high fibre intake to increased collagen production.
- Glutathione is "abused and misleading," she says: it is a genuine master antioxidant (its skin-glow effect was noticed in cancer patients), but oral glutathione alone does nothing without the rest of skincare, IV glutathione needs long, continued use and results fade within ~a month of stopping; antioxidant-rich foods like blueberries/polyphenols work on free-radical damage in their own ways.
- Psoriasis is "a loud disease" — thick red plaques, usually itchy, most often on the trunk/scalp, anywhere from scalp to toe — and her central claim is that it is not just a skin condition: she treats most psoriasis patients mainly with strict diet and lifestyle changes and hardly any medication, with slower but sustained results and milder relapses. Triggers she names: leaky gut/immune disturbance, stress (flares predictably follow exams and year-end work closures), poor sleep, infections, smoking, and very hot showers.
- Her psoriasis non-negotiables: no hot water (lukewarm only — hot water strips the barrier, causes transepidermal water loss and histamine-driven itching), loose cotton clothing only (no synthetics/nylon), stress management, and sleep discipline — lights off by ~9:30 pm, asleep by 10, at a consistent time, because repair hormones peak in the 5th–6th REM cycle (each ~90 minutes), so under ~6 hours of sleep misses that final repair push. Melatonin supplements are no substitute for natural sleep timing.
- Hair: pattern balding (androgenetic alopecia) is now appearing in teenagers, mainly boys, driven by genetics (DHT attacking predisposed follicles) plus lifestyle; it is progressive, unlike telogen effluvium hair fall (shedding after fever, deficiencies, stress, late nights — temporary once the cause is fixed). See a dermatologist immediately for thinning, because follicles lost permanently can't be recovered and hair is a limited resource — a transplant only redistributes your own follicles and never restores original density.
- Finasteride vs minoxidil, as she explains it: finasteride (1 mg/day) pauses the process by blocking testosterone-to-DHT conversion (cutting DHT ~60–70%); minoxidil cannot stop the process but drives regrowth by increasing blood flow. The feared finasteride sexual side effect occurs in under ~3–3.8% of patients, is monitored (liver tests, symptom checks), and reverses on stopping; both drugs are effectively lifelong. For higher grades she adds GFC (growth factor concentrate), which she finds more effective and less painful than PRP, and expects to know within ~6 months how much hair can be regained (~60–70% improvement with consistent treatment). Iron deficiency is a major added factor in Indian women.
- Hair gummies: "don't buy into it" — mostly low-dose biotin (2,000–2,500 mcg vs 5,000–10,000 in tablets) plus sugars/additives for palatability, and biotin only helps if you are biotin-deficient, which is rarely the sole cause. Take a properly curated multivitamin after blood work instead, with cyclical (not year-round) supplementation. Two to three different-coloured fruits a day may cover many vitamins, but not vitamin D for a deficient vegetarian.
- Myth-busting: toothpaste on pimples shrinks the pimple but irritates skin and leaves a dark spot (and toothpaste foam residue around the mouth can itself cause pigmentation — rinse thoroughly after brushing); oily/fried food has no direct link to acne (sugar/milk and overall inflammation do more); ice-dunking only temporarily constricts pores and overdoing it can burn/sensitise skin; tingling usually means barrier irritation, not that a product is working; and skincare is for everyone, men included. For vitamin D sun exposure she suggests ~20–30 minutes around 10 am–2 pm with sunscreen kept on the face/neck and the rest of the body exposed.

## Claims to be careful with
- Her position that blue light from screens is the most important driver of facial pigmentation in indoor IT workers is her clinical view; the strength of blue-light pigmentation evidence is still debated.
- Treating psoriasis primarily with diet and lifestyle and "hardly any medications" is her personal practice approach; moderate-to-severe psoriasis often needs medical therapy, and readers should not stop prescribed treatment based on this.
- The finasteride side-effect rate (~3–3.8%), DHT reduction (~60–70%), and expected ~60–70% improvement figures are as she stated them in conversation.
- Her endorsements of collagen peptides, GFC over PRP, and party peels reflect her practice experience and selected newer studies, not settled consensus.
- The claim that repair requires reaching the 5th–6th REM cycle and a fixed 10 pm bedtime is a simplification of sleep science presented as a firm rule in the episode.
