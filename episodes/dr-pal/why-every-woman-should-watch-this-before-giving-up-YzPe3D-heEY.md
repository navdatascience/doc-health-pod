## Important tidbits
- Malika's turning point: raised in a strict, sheltered home with no career expectations, she struggled after marriage — difficulty conceiving, anxiety about facing people, and a breakdown 12 days after her daughter was born because she felt she would fail as a mother. She rebuilt herself through a therapist, a life coach, and other mentors, with her husband's support.
- She now runs a program of about 10 weeks for women (largely ages 25–55) that works through emotions one by one — courage, confidence, boundary-setting — with homework and a judgement-free community, because many women feel lonely and unheard even when surrounded by people.
- Her "dead rat" analogy for the past: an unresolved past is like a dead rat in the middle of a room — covering it with an expensive carpet or spraying perfume doesn't help; it has to be thrown out, and while it stays, it makes the whole family miserable. Bringing old fights into every new argument only adds chaos.
- It is okay to be a "B-grade" or number-10 mother in one area: the belief that a woman must be number one as wife, mother, and earner simultaneously is what stops most women from starting anything for themselves; the family doesn't need to be let go of, just balanced.
- Her anger protocol: do not react, speak, or respond in the moment — go into isolation (a room or even the restroom), close the door, set a 20-minute timer, and let the emotion out safely there (cry, crush a paper, hit a pillow — not the phone). Only after the emotion drops should you sit with the person and discuss what happened, because "when your emotions go high, your intelligence goes low."
- She quotes the Buddhist saying that staying angry is like drinking poison and expecting somebody else to die.
- Consistency changes how people treat you: she went from crying 3–4 days a week to crying 2–3 times a year, and her husband stopped brushing her tears off and started taking them seriously — the people around you adjust once they see sustained change.
- Financial independence: "Every single woman should be financially sound, otherwise other people will become your life deciders." She advises women not to feel guilty about spending a husband's money while building their own income — her husband sponsored roughly the first ₹10 lakh she invested in her own training, without which she says she wouldn't be where she is — while working in parallel toward earning themselves.
- Fear of failure is usually fear of judgement: her mentor's reframe was that another person's thought lives in their mind — "how and when did another person's thought become your problem?" If nobody knew about the attempt, most women admit they would be fine with failing.
- The three-layer approach for saying no: positive – negative – positive. Thank the person warmly, place the refusal in the middle ("unfortunately I won't be able to attend"), and close with another positive ("you'll have a great show; I'm always here for anything else") — assertive without appearing rude or "bad."
- Harmful gossip about another woman usually hides jealousy — she has something you want. Malika advises asking yourself that question when gossiping, and instead becoming the person's cheerleader; people doing well themselves rarely gossip harmfully, while light, fun gossip among friends is normal.
- Surround yourself with people on a bigger journey: after she began spending time with career-oriented people with a higher vision, she subconsciously became like them; the company you keep shapes your thinking even through occasional contact.
- Relationships go stale because effort stops after marriage: partners give love in their own love language instead of the spouse's. Of the five love languages — physical touch, words of affirmation, quality time, acts of service, and gifts — her husband's is acts of service (making his coffee, a 10-minute head massage) while hers is gifts; the fix is a heart-to-heart conversation about what each person actually expects.
- Red flags of a controlling marriage: a husband or in-laws dictating what she wears, whom she meets, and what she does, with the elders' preferences imposed over her own desires — she says this is common and direct, not a matter of intuition.
- Start something of your own even if it might fail: her mother dedicated her entire life to her husband and children and now feels her life became meaningless; attempting and failing still builds the confidence to try the next thing.

## Claims to be careful with
- This is a motivational coaching conversation, not medical or psychological treatment advice; the anger, relationship, and confidence techniques are Malika's coaching methods, shared as personal practice rather than evidence-based therapy.
- Outcomes described for her program and clients (for example, a client whose marriage improved and who is now pregnant with a second baby) are anecdotes, not measured results.

## Summary
Women's empowerment coach Malika Ravi Kumar — daughter of director K. S. Ravikumar — describes breaking down after marriage and early motherhood, rebuilding through therapy and mentors, and the framework she now teaches women aged roughly 25–55: discard the "dead rat" of the past, drop the pressure to be a perfect mother, manage anger with a 20-minute isolation rule, pursue financial independence without guilt, say no using a positive–negative–positive structure, recognise jealousy behind gossip, and revive relationships by learning a partner's love language.
