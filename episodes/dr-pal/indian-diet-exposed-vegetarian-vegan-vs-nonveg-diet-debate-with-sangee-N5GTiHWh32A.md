## Important tidbits
- Sangeetha Aiyer's four-point program for diabetes and metabolic health: (1) prioritise protein with healthy fat first, (2) choose complex, fibre-rich carbohydrates — non-starchy vegetables and low-glycaemic fruits such as berries, guava, citrus, kiwi, apple and pear, avoiding high-fructose fruits like jackfruit and chikoo, (3) shrink grains to a small part of the day — ideally one meal — replacing them with pulses and legumes, and (4) build muscle, because skeletal muscle is the body's largest glucose-disposal site; if the gym is unrealistic, walk 45 minutes a day, with even a 15-minute stroll after meals measurably lowering glucose.
- The RDA of 0.8 g protein per kg is, in her practice, too low; she targets 1–1.2 g/kg, partly because constantly elevated insulin leaves diabetic patients perpetually hungry, and protein plus fat restores satiety.
- Both speakers' practice data agree that the average Indian vegetarian eats only about 30–40 g of protein a day (her sample of ~600 vegetarians: ~30 g; Dr Pal's ~1,000 patients: ~40 g) against a need of roughly 70 g — while looking deceptively normal at a BMI around 23.5 (the Indian BMI cut-off should be ~23, not the Western 24.9).
- The central misconception she corrects: dal, chana and rajma are not "protein" — they are complex carbohydrates with protein. Class 1 proteins (meat, eggs, seafood, dairy) have a complete amino-acid profile in the right balance; plant proteins are incomplete and carry a carb-to-protein ratio of about 3:1 or 4:1 (100 g raw moong gives ~22–24 g protein but 70+ g carbs), whereas an ideal protein source has carbs at 0.5–1× its protein (egg, chicken, fish and paneer have essentially none).
- Hitting a 70 g protein target on a whole-food plant-based diet can push intake to 2,200–2,500 kcal, which is why even vegan bodybuilders supplement with whey, pea or soy protein isolates — more protein, more bioavailable, in fewer calories.
- For lacto-vegetarians her non-negotiables are one or two daily primary proteins: paneer, hung curd (curd drained in muslin for 1–2 hours), Greek yogurt (10–12 g protein per serving; always unflavoured — flavoured versions add sugar, so add real berries instead), whey or plant protein isolate (start with half a scoop on alternate days), and fermented soy — tofu and tempeh (tempeh ~19 g protein) digest far better than unfermented soy. She explicitly warns against soya chunks and soy chaap, the latter being vegetable oil mixed with maida — "junk" marketed as a tandoori-chicken equivalent. A paneer–tofu mix cuts fat while raising protein and improving texture.
- Legumes are demoted to "support protein" partly because of SIBO: slow small-intestinal motility lets colonic bacteria (normally ~100 trillion in the colon vs only ~1 million in the small intestine) back up and ferment carbohydrates, producing the bloating people blame on dal. This is also why the low-FODMAP diet — which temporarily removes lentils and legumes — treats IBS, whose classic pattern is eat, cramp, rush to the bathroom, feel better.
- Dose ramps matter for everything: protein targets should climb over weeks (1.0 → 1.3 → 1.4 g/kg), fibre should rise slowly from ~5 g toward 30 g/day, and eggs from one to several a day gradually; expect a minimum of six weeks, realistically three months, for the gut to adapt — "people want a one-month trial; the damage didn't happen overnight."
- The protein-leverage idea (Raubenheimer and Simpson) explains a common early complaint: on adequate protein (1–1.1 g/kg) people report "I'm not hungry, am I eating enough?" — that is a good sign, because humans keep eating until their protein requirement is met, and low-protein diets drive overeating of carbs and fat.
- Her toughest Indian re-education: carbohydrates are used as a "delivery vehicle" — sabzi needs a roti, dal needs rice, chole needs a puri. Her plate rule flips the hero: no more than ~80 g cooked rice or one roti, at least two sabzis, a 300 g/day vegetable target, and a simple per-meal goal of 20 g protein plus 10 g fibre (60 g protein and 30 g fibre across three meals). A typical vegetarian day she audits has 30–40 g protein but nearly 300 g of carbohydrate once biscuits and sweets are counted — "energy toxicity."
- On fats: high LDL is driven by fried and ultra-processed food, not by one or two spoons of ghee. Total added fat should be about 2 litres a month for a family of four — 500 ml per person, roughly 1.5 tablespoons per meal. Rotate traditional oils (sesame, peanut, mustard, coconut, ghee) as regional cooking always did; the real dangers are restaurant deep-frying in repeatedly reheated oil and daily app-delivered food — she advises eating out less, never ordering deep-fried dishes out, and having fried food about once a month at home in fresh oil.
- The omega-3 to omega-6 ratio should ideally be 1:2 (1:4 tolerable) but now runs 1:22 to 1:30 in some populations, which she links to inflammation; industrially processed seed oils (soybean, canola, rice-bran, sunflower, safflower) dominate commercial kitchens because they are cheap with long shelf life. Flaxseed ALA converts poorly, so vegetarians who don't eat fish may need an omega-3 supplement. Nuts and seeds: a fistful of nuts a day or 1–2 tablespoons of seed mix sprinkled on vegetables or yogurt — but beware nuts-and-jaggery laddus, which are calorie-dense marketing, not health food.
- Fasting practicalities: time-restricted eating (12 hours progressing to 16) rests insulin overnight. Because dinner is India's family and social meal, she extends the morning fast instead, using black coffee, diluted salted buttermilk, salted lime soda (for electrolyte-withdrawal headaches) or ghee/butter coffee — fat barely stimulates insulin. Progress counts: going from a 12- to a 13-hour fast is success, not failure. The 4–6 pm "witching hour" is when low-protein days collapse into junk snacking; ~30 g protein in each of the first two meals prevents it.
- Dr Pal's own prescription for young GI patients without red flags is deliberately drug-free: finish the last meal by 7 pm (he says 7 knowing patients land at 9), sleep seven hours, and never sleep within three hours of finishing eating — the fast clock starts only after you finish and wash your hands, since digestion keeps insulin active for roughly 2–3 hours after the last bite. He also notes the 4 pm "hunger" is often thirst — a water bottle on the desk fixed his own.

## Claims to be careful with
- The omega-3:omega-6 ratio figures (ideal 1:2, current 1:22–1:30) and the framing of seed oils as a co-factor in insulin resistance and obesity are presented confidently, but this is a contested area of nutrition science, not settled consensus.
- Protein leverage is described as "proven" as a driver of obesity; it remains a hypothesis with supportive but not conclusive evidence.
- The SIBO explanation (billions of bacteria found in small-bowel aspirates) reflects the speakers' clinical impressions; routine SIBO testing and its thresholds are themselves debated.
- The suggestion that ghee/butter coffee or buttermilk "doesn't break the fast" because fat minimally stimulates insulin is a practitioner heuristic, not a universally accepted definition of fasting.
- Mustard oil is described as banned in the US/EU over an acid content (erucic acid) without detail; regulations are more nuanced (it is sold for external use), so treat the blanket statement cautiously.
- Protein and calorie figures for specific foods (moong, tempeh, idli, Greek yogurt brands) are approximations given conversationally and vary by brand and preparation.

## Summary
Nutritionist Sangeetha Aiyer argues the Indian vegetarian diet fails not on intent but on arithmetic: the plate is a mountain of grain delivering only 30–40 g of protein against a ~70 g need, because dal and legumes are really carbohydrates with some protein. Her fix is protein-first eating (paneer, Greek yogurt, eggs, whey or fermented soy as non-negotiable primaries, legumes as support), grains shrunk to one small portion, 20 g protein and 10 g fibre per meal, about 1.5 tablespoons of added fat per meal from rotated traditional oils, and pragmatic fasting built around Indian work and family life — all ramped gradually over six weeks to three months so the gut, and the person, can adapt.
