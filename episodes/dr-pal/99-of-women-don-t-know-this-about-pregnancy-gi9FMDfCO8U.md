## Important tidbits
- Dr Vasan says roughly 90–95% of conceptions end in a live baby; about 5% end in stillbirth or intrauterine death. After 28 weeks, the key home check is the daily fetal movement count (DFMC) — if you notice no baby movement for about two hours, go in immediately rather than waiting.
- In late pregnancy the baby should gain about 200 g per week; she describes an IUGR case where the baby gained only 100 g in four weeks (1.5 kg to 1.6 kg), and an emergency caesarean at ~36 weeks after the amniotic fluid dried up saved the baby — a day's delay could have meant stillbirth.
- More than three pregnancy losses is "recurrent pregnancy loss" and needs tertiary, high-risk care with close supervision. The most common cause she sees is antiphospholipid antibody syndrome (an autoimmune condition); other causes include high thyroid levels, infections such as toxoplasmosis and syphilis, consanguinity — and a large chunk remains unexplained even after all tests.
- On consanguineous marriage (marrying within the family — second-degree, e.g. a mother's brother, is most common in her Chennai practice): recessive genes from both sides can combine to cause structural/chromosomal anomalies and recurrent abortions; Dr Pal shares that his own family history and four losses with his wife were traced to exactly such a shared genetic defect.
- A caesarean is done for the safety of mother and baby, not for money — she says the doctor's charge is the same either way. Four things must go right for a normal delivery: the cervix opens to 10 cm, the head descends on its own, the baby's heartbeat stays good, and the baby does not pass meconium (which can cause meconium aspiration and days on a ventilator). After a caesarean today, mothers eat within 6 hours, walk within 12 hours, and go home in 24–48 hours.
- Forced or prolonged vaginal delivery carries real risks she lists: episiotomy (a 3–4 cm cut) can extend into the anal sphincter, heavy postpartum haemorrhage can follow, and babies — especially macrosomic babies over 4 kg born to diabetic mothers — can suffer Erb's palsy or clavicle injury; a second stage delayed beyond 1.5–2 hours risks cerebral palsy from reduced blood supply to the baby's brain.
- The i-Pill (emergency contraceptive) is a progesterone pill, not a contraception method: repeated use causes withdrawal bleeding every few days, destroys track of the real menstrual cycle, and is not 100% effective. She says it is banned/not available in Tamil Nadu and that she does not prescribe it.
- Medical termination with tablets is only for under 7 weeks (within 49 days of the last period), and only after a scan confirms the pregnancy is inside the uterus — taking the tablets with an undiagnosed ectopic (tubal) pregnancy can rupture the tube and be life-threatening. From 7 to 12 weeks, suction evacuation is preferred over curettage, which can scar the cavity (Asherman's syndrome) or shorten the cervix; terminations should only be done in certified centres. She estimates unwanted-pregnancy terminations in her practice have risen from ~10% to 25–30% of patients over ten years.
- Stop both smoking and alcohol three months before trying to conceive; no amount of alcohol is safe in pregnancy. Alcohol can cause growth-restricted babies (under 2.5 kg) with low immunity and long NICU stays — a fully modifiable risk factor.
- Sleep and rest: 8–10 hours of sleep at night plus about 2 hours lying down in the afternoon. After 20 weeks, sleep on the left side (she says ~90% left) so the growing uterus does not compress the aorta and inferior vena cava against the spine and reduce blood flow to the baby; the right side is still better than lying flat on the back, and you do not need to sit up every time you change position.
- Myths busted: a fetal heart rate anywhere between 120 and 160 is normal and does not indicate the baby's sex, and neither does the shape of the abdomen.
- Her single biggest nutrition advice for pregnancy is protein, because the typical South Indian day (idli, rice, dosa) is almost all carbohydrate — vegetarians can use soya chunks; non-vegetarians fish, egg and chicken; plus vitamin C-rich and iron-rich foods.
- Folic acid should be started 3 months before conception and continued through the first 3 months (till 12 weeks) to prevent neural tube defects; avoid prenatal vitamins that contain vitamin A, which is contraindicated in pregnancy. Iron tablets given later also contain folic acid, so it effectively continues through the pregnancy.
- Endometriosis, she says, is not curable and is not caused by diet or lifestyle — she calls it a chromosomal problem/destiny. Warning signs: severe period pain starting 2–3 days before the period and lasting 4–5 days after, and heavy bleeding. Retrograde blood flow can form ovarian cysts (endometriomas) and progress to "kissing ovaries" and a frozen pelvis at grade 4, so early identification matters.
- What is normal for periods: a cycle of 24–35 days, bleeding of 3–5 days, about 3–4 pads a day, with first-day pain being normal. See a gynaecologist if you soak more than 6–7 pads a day, pass clots, bleed beyond 6–7 days, or have pain before/after the period. Thyroid only disturbs periods when it is significantly abnormal — a TSH of around 10 or above.
- After delivery, start Kegel exercises within a week — you can do them while feeding the baby or watching TV — and note that a breastfeeding mother needs only about 400–500 extra calories, not the ghee-heavy overfeeding families often impose. For post-delivery problems (loose abdomen, stress urinary incontinence, prolapse), cosmetic gynaecology options range from a magnetic Kegel chair and laser tightening to tummy tuck and labial fat augmentation.

## Claims to be careful with
- The description of endometriosis as a "chromosomal problem" and "destiny," and the statement that it is worse than cancer because cancer is curable, are the guest's characterisations; the causes of endometriosis are not fully established.
- The claim that the i-Pill is "banned" in Tamil Nadu is stated by the guest and was not independently verified in the episode.
- Robotic hysterectomy benefits are described in near-absolute terms by the surgeon who performs them — blood loss "almost zero" (under 10 mL), near-zero damage to neighbouring structures, operating even at a haemoglobin of 7 without transfusion, and walking 12 hours after surgery. Individual results will vary.
- Practice-based figures (90–95% live-birth rate, terminations rising from ~10% to 25–30%, the doctor's fee being identical for normal delivery and caesarean) are Dr Vasan's own estimates from her practice, not published statistics.
- Vaginal PRP injections for tightening and "G-spot enhancement" (the "O-shot") are presented as established offerings; evidence for these procedures is limited.

## Summary
Obstetrician Dr Sandhya Vasan of SIMS Hospital, Chennai, walks through what women rarely hear about pregnancy: why most conceptions succeed but some end in stillbirth or recurrent loss (autoimmune conditions, consanguinity, thyroid), why caesareans are a safety decision rather than a money decision, the strict rules around the i-Pill and medical termination, preconception folic acid and protein, left-side sleeping, endometriosis red flags, and the growing menu of post-delivery cosmetic gynaecology — closing with her own routine of daily Pilates, dumbbells, fish and an 11 am–9 pm eating window.
