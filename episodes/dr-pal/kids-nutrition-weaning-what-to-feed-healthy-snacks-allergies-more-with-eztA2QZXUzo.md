## Important tidbits
- Exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months — not even water, because breast milk is about 88% water and covers hydration; if the baby needs more fluid, hydrate the mother and feed more often. Breastfeeding can continue for 2 years and beyond, per Dr Nithya (citing AAP guidance).
- Expressed breast milk keeps 4–6 hours at room temperature, 24 hours in the refrigerator, and 3 months frozen. Donor breast-milk banks screen mothers for infections including HIV and pasteurize the milk before giving it to preterm/NICU babies.
- First weaning food: a rice-based porridge with roasted gram dal for protein, because rice is digested well by most babies. A homemade porridge powder stores for about 21 days; start once a day, move to twice a day after 3–4 days, and introduce one new food every 3–4 days. Mixing in a little expressed breast milk helps a baby accept the new taste.
- Under 1 year of age, do NOT give salt, sugar, honey, nuts, or cow's milk. Eggs and non-vegetarian food can start around 9 months; by the end of the 7th month babies can have idli and rice-dal-vegetables with a little ghee or coconut oil.
- Milk limit: beyond 1 year, a child needs no more than about 400 ml of milk per day, per Dr Nithya — excess milk fills the stomach, kills hunger for meals, and creates a milk-instead-of-food loop (she has seen children drinking 2 litres a day). Protein and calcium can come from pulses (chana, rajma), egg, ragi, and non-veg food instead.
- Jaggery, brown sugar, palm sugar (panangkalkandu), and honey spike blood sugar just like white sugar — "millet biscuits" and jaggery snacks are not healthy free passes. Moderation in practice: something like one packet of chips once in two weeks, and take 2 biscuits out of the packet rather than handing over the whole packet.
- Balanced-plate rule for every meal: one carbohydrate, one protein, one vegetable, one fruit (fruit can double as dessert). Start family meals and self-feeding around 9–10 months, when the child can sit independently and has hand-to-mouth coordination; keep mealtimes happy and never force-feed — force-feeding makes children detest mealtimes and damages their relationship with food.
- The first 1,000 days (pregnancy plus the first two years) decide growth and brain development; per research cited by Dr Nithya, deficiencies of iron, iodine, zinc, and selenium in this window are associated with cognitive development lower by around 10 points.
- Healthy homemade snacks she recommends: yogurt bark (thick/strained yogurt with a teaspoon of honey and fruit/nuts, frozen 5–6 hours and broken into pieces), date-and-nut balls (keep 1 week to 10 days), homemade granola (oats, nuts, seeds, a little honey, baked or dry-roasted), and chana cutlets with sweet potato.
- Homemade peanut butter takes about 5 minutes (peanuts, a little jaggery, a little flavorless oil, blended until the fat releases); packaged nut butters carry extra sodium, sugar, and preservatives and should be an indulgence, not a daily breakfast. Sugary breakfasts (jam, Nutella-type spreads) make children hyperactive and unable to sit and concentrate in school, she says.
- For picky eaters, hide vegetables in attractive formats: mini coin-sized uttapams instead of one large one, broccoli rolled into an omelette, beetroot chapati stuffed with paneer given a fun name like "red velvet cheese paratha" — and parents must eat healthy themselves as an example.
- Anaphylaxis is the severe end of food allergy: rash plus swelling of the face and neck, a change in the child's voice (swelling of the vocal cords), and breathing difficulty that can be fatal because children's airways are narrow. Reactions get worse with each re-exposure, so a food a child has reacted to must never be "tried again"; introduce allergy-prone foods during the daytime when help is available.
- No honey before 1 year because honey can carry Clostridium botulinum spores, and an infant's immature gut cannot handle them (infant botulism).
- Choking hazards: whole grapes, whole nuts, and suck-out jelly cups have all caused airway emergencies in her practice; cut finger foods to under half an inch, and never let children eat while watching TV or distracted.
- Vitamin D deficiency is extremely common — 8 to 9 of the 10 children she tests have low levels, mainly from lack of outdoor play — but she does not routinely test asymptomatic, well children; she supplements when levels are found low.
- Eating disorders are rising in Indian adolescents: mostly anorexia-type restriction in girls and muscle-building obsession in boys. Warning signs include unhappiness around food, postponing meals, lunch boxes coming back uneaten, falling immunity, poor concentration, and moodiness. For toddler tantrums, her advice is simply to ignore them — giving in teaches the child that tantrums work.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Pal cites research in conversation suggesting that a vegetarian, complex-carb-rich diet for roughly the first 8–10 years lowers later disease risk — presented as a study finding without a named source, not an established feeding guideline.
- The link between sugary breakfasts and hyperactivity/inattention is asserted from clinical experience and anecdote rather than cited trial data.
- The "around 10 points" cognitive impact of micronutrient deficiency in the first 1,000 days is quoted from research without the study being named.

## Summary
Pediatrician and MasterChef runner-up Dr Nithya Franklin walks through feeding children from birth to adolescence: exclusive breastfeeding for 6 months (no water needed), rice-porridge weaning with strict no-salt/sugar/honey/nuts/cow's-milk rules under age one, a 400 ml/day milk cap after age one, balanced plates eaten as a family from about 9–10 months, homemade snack alternatives to packaged junk, and clear safety guidance on anaphylaxis, infant botulism from honey, and choking hazards.
