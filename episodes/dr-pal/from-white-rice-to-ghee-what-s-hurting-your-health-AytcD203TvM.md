## Important tidbits
- Dr Sesikeran says most Indians reach only 50–70% of recommended protein intake: roughly 250 g of cereals a day provides ~20 g protein; pulses are recommended at 80 g/day (~20–25 g protein) but actual intake is barely 40 g; total daily protein is typically 40–50 g at best and often just 25–30 g, which matters because Indians carry relatively higher body fat and less muscle.
- Milk numbers he cites: India's per-capita milk availability is ~450 ml/day; the recommended intake is ~300 ml/day as milk, curd or other dairy, giving 10–12 g of good-quality protein — but actual intake, even among milk producers, is barely 100–120 ml.
- Soy is a complete vegetarian protein, "nearest to milk": its PDCAAS is ~0.95 versus milk's 1.0, while most other legumes score ~0.7–0.8. Soy's isoflavones (genistein, daidzein) look like estrogen but bind the ER2 receptor, not ER1 — he says they do not cause the feared effects on puberty or breast cancer and may actually help bone formation in menopausal women; the active compound equol is produced by gut bacteria, which is why benefits show more in East Asian than Western populations. Soy must be properly soaked/cooked/fermented to remove anti-nutrients, and normal intake levels are unlikely to affect thyroid function.
- Protein requirement: 0.83 g per kg body weight covers ~99% of people with sedentary-to-moderate activity (half the population manages on ~0.6 g/kg); 1–1.2 g/kg is for the very active and up to 2 g/kg only for intensive training. Extra protein without activity is largely excreted via urine, stresses the kidney over the long term, and adds 4 kcal/g — though high-protein eating also tends to displace carbohydrate calories through satiety.
- Plant protein is less digestible (~70% for an exclusively plant eater) because protein is bound up with fibre; fix it by eating ~30% more or by processing (soaking, steaming, pressure-cooking), and combine cereals with pulses so the amino acids complement each other. Diversity of plant sources is his stated solution.
- Tofu vs paneer: he would choose tofu as a vegetarian — paneer carries substantial fat and no fibre, while tofu is almost entirely vegetable protein with some fibre.
- On milk and inflammation: the A1/A2 casein issue is largely irrelevant in India because native Indian cows and all buffaloes produce A2 milk (only Jersey crossbreeds produce A1); fermented forms (curd, yogurt, buttermilk) have no lactose and more digestible protein. He calls "A2 ghee" marketing false — ghee is pure fat with no protein, so A1/A2 does not apply. Buffalo milk is fine scientifically, just higher in fat and calories than cow's milk.
- Ghee and fats: total visible oils/fats should be ~25 g/day, split roughly in thirds across saturated, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated — i.e. about 8–10 g of saturated fat a day. Ghee in small quantity (half to one teaspoon) does no harm and saturated fat is genuinely needed (brain lipids, cell membranes); the real problem is bingeing via ghee-laden sweets and festive foods, not moderate home cooking.
- Fibre: minimum 25 g/day (Dr Pal personally targets 38 g). Insoluble fibre (cellulose/lignin) absorbs water, bulks stool and prevents constipation; soluble fibre forms a gel that slows glucose and cholesterol absorption, lowers glycemic impact, and feeds gut bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids maintaining the gut lining (preventing "leaky gut" and inflammation). Natural foods contain both.
- White rice: polished rice became common only after the 1960s; polishing removes the bran — the fibre and micronutrients — leaving essentially starch that converts to glucose quickly. His swap: 90 g whole wheat atta makes ~3 chapatis with ~7.5–9 g fibre (about a third of the daily need), versus only ~2 g fibre in 90–100 g cooked white rice. He endorses replacing one rice meal (or part of it) with chapatis a few times a week; dietary guidelines say at least 50% of cereals should be whole grains. Maida has no fibre and does not count.
- "Healthy" packaged foods: a millet biscuit built on maida and sugar is not a healthy option — read the label for added-sugar percentage and actual millet/whole-wheat content. "Diabetic-friendly" sweets are a portion trap: people eat 4–5 "good" biscuits or 2–3 sugar-free gulab jamuns where they would have eaten 1–2 ordinary ones, while the fat and maida remain.
- Fruit: choose by the fibre-to-sugar ratio, not by fruit being "good" or "bad" — guava and papaya are high-fibre/lower-sugar and guava is his top pick for diabetics; banana and sapota are higher-sugar/lower-fibre (banana still has potassium value, so limit rather than ban). Watermelon has a high glycemic index but a low glycemic load because it is ~95% water. Eat fruit whole, never as juice.
- Micronutrients: iron deficiency worsened after COVID (prevalence rising from ~50% to ~60%); only about one-third of India's anaemia is iron deficiency — the rest involves protein, B12 and folate deficiency, so iron alone won't convert to haemoglobin without the full package (the Anemia Mukt Bharat approach). Fortified rice kernels are blended 1:99 with ordinary rice to deliver ~1/3 of daily iron needs; oils are fortified with vitamins A and D. He says 80–90% of Indians are vitamin D deficient — get levels checked and supplement; B12 comes only from animal/dairy sources for vegetarians.
- Context for the diabetes epidemic: Indians get ~62% of calories from carbohydrates; when he trained in Chennai, diabetes prevalence was 1.5–2%. Jaggery is a myth-bust: it gives the same glucose load as sugar, and "no sugar, only jaggery" products make no real difference. Sedentary life is stark — an awake resting person burns only ~1.2x their sleeping calorie rate.
- His closing advice: read food labels; return to traditional, region-specific foods; prefer home-cooked food; stay physically active at any level (even movement inside the house for the elderly) and avoid sitting for long periods.

## Claims to be careful with
- The figures he quotes (e.g. iron deficiency rising to ~60% after COVID, 80–90% vitamin D deficiency, per-capita milk availability of ~450 ml) are presented from his experience and memory in conversation, not cited to specific reports in the episode.
- His statement that soy isoflavones have a shown *beneficial* effect in breast cancer reflects his reading of the evidence; listeners with hormone-sensitive conditions should not take it as personal medical guidance.

## Summary
Former NIN director Dr B. Sesikeran walks Dr Pal through India's everyday nutrition mistakes with numbers: protein intake at barely half to two-thirds of recommendations, polished white rice (a post-1960s habit) stripped of fibre, ghee feared or worshipped when the real rule is ~8–10 g of saturated fat a day, jaggery mistaken for a safe sugar, and "millet" or "diabetic-friendly" packaged foods that are still maida and sugar. His fixes are unglamorous — combine cereals with pulses, swap some rice meals for whole-wheat chapatis, choose high-fibre fruits like guava, read labels, fortify and supplement vitamin D and iron properly, and move more.
