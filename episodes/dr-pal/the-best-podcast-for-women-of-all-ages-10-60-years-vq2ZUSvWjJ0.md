## Important tidbits
- A normal menstrual cycle can range from 22 to 35 days with flow of 2 to 7 days; it only becomes abnormal if your cycle length varies by more than about 8 days from your own usual pattern, if pain needs 3–4 painkillers a day, or if there is bleeding between periods, after intercourse, or after menopause.
- Periods are controlled from the brain via the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian (HPO) axis, which is why stress, poor sleep and exam pressure disturb cycles before anything else.
- Dr Kumar says PCOS now fills half her outpatient practice (with endometriosis) and cites statistics that 1 in every 3 to 5 women globally has some manifestation of PCOS; she frames insulin resistance — not the reproductive hormones themselves — as the root driver.
- In PCOS, many small follicles develop but none matures into a dominant follicle, so ovulation fails; she lists insulin resistance, high cortisol, low melatonin, obesity, poor diet, hypothyroidism and autoimmune factors as causes of anovulation.
- She links inadequate, non-circadian sleep to PCOS and notes many of her PCOS patients also have hypothyroidism or high prolactin, again pointing to the brain/pituitary level.
- Her first diet step is measurement, not change: patients weigh their food for a week and often discover they eat ~300 g of carbohydrate a day; a roti carries roughly 8–20 g of carbs and a dosa 18–20 g, and she says insulin resistance cannot handle ~300 g — around 150 g or less is the target zone.
- Infertility is defined as no pregnancy after one year of unprotected intercourse (quoted incidence ~17–18%); causes split roughly 40% female, 40% male, 10% both partners and 10% unexplained, so semen analysis (count, motility, morphology, and DNA fragmentation index) matters as much as testing the woman.
- Age is the single most important variable for getting pregnant, she says — the easiest pregnancies are in the early 20s; a woman is born with millions of eggs, has roughly 3 lakh at puberty and perhaps 40,000–50,000 by her mid-20s, with wastage every cycle.
- Ovarian reserve can be checked with an AMH blood test, a day-2 FSH, and a day-3 ultrasound antral follicle count (about 7–8 follicles per ovary is average); she advises women delaying pregnancy to test AMH around age 25–26 and consider egg freezing early, aiming to store 10–15 good-quality eggs.
- Embryo freezing gives better pregnancy rates than egg freezing, and a frozen egg's chance of converting to an embryo is only about 40–50%; she also notes 10–15% of all pregnancies end in miscarriage, so "pregnancy rate" differs from "take-home baby rate."
- Smoking (and vaping) is, in her words, one of the major reasons for premature ovarian insufficiency and also lowers sperm count and motility and contributes to erectile dysfunction — if a patient quits only one thing, she says quit smoking.
- Menopause before age 40 is premature; the timing otherwise varies genetically (roughly 42 to 58). After menopause the two big risks are osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease because estrogen no longer protects bone and heart — she prescribes strength training plus vitamin D and calcium, and herself does 3 days of strength training and 4 days of yoga near age 60.
- Cervical cancer screening: Pap smear from age 21, every 3 years (every 5 years if combined with HPV testing); the HPV vaccine is recommended from age 9, is best given at 9–26 years, is a "rescue" option at 26–45, and is of little use beyond 45.
- Birth-control pills do not cure PCOS, though they can be used short-term for control; there is no single "best" contraceptive, and she argues vasectomy — a small outpatient procedure — is underused in India compared with the much bigger tubal-ligation surgery women undergo.
- PMS and PMDD are real medical conditions driven by hormonal effects on brain chemistry, not something women "make up"; severe cases need medical treatment.

## Claims to be careful with
- Her statement that if PCOS patients cut carbs to ~100 g/day for 6 months to a year "we can close all the IVF centers" is rhetorical emphasis, not a literal prediction.
- Her figures that ~80% of PCOS patients have associated hypothyroidism and ~50% have high prolactin are her clinical-practice estimates and are higher than typical published rates.
- Her remark that birth-control pills slightly increase "pre-cancer" risk is a simplified framing; she herself stresses the pills are safe when prescribed by a doctor.
- The strong causal weight she places on smoking for premature ovarian insufficiency and on lifestyle alone for reversing PCOS reflects her clinical experience; individual cases vary.

## Summary
Gynaecologist Dr Anjali Kumar walks through women's health from puberty to menopause: what a normal cycle looks like, how the brain-driven HPO axis and insulin resistance produce PCOS and anovulation, why sleep, stress and carbohydrate load matter, how ovarian reserve declines with age and when to test AMH or freeze eggs, why smoking damages fertility in both partners, and why strength training, cervical screening and HPV vaccination anchor prevention at every age.
