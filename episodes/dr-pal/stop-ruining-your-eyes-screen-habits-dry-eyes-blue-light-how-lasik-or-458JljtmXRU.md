## Important tidbits
- Dr Sachdev calls the post-Covid surge a "myopia epidemic" and says it is 100% related to screen time. Apart from heredity, he names two proven causative factors: excessive near work — the ciliary muscles that change the lens shape go into overaction, straining the eye and lengthening the eyeball — and lack of sunlight exposure, which reduces dopamine secretion that normally restrains eyeball elongation.
- Scale he cites: in Taiwan, Korea, Hong Kong, Singapore and China about 70% of young adults need minus glasses; in India the share needing minus glasses is rising by about 1–2% every year. Singapore has made a minimum of 1 hour of outdoor activity per day mandatory in schools, he says.
- Myopia progression is mainly a childhood problem because the eye is still growing: it typically stabilises by about age 20–21 in boys and 18–19 in girls, though he has seen new myopia develop in the mid-30s with computer-heavy occupations.
- Early signs in a child: falling school performance (cannot see the board from the back bench), irritability, headaches, squinting/screwing up the eyes to see, and sitting very close to the television. Parents can screen at home with an online Snellen chart sized to the screen, testing each eye separately — and he warns that a child who needs glasses but does not get them strains more, so myopia progresses faster.
- His examination schedule: an eye exam at birth (for congenital problems such as a blocked nasolacrimal duct), again after one year, before starting school, and then every year. The first refraction in a child should be cycloplegic — drops relax the ciliary muscle, because a child's strong accommodation produces false myopia on computerised machines — and a myopic child needs a dilated retinal examination; keratoconus can appear at puberty.
- Screen rule he prescribes — the 20-20-20 rule: after 20 minutes of screen time, look at something 20 feet away, rest about 20 seconds, and take about 20 blinks. He explains that screen users do partial blinks, which causes scratchy, irritable, red eyes, and that pixelated images add focusing strain. Keeping a screen about one hand/arm distance away is fine, he says.
- On blue light, he says blue-light filter glasses are "questionable": they do filter blue and ultraviolet light, do no harm, and blue light before sleep disturbs the circadian rhythm, but it is not an established fact that the glasses are very helpful. UV protection matters most outdoors — including light reflected off snow or water — because UV exposure is associated with age-related macular degeneration (ARMD), typically after 60; the wet type causes sudden vision loss and distorted shapes (a square looking oval).
- Diet facts vs fiction: vitamin A deficiency (which caused keratomalacia, the cornea melting) and trachoma have declined significantly in India. Vitamin A, vitamin D and omega-3 fatty acids, green leafy vegetables and carrots support eye health and tear secretion — but he dismisses the belief that one carrot a day removes the need for anything else: it is "at best a supplement." Smoking, he says, is very bad for the eye (optic nerve damage, retinal problems, diabetic retinopathy), as are uncontrolled blood pressure, lipids and diabetes.
- Dry eye is "galloping," he says, driven by air pollution (particulate matter breaks the tear film and shortens tear break-up time) plus partial blinking on screens. Paradoxically, dry eye presents with watery eyes — reflex tearing because the lid moves over an unlubricated surface. See a doctor promptly if there is purulent/mucoid (pus-like) discharge, vision is affected, light intolerance (photophobia) develops, or redness persists.
- Lubricant eye drops are safe over-the-counter products worldwide and can be used two to three times a day like artificial tears. But he warns never to self-use steroid drops: a steroid bottle or pack carries a red line/circle — "that is like danger" — and steroids should never be bought without a prescription. Overuse of antibiotic drops (the same drugs as oral antibiotics, e.g. ciprofloxacin, erythromycin) is breeding resistance, he says.
- Why everyone needs a yearly exam even without symptoms or glasses: silent diseases. Glaucoma ("kala motia") — blocked drainage of the aqueous fluid raises eye pressure and slowly atrophies the optic nerve, causing irreversible blindness. Diabetic retinopathy — fragile new retinal vessels leak and bleed, and many people learn they have diabetes only years late. And a changing glasses number may actually be a developing cataract, which an optical shop will miss.
- On ageing: cataract is the lens losing transparency as its proteins denature — given a long enough life, everyone develops one, and no homeopathy, Ayurveda or home remedy stops it, he says. Indians develop cataracts about 10 years earlier than Western populations, possibly from genetics, childhood diarrhoea/dehydration episodes, nutrition and greater sunlight exposure. Presbyopia is universal after about 42–45 (lens elasticity falls; first sign is holding reading material farther away, worse in dim light or when tired) and cannot be prevented, only possibly delayed by healthy diet and exercise.
- Modern cataract surgery, as he describes it: phacoemulsification (pioneered by Charles Kelman in the 1960s) breaks the lens with an ultrasonic needle through a 2–3 mm incision — versus the old 20–22 mm incision and thick "+10 soda-water" glasses — and is now stitchless, done under topical (drop) anaesthesia only, with no injection and no bandage: a walk-in, walk-out surgery. Femto laser-assisted cataract surgery (FLACS) uses a femtosecond laser (fired for a quadrillionth of a second, ~1-micron spot) for precise, computer-controlled steps; he says 80–90% of his patients choose it, cost being the only barrier, and he would choose it for himself.
- Do not wait for a cataract to "mature," he says: a mature cataract turns amber to brown to black, gets hard like a betel nut, needs far more energy to break, and causes more collateral damage. The modern indication for surgery is simply when daily activities are hampered. The implanted intraocular lens (IOL) can also remove most glasses power, making the surgery refractive as well as restorative. IOL choices: monofocal (single focus; reading glasses still needed), toric (corrects cylindrical power), trifocal (distance, intermediate and near; possible glare/halos at night), and EDOF — extended depth of focus — lenses giving a continuous range of vision.
- LASIK (Laser-Assisted In Situ Keratomileusis) reshapes the cornea permanently with an excimer laser: flattening the centre corrects myopia, an oval pattern corrects astigmatism, and steepening the centre corrects hyperopia. A hinged corneal flap is made first — originally with a blade (microkeratome), now with a femtosecond laser (bladeless/femto-LASIK) — then the laser reshapes the tissue under the flap, so no stitches are needed. He notes the excimer laser came from Srinivasan's work at IBM on etching computer chips.
- Flapless lenticule extraction — SMILE, SILK, CLEAR and ATOS — uses the femtosecond laser to cut a thin slice (lenticule) inside the cornea, removed through a small incision without burning tissue or making a flap; he says his team performed the first SILK procedure globally. Surface ablation (SmartSurf) lasers the corneal surface directly, preserving flap thickness for thin corneas, but involves pain and watering until the epithelium regrows under a bandage contact lens.
- Alternatives when the cornea is too weak or thin for laser: an implantable contact lens (ICL) placed inside the eye on top of the natural lens (available in minus, plus and cylindrical powers), and refractive/presbyopic lens exchange — removing the natural lens even without cataract and replacing it with an EDOF-type lens. Presbyopia itself can be treated with PresbyMAX/Presbyond LASIK, which induces spherical aberration plus blended monovision (dominant eye corrected for distance, the other left about −1 for near).
- Contact lenses are a medical device, not a store-bought accessory, he stresses: do not sleep in them, reduce wearing time, prefer daily disposables, and rub and clean lenses properly because a biofilm of tears and protein forms on them and harbours infection that can cause corneal ulcers; even lens solutions have caused infection clusters when contaminated.
- His closing advice on choosing surgery: do not fit a procedure to the eye — the procedure must be chosen for the eye after corneal-strength testing, ideally at a centre that has all the technologies (including wavefront-guided treatment and high-speed eye trackers) and can manage complications. His last message: "Eyes are the most wonderful gift of God. Preserve them, look after them."

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Sachdev is Chairman of Centre for Sight and describes his own centre's technologies in strongly promotional terms — femto cataract outcomes with "no ifs and buts," being global first with SILK, and "supervision" claims — a commercial interest to keep in mind; other surgeons may reasonably offer standard phaco or other platforms.
- His suggestion that blue-light exposure is expediting age-related macular degeneration, and that sunlight/dopamine deficiency elongates the eyeball, are presented as mechanisms with varying strength of evidence; he himself calls blue-light glasses' benefit unproven.
- The claim that Indians develop cataracts 10 years earlier because of childhood diarrhoea/dehydration is offered as a possible reason "not fully known."
- Statements that healthy diet and exercise can delay presbyopia, and that femto laser surgery is preferable for 80–90% of patients, reflect his clinical preference rather than universal guidance; standard phacoemulsification remains a widely accepted, lower-cost standard.
- The 70% East Asian myopia figure and India's 1–2% annual rise are his cited figures from the episode.

## Summary
Padma Shri ophthalmic surgeon Dr Mahipal Sachdev explains to Dr Pal how screens are ruining eyes: the myopia epidemic in children (near work plus lack of sunlight), the 20-20-20 rule, what blue light does and does not do, dry eyes from pollution and partial blinking, safe versus dangerous eye drops (lubricants fine, steroids never self-used), and the silent diseases a yearly exam catches — glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy and cataract. He then walks through modern solutions in detail: stitchless phaco and femto cataract surgery with monofocal, toric, trifocal and EDOF lenses, and the full menu of glasses-removal procedures — LASIK and femto-LASIK, SMILE/SILK lenticule extraction, surface ablation, ICL and refractive lens exchange — stressing that the procedure must be chosen for the individual eye, not sold from a single machine.
