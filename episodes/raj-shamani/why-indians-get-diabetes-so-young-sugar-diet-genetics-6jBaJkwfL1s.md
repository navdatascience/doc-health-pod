## Important tidbits
- Diagnostic thresholds Dr Mohan gives: normal is fasting glucose under 100 (about 70–100), post-glucose under 140 and HbA1c under 5.6; pre-diabetes is fasting 100–125, post-glucose 140–199 and HbA1c 5.7–6.4; diabetes is fasting 126 or above, post-glucose/random 200 or above and HbA1c 6.5 or above. He stresses diabetes is not one disease — there are 50–60 types — and that type matters, e.g. a C-peptide test showing zero insulin secretion points to Type 1.
- Indians progress faster: he says at least 40% of Indians diagnosed with pre-diabetes develop full diabetes within 8 years, and the rate is "even higher" — because of genetics from consanguineous marriage (marrying cousins/uncles multiplies the gene), mothers who were malnourished having babies born with a smaller pancreas and fewer beta cells, very high carb intake, and very little exercise.
- "Thin-fat Indians" (TOFI — thin outside, fat inside): Indians have limited subcutaneous fat cells, so overfeeding quickly overflows fat into the abdomen (visceral fat), liver and pancreas, he says — a thin Indian can therefore be at higher diabetes risk than an obese American whose fat is mostly subcutaneous.
- Carb quality has changed, not just quantity: rice mills in India went from 29 in the 1970s to about 3 lakh today, polishing away husk, fibre and protein so modern white rice is "pure starch, like glucose," while physical activity has fallen and ultra-processed, high-sugar/fat/salt food has risen. In his INDIGO / Nature Medicine work he describes cutting carbs by about 10% (from 60–70% of the plate to 50–55%) and raising protein from 8–10% to 18–20%, with that added protein preferably from plant sources (rajma, Bengal gram, green gram, black gram, paneer, milk) because they carry less fat, while noting egg, chicken and fish are acceptable animal proteins and red meat is not good.
- His healthy plate is: half green leafy vegetables, one quarter protein, and only one quarter carbohydrates — the opposite of a typical rice- or chapati-dominated meal. Risk clues for pre-diabetes without a test: family history, being overweight, waist 90 cm or more in men / 80 cm or more in women, and having hypertension, cholesterol/triglyceride problems or fatty liver (metabolic syndrome — the conditions overlap).
- Type 1 diabetes is autoimmune (linked to HLA patterns such as DR3/DR4, possibly triggered by a virus), not lifestyle-caused, and reversal talk applies only to Type 2 — not to Type 1, fibrocalculous pancreatic diabetes (FCPD) or MODY, he says. He mentions AI-driven insulin pumps (about ₹6.5 lakh plus about ₹20,000/month running cost) that sense glucose and dose automatically, with Indian-made versions in development.
- Pre-diabetes reversal is "not very difficult" in his protocol: lose about 5 kg — especially about 2 inches off the tummy — cut carbs, add protein, do regular aerobic plus muscle training (muscle is where glucose is burnt), sleep better and on time, don't eat late, and walk after meals; people usually fail because they do it for 2–3 months and give up.
- His ABCD rule for a complications-free life with diabetes: A — HbA1c below 7%; B — blood pressure below 140/90, preferably 130/80; C — LDL cholesterol below 100 (below 70, even below 50, is better); D — Discipline: diet, exercise, sleep, check-ups and no smoking. Each 1% rise in HbA1c above 7 adds about 25% risk of eye, kidney and nerve complications — 7–8 is 25%, 8–9 is 50%, 9–10 is 75%, above 10 is 100% — and each 1% lowering reduces risk by about 25%.
- Kidney damage is staged and early damage is reversible: microalbuminuria (albumin 30–300) can return to normal with tablets and control of sugar and blood pressure, but above 300 (macroalbuminuria) reversal becomes difficult, creatinine rises from a normal ~0.8–0.9 and eGFR falls from ~90 toward end-stage disease, where the only options are dialysis, transplant or death — he cites 101 million people with diabetes in India versus only about 10,000 kidney transplants a year.
- A diabetic wound fails to heal for three reasons, he says: glucose in tissue feeds bacterial growth, neuropathy means pressure is not felt so damage and bone injury continue, and — most deadly — blocked circulation (ischaemia), because without blood flow no nutrient or antibiotic can reach the wound, leading to gangrene and amputation.
- Sweetener ranking from the episode: stevia and sucralose (Splenda) are ranked safest, based on his own 3-month study in coffee/tea (2–3 cups a day) showing 1–2 kg weight loss and 1–2 cm waist reduction when sugar was swapped for sucralose; then aspartame (Sugar-Free) in small quantities; then jaggery (slightly better than coconut sugar, then brown sugar — all similar and all still raise sugar, so people with diabetes should avoid sugar in any form); white sugar is worst. He advises people with diabetes against fruit juice (whole/cut fruit is fine) because juicing concentrates fruit and calories.
- Best Indian breakfast, in his view, must include fruit and protein: low-GI fruit such as apple or fibre-rich guava (orange and papaya are okay), plus egg — protein at breakfast keeps you full until lunch, whereas poha/idli/dosa alone leaves you hungry in about 2 hours. His own breakfast is fruit, egg (mostly egg white), and milk packed with varied nuts and seeds (chia and pumpkin seeds always, plus flaxseed, almonds, cashew, pistachio and berries).
- Neonatal diabetes — diabetes diagnosed before 6 months of age — is genetic and testable: about 50% of cases (KCNJ11 / ABCC8 mutations) can stop insulin and switch to a cheap tablet (about ₹1 per tablet). He recounts a 3-month-old from Kolkata spared an estimated 17,000 injections and about ₹3.5 lakh in costs over 10 years, says he offers the genetic test free for any child in India diagnosed under 6 months, has found about 500 such children, and estimates about 10,000 exist in India. Do not stop insulin and start tablets without the genetic test, he warns.
- "Mohan's Criteria" are his diagnostic criteria for fibrocalculous pancreatic diabetes (FCPD): occurring in a tropical country, diabetes present, chronic pancreatitis with pancreatic stones/fibrosis present, and not caused by alcohol — a tropical, malnutrition-linked disease most common in Kerala and Tamil Nadu that damages both insulin production and digestion and carries high pancreatic cancer risk.
- On GLP-1 drugs (Ozempic, Mounjaro/semaglutide): not for everyone — lifestyle remains the base treatment, BMI cut-offs around 27/30 apply, they work only while taken and weight returns on stopping, and they need medical evaluation (he cautions against use with gallstones and severe retinopathy, among other conditions). Used appropriately for severe obesity or advanced fatty liver, he says they have stopped insulin, reduced other medicines, and in some patients avoided knee replacement or bariatric surgery.
- On brain health: recent memory goes first in dementia, vascular dementia is definitely higher with diabetes, and new blood tau/biochemical markers plus MRI in studies such as the CARS study (about 25,000 people followed in Chennai and Delhi for 15–20 years, with Emory University and NIMHANS) aim to predict dementia 5–10 years early; until drugs arrive he prescribes daily exercise, salads/fresh vegetables, on-time sleep, stress reduction, pranayama/yoga, puzzles, learning new skills/languages/instruments and social engagement to slow decline.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Mohan's warning that erythritol (in monk fruit sweetener) is one of the worst sweeteners, with cases of blindness and strokes described, and that monk fruit sugar may be worse than white sugar in overall damage — he notes this is from his reading, not his own research, and it goes beyond the ranking of the other sweeteners he studied.
- His claim that PM2.5 air pollution can enter the blood and cause diabetes, blood pressure problems, heart attacks, cancers and dementia, and is a reason diseases of old age are appearing in young people.
- The host's claim that every 1% rise in HbA1c equals two years of brain ageing — Dr Mohan explicitly says you cannot put an exact number on it, and notes some studies find Alzheimer's may actually be lower in people with well-controlled diabetes.
- His prediction that dementia drugs within a year or two will dissolve brain tangles/clumps and restore memory, likened to a GLP-1-style revolution — presented as expectation about drugs still in phase 2/3 trials.
- Statements that AI insulin pumps have never failed and are "better than human," and the historical aside that salt only entered human use about 300–400 years ago as a shipping preservative — both stated conversationally without supporting evidence in the episode.
- The label "Type 3 diabetes" for Alzheimer's — Dr Mohan clarifies there is no official Type 3; the pancreatic form is Type 3C, and the Alzheimer's usage is informal.

## Summary
Diabetologist Dr. V Mohan explains why Indians develop diabetes younger and faster than any other population — consanguineous-marriage genetics, babies born to malnourished mothers with smaller pancreases, thin-fat visceral fat storage, polished high-GI carbs and inactivity — and gives his practical playbook: know the fasting, post-glucose and HbA1c thresholds, fill half the plate with vegetables and only a quarter with carbs, raise plant protein, lose 5 kg to reverse pre-diabetes, and live by ABCD (HbA1c <7%, blood pressure <140/90, LDL <100, and discipline) to avoid blindness, kidney failure, amputations and dementia. He also ranks sweeteners, cautions on GLP-1 drugs, and describes his Mohan's Criteria for pancreatic diabetes and his free genetic testing that has taken hundreds of infants with neonatal diabetes off insulin.
