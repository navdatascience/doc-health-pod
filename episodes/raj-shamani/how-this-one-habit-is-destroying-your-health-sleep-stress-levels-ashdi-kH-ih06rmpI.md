## Important tidbits
- The "one habit" of the title is phone addiction: he says constant scrolling gives the brain slot-machine dopamine hits that dysregulate hormones and neurotransmitters and make ordinary life feel flat, and that human connection is the casualty. His house rules: phones allowed in only two places (the sofa and the standing desk), never at the dining table, in the bedroom or in the toilet — he notes mammals naturally finish a bowel movement in about 90 seconds, while people now sit for 30 minutes with a phone.
- Sleep hygiene through environment: the bed must be associated only with sleep — no TV, computer, reading or even tying shoelaces on it — because the brain ties places and routines to behaviours. His own trick: he sleeps vertically for a full night's sleep but lies horizontally, feet hanging off, for a 20-minute power nap, so his brain reads it as a nap.
- Sleep with energy, don't sleep exhausted: he says going to bed with energy brings faster deep-sleep cycles and better REM sleep, during which cerebrospinal fluid clears waste products from the brain — which is why poor sleep leaves a "fog head" in the morning.
- His headline testosterone numbers: cutting sleep by 2 hours can lower testosterone by about 40%, and several days of 5-hour sleep makes it "drop completely." Low testosterone in men, he says, shows up first as losing the desire to achieve.
- Why male testosterone is falling decade by decade, per him: (1) chronic stress; (2) obesity, especially belly fat; (3) too little high-output exercise — heavy lifting/powerlifting triggers testosterone build-up, with HIIT for cardiovascular and weight goals; (4) overheating "down there" — laptops on laps and hot showers; (5) poor sleep. Fixing sleep alone, he says, starts moving levels back up.
- Stress framework: he distinguishes eustress (good stress that builds you, like training) from extreme stress that breaks you, and chronic stress (unprocessed events from 6+ months ago) from acute stress (a sudden event, like a car hitting yours). His acute-stress tool is box breathing — in 4 seconds, hold 4, out 4 — followed by deliberately finding gratitude in the event; the stressor is the same for everyone, he argues, but stress itself is your reaction/story about it, so reframing changes it. Stress responses are also "inherited" behaviourally from parents, not genetically.
- Anger tool — the "post-mortem": don't try to stop an outburst mid-eruption; afterwards, dissect what triggered it and ask why the gun was loaded ("the trigger isn't the problem — why was there a bullet in it?"). He tells of a billionaire client who threw pens at people when angry; the fix was a pattern-break — excuse himself to the toilet when he felt it rising — after which people stopped fearing him and his business grew.
- Two counter-intuitive sleep habits: wear sunglasses at night, not in the day. Daytime sunglasses, he claims, block the signal for melanin production, so skin burns instead of tanning (build sun exposure gradually, ~10 minutes at a time); at night, sunglasses block blue light so melatonin can rise. He says the body's first sleepiness wave arrives around 8:30 pm and repeats roughly every 90 minutes — catch the first wave or you'll be wide awake until the next. Eyes moving downward also induce sleepiness; raise your laptop if you must stay awake.
- His top unusual habits for quality of life: (1) a fortnightly "relationship review" with your partner (and parents) — three things I did in the last two weeks you didn't like, three you'd like more of, with no justifying or arguing during the review; (2) a fasted slow morning walk — clothes and shoes kept ready the night before, out the door before eating anything (even soaked almonds), because a slow walk burns stored glucose/glycogen for metabolic health (a fast walk is for cardiovascular health), lowers the breakfast sugar spike, and adds early vitamin D; (3) deleting guilt-words from your vocabulary — "try," "should," "must" and "duty" — and his 3-step guilt release: acceptance, identity ("am I the kind of person who does this?"), then change.
- Underwear segment (the episode is in partnership with an innerwear brand): most men, he says from his company's consumer research, never buy their own underwear — mothers buy it before marriage and wives after — so they wear whatever fits badly. Daily underwear must let the testicles move up and down to regulate temperature (very tight briefs prevent this); choose label-free (printed labels) to avoid skin irritation. For workouts, choose moisture-wicking, stretchable, breathable mesh fabric; he prefers trunks for weight-training days and briefs for cardio days, and says trunks that don't ride up matter on long walks (chafing). He warns that wearing synthetic fibres all day may involve chemical leaching (a bigger concern, he says, in women's underwear), and advises against going commando for hygiene — and never with a zip.
- On tight underwear and fertility: he says sperm die at a particular heat temperature, so heat and non-breathable tight underwear affect fertility "for sure," while a testosterone effect is sensed but "not 100% proven"; constant moisture also raises fungal-infection risk. He cites a study he read years ago suggesting tight underwear during the foundation years can restrict genital growth.
- Why few people reach the top: goals differ across money, relationships and health, so someone can be winning in one and average in another; his first exercise with every client is a "magic wand" — write in full detail what your perfect life looks like five years from now, which reveals the real unmet need (75 Rolls-Royces usually means a need for acknowledgement, not cars). His own turning point: after years of 4-hour sleep, butter chicken, cakes and coffees, he collapsed on the floor at age 30 with his heart pounding — and, he says, got more success after slowing down. His best investment: the books he read in his low phase, from which his entire habit-coaching business was built.

## Claims to be careful with
- The guest is a habit coach, not a medical professional — he says so himself ("my surname is Doctor; please don't take this as medical advice") — so the physiology in this episode (testosterone, sperm, brain cleansing) is second-hand popular science, not clinical guidance.
- The figure that 2 hours less sleep lowers testosterone by ~40% is much larger than the effect sizes in the sleep-restriction studies usually cited (~10–15%); treat it as his number, not an established one.
- Claims that daytime sunglasses stop melanin production so you burn instead of tan, and that tight underwear in youth restricts genital growth, are presented from memory of studies he cannot name; the sunglasses advice in particular conflicts with standard eye-protection guidance.
- The underwear segment is a brand partnership and the guest repeatedly recommends the sponsor's products and proprietary fabric technology — treat those product claims as advertising.
- Heat's effect on testosterone (as opposed to sperm production) is, by his own admission in the episode, "not 100% proven."

## Summary
Habit coach Ashdin Doctor argues that the habits quietly destroying health are phone addiction, broken sleep and unmanaged stress. He links them to falling male testosterone — via stress, belly fat, heat, laptops on laps and short sleep — and offers his toolkit: phone-free zones, a bed reserved for sleep only, box breathing plus gratitude for acute stress, an anger "post-mortem," a fasted slow morning walk, fortnightly relationship reviews, sunglasses at night, and properly chosen underwear (in a segment sponsored by an innerwear brand).
