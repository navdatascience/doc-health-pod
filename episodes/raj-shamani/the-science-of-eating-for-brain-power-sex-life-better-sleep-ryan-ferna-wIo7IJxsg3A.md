## Important tidbits
- Fernando took a live finger-prick blood sample from Raj on camera and tracks clients month-on-month on energy levels, muscle/fat change, brain health and productivity, using blood tests covering vitamins, minerals, body composition, cholesterol and triglycerides.
- Only about 30% of the body's fats/cholesterol come from diet, he says; the rest is produced by the liver. Cheap cooking fats such as palm oil raise palmitic acid and inflammation, so fat quality matters more than fat quantity — and he advises shifting from office/restaurant food to a home tiffin box.
- The brain is about 60% fat. The correct omega-6 to omega-3 ratio is 3:1; Fernando says his own test once came back at 13:1, after which he cut outside food.
- Genetics should guide diet: Raj's ancestry test showed roughly 41% South Indian and 9% Rajasthani genes, and Fernando argues your ancestors' region — not the food culture of the city you now live in — should shape what you eat. His own DNA test revealed a gluten allergy and lactose intolerance behind the acne he had until age 40, which led to his book on going wheat-less.
- On sleep and the brain: for every 2 hours cut from 8 hours of sleep, you lose about 40% of the next day's focus and memory, because the hippocampus needs 8 hours to be ready, he says.
- Citing an Uppsala University study, he says junk food on weekends lowers deep slow-wave (SWS) sleep, which reduces growth-hormone and stem-cell release and slows recovery — which is why he tells elite athletes there is no cheat meal, even if an average person can allow one.
- Chronotypes are genetic: roughly one-third of people are morning risers, one-third night owls, one-third in between. Night owls should still get 8 hours (e.g. 2 a.m. to 10 a.m.), because the liver needs about 3 hours to detox during sleep.
- Protein math: net protein utilisation is about 10 g per hour; 10 g is maintenance ("no gain, no loss"), and his "sweet spot" is around 20 g — take 20 g of whey right after a workout and another 20 g after 1–1.5 hours; for fat loss, early morning works well. Always take a protein shake with a fruit or vegetable (fibre) to avoid constipation.
- Avoid artificially flavoured whey and chemical sweeteners such as sucralose and aspartame, he says. Too much protein raises blood urea nitrogen and ammonia, and excess ammonia makes you "dull in the brain."
- Alcohol: heavy training releases creatine kinase that circulates for about 24 hours and aids muscle recovery, and alcohol slaps that down. He tells athletes and newly married couples planning a pregnancy not to drink at all. His worst-to-least-bad ranking: beer worst (calories and gas), then wine (grapes are in the "Dirty Dozen" for pesticide load, though wine also has tannins, polyphenols and resveratrol), then single malt, with gin/vodka with zero-calorie mixers the least bad. He says newer brain-scan research shows even a little alcohol affects the brain.
- On falling testosterone: whoever has come to him with low testosterone, he says, he fixed by improving vitamin D, sleep and micronutrients — zinc, selenium, chromium and manganese (pumpkin seeds, sunflower seeds, dark chocolate). Vitamin D is low across the Indian population because of low sun exposure; he has seen men with testosterone levels of 200–300 improve through diet and exercise alone.
- Erectile dysfunction is a function of too much sugar, he says; less sugar, less smoking and less alcohol improve sex performance. He also blames easy porn access for desensitising young men to real intimacy.
- Brain foods he recommends, including for students: amla with honey, dark chocolate, magnesium-rich foods (he cites an average of 500 mg-plus of dietary magnesium — sunflower seeds, pumpkin seeds, dark chocolate), flax/chia/basil seeds and walnuts for omega-3, berries, and lutein/zeaxanthin for eye–brain coordination. No coffee under age 14; 20 minutes of walking, cycling or skipping on a study day improves next-day memory in exams.
- His sleep protocol: sleep in a cold room, with a heavy cover, at the same time every day. Reset a broken cycle gradually — if you sleep at 3 a.m., shift to 2:45 for a month, then 2:30; it takes about 16 weeks to reset a sleep cycle. Kiwi, walnuts, pistachios, tart cherry and chamomile tea before bed help; keep blue light and phones away.
- Myth round: rice does not inherently cause weight gain — quantity does; choose low-GI rice, or freeze rice and eat it the next day for resistant starch. Eating carbs after 6 p.m. is a "fact" to avoid for desk workers — evening food should be vegetables and a little protein. He prefers a full-sugar Coke once a month over daily Coke Zero, claiming zero-calorie sweeteners disrupt the gut microbiome. Natural spring/alkaline water simply contains minerals. Natural soy is fine — post-40 soy milk can lower cholesterol — but avoid GMO soy and frequent soy if worried about testosterone.
- On reversing obesity and ageing: with diagnostics, diet, exercise and sleep, he says 10,000 steps a day gives roughly 800–900 g of fat loss on his plan. He claims his own metabolic age is 33 against a birth-certificate age of 48, and that a Dubai lab-founder client dropped from a body age of 50 to 35 in about three months. His closing line: your body is the most expensive real estate you own — you can change your house, laptop or business, but not your body.

## Claims to be careful with
- His claim that Wi-Fi left on at night affects the blood-brain barrier and causes vivid/scary dreams is based on his personal experience; he admits the research is "not very conclusive" and alleges, without evidence offered, that telecom companies suppress such research.
- Precise figures — 40% focus loss per 2 hours of lost sleep, 10 g protein utilisation per hour, 800–900 g fat loss from 10,000 steps, 500 mg magnesium — are stated without cited sources and should be treated as his clinical assertions.
- The metabolic-age reversals (his own 48 → 33; a client 50 → 35 in three months) rest on his clinic's fat/muscle-ratio scale markers, not independently verified measurements.
- His alcohol ranking is a "least bad" framing; it sits uneasily with standard public-health guidance, and the resveratrol defence of wine is contested.
- The claim that artificial sweeteners in Coke Zero cause obesity via the gut microbiome is presented as settled research; the evidence is debated.
- Statements about soy, GMO foods and testosterone are more nuanced in the literature than the episode's myth/fact format allows.

## Summary
Celebrity nutritionist Ryan Fernando uses a live on-camera blood test on Raj to argue that diet should be built on diagnostics and genetics — your ancestral region's food, not your current city's — and walks through sleep science (deep SWS sleep, chronotypes, a 16-week reset), protein dosing, alcohol's cost to recovery and fertility, vitamin D and micronutrients for testosterone, brain foods for students, and rapid-fire myth-busting, closing with his biohacking pitch that body age can be reversed because the body is "the most expensive real estate you own."
