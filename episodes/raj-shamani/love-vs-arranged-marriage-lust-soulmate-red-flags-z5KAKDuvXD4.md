## Important tidbits
- Premarital counselling barely exists in India: Dr Singh says that of the marital cases that reach her, roughly 99% of couples have never even heard of premarital counselling — couples meet, socialise, watch movies, and never discuss the things they later fight about.
- The first year of marriage is the worst, not a honeymoon period: after the wedding euphoria there is a "vacuum" when ground reality hits, and partners feel the other person "changed overnight."
- Her top 3 questions to settle before marriage: (1) your financial health and financial aspirations, (2) your responsibilities toward your family members, and (3) children — whether, when, and what each partner's parenting role will be.
- In her counselling experience, money and in-laws are the two factors behind most marital fights; children are the third.
- She estimates around 80% of pregnancies are unplanned — couples rarely discuss maternity/paternity leave, finances, or parenting roles in advance, and often conceive under family pressure ("it's been a year, just do it now").
- She lists seven compatibility factors for a smoother marriage: similar family background (religion, region, culture); similar family structure; similar values of life; similar hobbies and interests; agreed sharing of responsibilities toward each other's families; complete financial transparency; and sharing updated, detailed medical reports with each other (thalassemia, HPV, HIV, dental issues, LASIK, etc.).
- "Opposites attract" is a myth, she says: opposites attract briefly like soda fizz and settle just as fast; long-term bonds — like lasting friendships — are built on similarity, down to small things like being a morning or night person.
- On arranged vs love marriage, her view is that arranged marriages start from ground zero and build upward together, while love marriages start at a peak of very high expectations that are hard to sustain once life becomes "dal, chawal, atta, ghee and mummy-papa"; she says roughly 65–70% of her marital counselling cases are love marriages versus about 35% arranged.
- Her breakdown of why people marry: men mainly for a sexual partner, social acceptance of being "settled," and cohabitation/companionship; women mainly for economic stability, children (she says the maternal instinct is more pronounced), and social sanction — she notes a woman is unlikely to say yes to a man who earns less than her.
- Love, in her framing, is something you give rather than something you find — people searching for love don't find it; don't wait for love, check the practical factors, and if they match with someone you love, marry without delay.
- Values are the hardest thing to change in a person: a mismatch in what each partner holds important (e.g., one wants four bungalows in Bombay, the other wants a quiet life in the hills) is the deepest fault line, harder to adjust than family background, structure, or finances.
- Codependent relationships ("you make me happy, your mood decides mine") feel sweet at first but become stifling and controlling over time; she flags frequent blaming "you" statements in fights ("you did this, you didn't do this") as an early red flag.
- On the psychology of falling in love: she attributes it primarily to hormones (oxytocin and dopamine) and partly to genetics, and notes people are often attracted to features or traits that resemble themselves or their family members — familiarity breeds attraction.
- Her definition of lasting love: years later, still feeling "I am so happy this person is in my life" — and she says consistent effort over time is what builds love, as opposed to the chemical "falling" at first sight.
- A partner's "body count" (number of past partners) does not matter, she says — it is mere number-crunching; someone with no past relationships can still have extramarital affairs, and she notes men who married early sometimes stray later feeling they "never had their fun."
- On alcohol: she frames drinking at least three times a week as being on the alcoholic spectrum, advises judging where on the spectrum a partner falls, and says you should not try to control a partner's drinking — if your happiness depends on what the other person does, you are on a roller coaster; her stated philosophy is moderation in everything.
- On narcissism: everyone sits somewhere on the narcissistic spectrum; it becomes a disorder at the extreme. She says a narcissist operates with four prime aims, the first being "fuel" — any emotional response or reaction they can extract from another person.

## Claims to be careful with
- The percentages Dr Singh quotes (99% of couples unaware of premarital counselling, ~65–70% of her cases being love marriages, ~80% of pregnancies being unplanned) are impressions from her own counselling practice, not population-level statistics.
- Her statement that drinking three or more times a week puts a person "on the alcoholic spectrum" is a simplified framing, not a clinical diagnostic criterion.
- Her conclusion that arranged marriages are "always" better is her professional opinion; she herself acknowledges exceptions and the role of individual temperament.
- Her explanations of attraction (genetics plus oxytocin/dopamine, resemblance to family members) are presented as settled psychology in the conversation but are simplified summaries of research areas.

## Summary
Psychologist Dr Madhumati Singh argues that Indian couples marry without ever discussing the things marriages actually break on — money, in-laws, and children — and makes the case for premarital counselling built around seven compatibility factors, from shared values and financial transparency to exchanging medical reports. She contends arranged marriages start from ground zero and grow, while love marriages start at unsustainable peak expectations; explains falling in love as hormones and genetics but lasting love as giving and effort; and covers red flags such as codependency, controlling behaviour, drinking habits, and narcissistic "fuel"-seeking.
