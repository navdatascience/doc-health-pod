## Important tidbits
- Sahni says diabetes is India's most common disease: roughly 7–8% of the population is known diabetic, and another ~6% are diabetic or pre-diabetic without having tested. Globally, he notes 1 in 9 adults has diabetes and ~250 million people don't know they have it; global healthcare spending on diabetes crossed $1 trillion last year.
- After diabetes, the next most common problems he sees in diagnostics are cholesterol (cardiac risk) and, increasingly, liver disease — he describes the gut–liver axis as having become an epidemic-scale problem in India.
- He blames India's gut problems partly on easy over-the-counter antibiotic use: people self-medicate for minor colds from chemists for years, damaging the gut, without taking anything to repair it, compounded by poor food.
- He expects cancer prevalence to keep rising: ten years ago people said someone in some household had cancer; now, he says, it feels like someone in nearly every family does — driven by environmental and lifestyle factors.
- Brain fog is rising, and he notes dementia/Alzheimer's is being projected in the US to become as big a crisis as diabetes; "neuro performance" therapies he describes include hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) combined with brain-training games, and a Swiss ultrasound-based brain scan that he says showed early signs of ~10% memory improvement every four weeks.
- Future diagnostics, per Sahni: "intelligent toilets" with built-in lab analysers that track urine/stool shape, colour and markers like protein and alert you on your phone; 365-day wearable sensor chips for glucose (replacing repeated finger pricks), with similar sensors coming for cholesterol; and non-invasive light-based attempts to measure glucose/HbA1c without blood (accuracy not yet confirmed, he cautions).
- He predicts regenerative medicine and stem cells will "revolutionise everything" — citing work at Osaka University, claims that knee replacements could disappear, Japanese technology for regrowing original teeth, protein-filtration approaches for dementia, stem-cell vision restoration, and permanent lenses whose focal power can be adjusted externally by microscope.
- Athletes (including a friend who plays in the NFL) are banking their own stem cells in Panama because storage isn't allowed in many countries; Sahni says India still restricts stem-cell use largely because storage conditions, misuse and long-term viability (10 vs 20 years) are not yet established, and regulators learn from Western failures first.
- Diseases he says still have no cure: autoimmune disorders as a class (e.g., thyroid conditions and type 1 diabetes — "there is no reversal to this"; treatment today means identifying and bypassing the trigger, not curing), and some cancers (oesophagus, stomach, colon) that are very difficult if detected late.
- He argues the most under-appreciated fact is that many chronic diseases can be reversed: "anything metabolic has now reached a stage where I can say it is solvable," and obesity is "well solvable today" — but obesity has many mixed root causes (genetics, thyroid, medication-induced, lifestyle), so assuming a person is simply lazy is wrong; he cites people who were athletes at 25 and 180 kg later.
- On his own journey: he built a diagnostics company from 1 city to 250 cities, ~10 to ~4,500 people, zero to ~₹450 crore revenue, raising ~$125 million and taking it from zero to a ~₹3,000 crore valuation, before taking a partial exit in January.
- His own health data drove his new venture: as a "managed" diabetic patient his reports showed two bad trends — either the disease marker kept rising on the same dose (HbA1c going 7.1 → 7.6 → 8) or the dose kept doubling every ~4 years (100 mg → 200 mg → 400 mg) while the disease stayed flat. Modern medicine, he argues, is about suppression, not cure.
- He cites data that only ~0.5–2% of people actually reverse/remit their chronic condition, and those are people who give their entire life over to their health; he also recounts a 2018 Goldman Sachs report ("The Genetic Revolution") on Gilead Pharma, whose revenue fell from $19.1 billion to $4 billion in about four years because its drug was so effective people stopped needing it — raising the report's question, "Is curing patients a sustainable business?"
- His venture Unblock charges ₹2,500 or ₹4,000 for an assessment to find the root cause ("block"); the follow-on protocol runs 3 or 6 months and can range from about ₹10,000/month to ₹30,000–40,000/month in rare cases needing expensive medicines/interventions. His framing: "Medicine is good, but forever medicine is not good" — medicine is for the interim until the root cause is found.
- Unblock is launching with a "cohort zero" of the first 300 patients whose anonymised data (day 0, 30, 90, 270) will be published live on the site, because, he says, if they can't prove results they shouldn't do the business.
- He sees a large business opportunity in Ayurveda for the West: Western consumers' trust in ancient medicine is rising (turmeric "golden lattes," ashwagandha and curcumin in every US pharmacy, brahmi picking up), a company he names (Maharshi) does ~$250 million revenue in the US from Ayurvedic supplements alone, and wellness as an industry has surpassed pharma — but authentic Indian supply is hampered by counterfeits sold under family names, which forced traditional makers to stop distribution entirely.

## Claims to be careful with
- The claim that a Swiss ultrasound brain scan produces "~10% memory improvement every four weeks" and that dementia can be treated by filtering out a causative protein are presented as emerging/experimental, without named studies.
- Predictions that stem cells will eliminate knee replacements, regrow teeth, and permanently restore vision without glasses in 7–8 years are speculative forecasts, not established treatments.
- The assertion that "anything metabolic is solvable" and that obesity is "well solvable today" is the guest's strong personal framing of chronic-disease reversal; reversal rates and durability vary widely by condition and patient.
- The Goldman Sachs / Gilead anecdote is retold from memory (revenue figures and dates as he states them) and is used to support a contested claim that the healthcare system is structurally disincentivised to cure.
- Deepak Sahni is the founder of Unblock, the service being discussed and priced in the episode, so his claims about root-cause reversal are also a pitch for his own business.

## Summary
Healthcare entrepreneur Deepak Sahni tells Raj Shamani that India's health burden is led by diabetes (7–8% diagnosed, ~6% undiagnosed), cholesterol and rising liver/gut disease — worsened by casual over-the-counter antibiotic use — with cancer and dementia as the fastest-growing threats, while autoimmune disease and some late-detected cancers remain incurable. Drawing on his decade building a diagnostics company (1 to 250 cities, ~₹450 crore revenue) and his own experience as a diabetic whose medication dose kept doubling while his disease stayed "managed," he argues modern medicine suppresses rather than cures, that most metabolic disease is in principle reversible by finding root causes, and that the future lies in intelligent toilets, year-long wearable sensors, non-invasive testing, stem cells and regenerative therapies — the thesis behind his new root-cause venture, Unblock.
