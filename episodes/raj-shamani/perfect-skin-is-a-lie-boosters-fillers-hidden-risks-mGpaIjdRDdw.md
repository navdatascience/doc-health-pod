## Important tidbits
- The biggest misconception about skin boosters, per Dr Kandhari: because it is an injectable, people think it will transform or change their face. It will not — a skin booster is for people who do NOT want lift, a bigger chin or bigger lips, and only want healthier, hydrated, supple, glowing skin.
- A skin booster is hyaluronic acid (a sugar), first introduced in India around 2014. Injected into the skin, it slightly expands the skin, which signals the skin to renew its cells; it brings hydration, starts collagen stimulation, and improves strength, tone and texture. Pigment on the surface also looks more dispersed — like a balloon looking lighter and smoother once inflated.
- Nothing removes stretch marks: a stretch mark is a scar that has already formed, so skin boosters (or anything else) cannot make it disappear.
- Why you can't just buy hyaluronic acid and apply it: HA comes in different molecular weights. High-molecular-weight HA in many creams cannot penetrate the skin and only works on the surface; low-molecular-weight HA can penetrate the barrier. He advises looking for "sodium hyaluronate" on ingredient lists — a smaller form of HA with better penetration — though no label guarantees it.
- Fillers vs boosters vs toxins: a filler stays where it is placed, like moulded clay, so it is used for lift, contouring, under-eye hollows and lip enhancement. A skin booster is placed in tiny droplets that merge across the skin for overall skin quality (tone, texture, firmness, elasticity). Toxins are used to relax muscles for moving expression lines.
- Expected result: within about a week the skin feels softer, smoother and more hydrated; boosters help premature-ageing, dehydrated and crepey skin. One syringe of a skin booster costs roughly ₹15,000–18,000.
- On age: he suggests around 35 as a fair mark for when someone should consider a skin booster.
- Technique options: many small pricks, or a one-prick cannula technique that covers the whole face from a single entry point per side — less visible trauma, and the cannula also helps loosen acne scars (subcision). The one-prick technique is also preferred under the eye, where skin is thin and bruises easily.
- Most common complication is bruising: it may resolve in 2–3 days, but on thin skin or the lower face it can last a week to 10 days. A small visible bead of booster can simply be dissolved on the spot.
- The serious complication is vascular compromise: if HA is accidentally injected into a vessel it can block that area's blood supply — an emergency. Warning signs include severe pain, a cold feeling, blanching/colour change, a mottled net-like appearance and ischemic pustules, progressing to necrosis (skin death) if ignored. It is usually picked up within 24 hours (lidocaine in the product can mask pain initially) and is treated by dissolving the HA with the enzyme hyaluronidase within a few hours. Because everyone's vessel anatomy differs, it is a blind procedure and the doctor may have to treat several branches.
- Dr Kandhari says he has never had a vascular-compromise or necrosis case from a skin booster in his own practice, though he has seen such cases; he notes there is no aesthetic practitioner who never has complications.
- Aftercare: an anti-inflammatory/antibacterial cream is given; avoid makeup that day; avoid tap water on the face on the day of the procedure (cleanse with micellar water or bottled water); moisturiser can be mixed with the prescribed cream; no swimming for 48–72 hours; avoid sauna/steam for about a week; take it easy on strenuous exercise that day and the next; sun exposure is fine after roughly a day.
- Who should avoid it: pregnant or breastfeeding women (no studies exist, so dermatologists stay clear), people with bleeding disorders, keloid tendency, or active facial infection/disease. He also counts excessively high expectations as a contraindication — a booster improves skin health gradually and cannot simultaneously lift, erase scars, pores and pigmentation.
- His patient mix is about 70% women and 30% men, and roughly 70–80% (or more) of skin-booster patients are women; men often decide instantly once told it works, while women ask more questions about pain and process.
- He warns against the treatment "black hole": once one procedure works, people keep wanting more (under-eye, then lines, then nose). Citing the Hippocratic principle of doing no harm — physically and mentally — he says the doctor's job is to tell patients when to stop.
- On the business side (his estimates): a reputed aesthetic dermatologist in South Delhi can generate ₹10–50 lakh a month in revenue; individual machines cost anywhere from ₹15 lakh to ₹1 crore; a high-tech clinic can be opened within ₹1 crore, while a top-end setup can take ₹3–4 crore of investment.

## Summary
Dermatologist Dr Rajat Kandhari explains that skin boosters are injectable hyaluronic acid meant to improve skin quality — hydration, suppleness, tone and glow — not to change facial structure, which is the job of fillers, while toxins handle expression lines. He covers why topical HA often can't penetrate the skin, the one-prick cannula technique, realistic costs (₹15,000–18,000 a syringe) and timing (around age 35), aftercare, and the risks: bruising is common, while vascular compromise is a rare emergency that must be dissolved with hyaluronidase within hours. He also cautions against unrealistic expectations and the endless-treatment loop, and notes that nothing can remove stretch marks, which are scars.
