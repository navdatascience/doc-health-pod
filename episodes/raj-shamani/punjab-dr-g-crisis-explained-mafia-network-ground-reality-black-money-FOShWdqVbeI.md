## Important tidbits
- Mukul's core framing: the drug problem is bad, but worse is that it keeps growing every day. Per his Punjab reporting, drug peddlers now outnumber addicts — sellers are roughly double the number of users.
- He investigated on the ground in Bathinda, Punjab: spent about a week meeting peddlers, addicts and families wiped out by drugs, and bought drugs from a peddler in broad daylight (3 pm) on camera.
- Supply chain as described by a peddler on record: drugs come from Afghanistan and other routes, are moved in small packets (including via drones across porous borders), reach Delhi, then Punjab, where a "drug lord" distributes them to small peddlers.
- The hook model: a young person (often 17–18) is pressured by friends to try it, then given drugs free for about 5–6 days; from around the sixth–seventh day the same person sells it to them — heroin ("chitta") at roughly ₹3,500 per gram, per the peddler he spoke to.
- Two broad drug categories in India: natural/non-synthetic (opium, charas, ganja, opioids) and lab-made synthetics such as tramadol and fentanyl. He cites published reports that a Mumbai pharma company illegally manufactures tramadol/fentanyl that is sold into African countries.
- Injection harm escalates: when the veins in the arms are destroyed, users inject near the groin/private parts, where he says the chances of death are highest.
- HIV link through shared needles: he cites data that around 500 people a year in Punjab were getting HIV-infected in 2020–21, versus roughly 90,000 in the 2024–25 data; he says the Health Ministry told Parliament about ~32,000 new HIV-positive cases a year, with drug use a major driver — five boys sharing one syringe means one positive user can infect the group.
- Punjab has the country's highest drug-overdose deaths, he says; he recounts a Ludhiana mother whose six sons all died of drug overdoses, and a village (Bir Talab) where nearly every household has lost someone to drugs.
- Addiction is not a willpower problem: de-addiction itself uses a tapering process because the body cannot quit heroin suddenly. A former state/zonal-level volleyball player told him he got hooked on chitta and now cannot play even 15 minutes.
- Because the trade is unregulated, "chitta" is sold in white, brown and pink "qualities" at different prices, mixed with unknown chemicals — users have no idea what they are consuming.
- He argues the issue stays unsolved because a parallel black-money economy has grown around drugs in which "everyone is involved"; one peddler phoned a policeman in front of him to arrange a delivery time and claimed he also supplies inside an army cantonment (a claim Mukul says he could not verify).
- Impact of his report: instead of denial or a defamation notice, the Punjab government acted — the SHO of the specific police station was suspended, several peddlers were arrested, and the ADG (Anti-Narcotics) spoke with him for an hour to understand the ground situation.
- The risks of such reporting are real: after publication, his local source was chased by 14–15 men from the peddler network and had to be rescued; an FIR and written complaint were filed and police provided security.
- He points to Portugal as a country that largely solved its drug problem: decriminalisation, treating addicts as citizens rather than criminals, and incentives (such as tax benefits for employers who hire people out of rehab) so recovered addicts reintegrate into society.
- In a related segment, he discusses Delhi's missing-persons numbers — 807 people reported missing between 1–15 January (about 54 per day), which police PRO Sanjay Tyagi publicly called "usual"; Mukul argues that 54 a day being treated as normal is itself the bigger problem.

## Claims to be careful with
- The HIV figures (about 500 infections a year in 2020–21 rising to ~90,000 in 2024–25, and ~32,000 new cases a year) are cited by the guest from ministry/parliamentary data as he presents it; the scale of the jump should be treated as his reporting, not independently verified here.
- The claim that a peddler supplies drugs to police personnel and inside an army cantonment comes from the peddler himself; the guest explicitly says he could not investigate or verify it.
- The Mumbai pharma company illegally making tramadol/fentanyl for African markets is attributed by the guest to published reports; no company is named.
- The ₹3,500-per-gram heroin price, the "peddlers are double the addicts" ratio, and village-level accounts are ground-reporting claims from his investigation and interviews.

## Summary
Investigative reporter Mukul Singh Chauhan describes what he found on the ground in Punjab: a drug network that moves heroin and synthetics in small packets from across the border via Delhi, hooks teenagers with free drugs for a few days and then sells to them, and sustains itself through a parallel black-money economy. He details the health fallout — destroyed veins, overdose deaths and HIV spread through shared needles — the threats he and his source faced after his report, the unusual official response (a suspended SHO, arrests, and an hour-long call with the ADG Anti-Narcotics), and Portugal's decriminalisation model as a possible way out.
