## Important tidbits
- Orbit is not about escaping gravity: gravity still exists at the ISS's ~400 km altitude. A spacecraft stays up because it is in continuous free fall around the Earth — its forward speed matches the rate at which it falls toward Earth's curvature (Newton's cannonball idea). The capsule first reaches roughly 200 km and then uses timed engine burns to raise its orbit toward the ISS at ~424 km.
- Docking is fully automated (with the commander able to intervene at any step) and extraordinarily precise: both vehicles are travelling at about 7.6 km/s, yet the final approach narrows from a "10-lane highway" of freedom to margins of centimetres per second, ending in a soft capture, a motor that pulls the vehicles together, and 12 bolts that lock radially. If the bolts don't lock, the crew backs off and tries again.
- The launch is over fast: in 8.5 minutes the capsule goes from 0 to ~200 km altitude and from 0 to 28,500 km/h. Acceleration builds to around 5G, felt on the chest while lying on your back — the chest and lungs are compressed so you cannot breathe actively through your lungs and must breathe through your stomach (a trained technique), your head is pushed back, your eyes feel as if they're going inside, and raising a hand to press a button takes five times the effort. In a worst-case abort, he says, the body could briefly face up to ~18G — "like a baby elephant sitting on your chest."
- When a stage cuts off and G-force suddenly drops, the fluid in the inner ear (the vestibular "orientation triad") sloshes, producing a powerful false sensation of tumbling/somersaulting forward even though you're strapped into your seat.
- In microgravity the otolith organs in the inner ear, whose hair-like structures hang vertically under gravity on Earth, go horizontal — your "datum" changes. The resulting sensory conflict (eyes, inner ear and proprioceptors disagreeing) causes space sickness in the first days: disorientation (there is no up, down, left or right without visual cues), headache, nausea and a noticeable slowing down — Shukla says even tasks he knew well took him deliberate extra time, and the urge to vomit came on suddenly rather than building gradually as on Earth. He did not actually vomit.
- Blood and fluid shift from the legs to the head: the face visibly swells and looks puffy (visible in his day 2–3 photos/videos speaking to the Prime Minister from space), while the heart — no longer pumping against gravity — actually shrinks by about 10–15% depending on how long you've been up there. "Your head gets bigger and your heart gets smaller — the worst possible combination," he jokes.
- You grow taller in space because the spine decompresses, which can cause back pain.
- The permanent head congestion from the fluid shift dulls the sense of smell, which significantly reduces the sense of taste; ISS food is also kept low in sodium for bone-health reasons. He craved salt and spicy food so much that he asked his flight surgeon in advance to bring something salty when picking him up after landing — he got chips.
- Almost everything on the station is recycled: when all systems are working, 98% of the water is recycled — including urine — prompting the station saying "yesterday's coffee is tomorrow's coffee."
- Tears don't fall in space: when he got emotional seeing India from the station, the tears just accumulated in his eyes. On a night pass from south to north, India stood out fully lit — he could make out Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Pune, Delhi and even the India–Bangladesh border — and thunderstorms over the dark ocean showed up as sudden purple flashes.
- The suit worn inside the capsule (IVA suit) weighs about 16–18 kg. The spacewalk (EVA) suit weighs around 120–140 kg because it *is* your spaceship: it carries all environmental control and life-support systems — thermal rejection, oxygen supply, moisture/water handling — and is itself pressurised, since there is no protective shell or artificial pressure around you outside the station.
- Astronaut selection medicals are exhaustive — bone health, heart health, muscle tissue, dental health and aneurysm screening among them; he notes dental health alone caused many rejections, and some findings make you permanently unfit while others are only temporarily unfit.
- Most astronaut training is not for the nominal mission but for failure scenarios — what to do when something goes wrong at any step — since a fully nominal flight runs automatically. On mental preparation he is blunt: "One little thing and I am gone."
- His path was accidental: fascinated by a fighter-jet air show in class 6 (near Lucknow), he filled in a friend's abandoned NDA form (₹20) without telling his parents, wrote the exam, and only when friends arrived home with the Employment News to celebrate did his parents learn he'd been selected; his father, who had quietly wanted him to join the civil services, gave permission but no money or logistical help. He never consciously imagined becoming an astronaut even though Rakesh Sharma flew in 1984.
- His advice to a child from a small village in the North East asking how he could become an astronaut: a mountain looks huge until you climb it one step at a time with your eyes down; "if it is possible for me, it is possible for anybody in this country."
- The worst advice nobody should follow, he says, is "it's not so simple / it's not so easy" — it discourages people from even starting; things become simple once you get into them and persist. He also rejects "first do this much, then we'll see" and (agreeing with Raj) "be practical," which he calls a substitute for scaling down your ambition — "only a person suffocated by an unlived dream tells you to be practical."

## Claims to be careful with
- Figures such as the heart shrinking "10–15%," 98% water recycling, and up to ~18G in a worst-case abort are as stated by Shukla in conversation; exact values vary by mission, duration and source.
- The episode is a personal-experience conversation, not a medical briefing: descriptions of space sickness, fluid shifts and taste changes reflect his individual experience, which he himself notes differs from person to person ("everybody gets it — to what degree may be different").
- This is an inspirational/educational interview rather than a clinical health episode; the health content is about space physiology, not advice applicable to everyday health decisions.

## Summary
Group Captain Shubhanshu Shukla, the first Indian to reach the ISS, explains spaceflight to Raj Shamani like a story: orbit as perpetual free fall, docking at 7.6 km/s with centimetre precision, an 8.5-minute launch to 28,500 km/h under up to ~5G where you must breathe through your stomach, and then a body in revolt — inner-ear fluid sloshing, space sickness, blood shifting headward so the face swells while the heart shrinks 10–15%, a decompressing spine that makes you taller, dulled taste from permanent congestion, tears that pool instead of falling, and 98% of water (including urine) recycled on station. He also describes the 16–18 kg IVA suit versus the 120–140 kg EVA suit that functions as your spaceship, the exhaustive medical selection (where dental health alone rejected many), and his own accidental path from a Lucknow air show and a friend's spare NDA form to space — closing with the message that big dreams are climbed one step at a time and that "it's not so simple" is the worst advice anyone can give.
