## Important tidbits
- Makhija says 73% of Indians don't get enough protein, and protein is needed at all three main meals — not just one bowl of dal a day. Protein is the raw material for immunity, repair, hair, skin, and healing.
- Her "dress your carbs" rule: Indians eat too many "naked carbs" (plain poha, plain pasta, aloo paratha, lemon rice). Dress carbs with fibre in front (vegetables, salad, soup) and protein behind (dal, pulses, sprouts, chicken, fish, eggs) to blunt glucose spikes — and she equates glucose spikes with inflammation.
- Meal sequencing: eat salad first, then about 50% of your vegetables and 50% of your protein plain, then eat the rest with your roti or rice. She says the order alone can reduce glucose spikes by up to 40%.
- Physiological sugar cravings are usually hidden protein or salt deficiency: when the body lacks protein it manifests as a sugar craving — the classic post-lunch "something sweet" in India.
- She defends salt: sodium is an essential mineral, excess salt leaves in urine while excess sugar is stored as fat. Low salt intake forces the kidneys to reabsorb sodium via raised insulin, which drops blood sugar and triggers hunger. Cook with iodised salt (iodine protects the thyroid), but don't switch entirely to pink salt.
- Mineralise your water: filtered water is stripped of minerals, so put one small crystal of rock salt (khadi namak) on your tongue with each glass. She says this lengthens the time before you need the bathroom, quenches thirst properly, and cuts sugar cravings; adults need 2–3 litres daily, and even 2% dehydration impairs memory, mood, and recall.
- Breakfast skippers, per a meta-analysis of 45 observational studies she cites, are 31% more likely to have belly fat, 48% more likely to be overweight, and 44% more likely to be obese.
- The "thrifty gene": famines under British rule (Bengal 1770, Madras 1870s) made Indian bodies "storers, not burners" — the TOFI phenotype (thin outside, fat inside): more visceral fat, less lean muscle, greater insulin resistance, hence higher diabetes and heart-disease risk even for Indians living in the West.
- Sleep is non-negotiable: 6 hours versus 7–8 hours of sleep makes you 40% more likely to get fractures and osteoporosis, she says, and chronic sleep deprivation raises all-cause mortality. Sleep also controls the ghrelin/leptin balance — Raj's poor satiety is, she tells him, his 4–5 hours of sleep, not his genetics; lifestyle is 70% of how you age.
- She claims sugar is at times more addictive than cocaine — hitting the same dopamine reward centres — and says sugar is "eight times more addictive than cocaine."
- Eating through a breakup, burnout, or crisis: pre-plan a 10-day cyclic menu (breakfast, lunch, dinner) with someone not in the slump, put it on the fridge, and follow it without debate to escape the cheat-punish-reward loop. Her top crisis foods: protein, good fats, magnesium-rich foods (seeds, nuts), hydration, and one small planned comfort food a day.
- The Indian thali, she says, could be "the best plate in the world" — salad, fermented pickle (probiotics), two vegetables, dal, and carbs, traditionally served in meal-sequencing order; ancestors also followed chronobiology (biggest meal at breakfast, smallest at dinner). In a study she cites, 100 obese women on identical calories lost double the weight eating 700 kcal breakfast / 500 lunch / 200 dinner versus the reverse split over 12 weeks.
- Travel and flights: bowel health follows "HMF" — Hydration (2–3 litres), Movement (60 minutes), Fibre. For long flights: take 2,000 mg omega-3 against low-oxygen inflammation, drink water with salt (flights leach about a litre of water every three hours), skip alcohol, and choose a Virgin Mary (tomato juice, salt, pepper) as the best in-flight drink; taste buds drop to about 50% at altitude, which is why flight food is over-flavoured.
- "Fake sleep": alcohol makes you fall asleep faster but disrupts REM and long-term sleep quality, so you wake up tired — the same applies to marijuana and cigarettes used as sleep crutches.
- Target nutrients once the basics are fixed: salt for bloating, magnesium for anxiety/depression, chromium picolinate for sugar balance in diabetics, zinc for immunity, curcumin and vitamin C for sinus blockage, omega-3 for inflammation.

## Claims to be careful with
- The claim that wheat was genetically modified from 5–6% gluten to 10–15% gluten, exhausting our digestive enzymes, is a contested simplification of wheat breeding history.
- "Sugar is eight times more addictive than cocaine" comes from animal studies and is widely disputed as a human claim; she presents it as settled.
- The saline argument — that IV saline (9,000 mg sodium) proves sodium lowers blood pressure and was "demonised so sugar could shine" — is a rhetorical framing, not clinical evidence; people with hypertension or kidney disease should not raise salt intake on this basis.
- The statement that by the time you feel thirsty you are "already 40% dehydrated" appears to be an exaggeration of standard dehydration guidance.
- Her celebrity anecdotes (Shahid Kapoor and Deepika Padukone as most disciplined; Ranbir Kapoor initially resisting her vegetable juice) are unverifiable colour, and the juice recipe claims (skin, eyesight, digestion) are testimonial.

## Summary
Clinical nutritionist Pooja Makhija argues that most Indian health problems trace back to five fixable basics — protein at every meal, dressed (never naked) carbs, mineralised water with adequate salt, sleep, and movement. She explains sugar cravings as masked protein and salt deficiency, defends the traditional Indian thali as a model plate, and gives concrete protocols for meal sequencing, crisis eating, travel, and long flights.
