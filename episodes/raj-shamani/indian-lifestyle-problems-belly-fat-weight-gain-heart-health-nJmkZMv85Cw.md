## Important tidbits
- Sarin opens with India's metabolic numbers (citing ICMR): 101 million diagnosed diabetics and 136 million prediabetics; cardiovascular disease causes 27% of deaths in India; the average heart-attack age is 53 in India versus 63 internationally; obesity prevalence is around 40%, with a projection of 450 million obese Indians by 2050 if nothing changes.
- One in 3 Indians has fatty liver — a figure he says reaches roughly 80% among IT workers in one report — and about 25 million couples struggle with fertility, with erectile dysfunction now appearing in men's 30s and PCOS in teenage girls. He frames all of these as one cluster: metabolic dysfunction whose common denominator is insulin resistance.
- Studies he cites put insulin resistance at roughly 1 in 3 Indian adults (30.5% in one tested sample), with about 90% unaware they have it; ICMR data he quotes says 57% of diabetics were unaware of their diabetes.
- His mechanism in brief: insulin acts like a "bouncer" moving excess glucose into muscle and fat cells. A typical Indian day — poha/idli/dosa/paratha breakfasts (~70% carbohydrate), sugary mid-morning tea with cookies, a roti/rice lunch, fried evening snacks, a carb-heavy dinner — keeps insulin elevated all day, and chronic hyperinsulinemia is, he says, the #1 cause of insulin resistance.
- The progression he describes: fat cells can expand ~100× like a water balloon and eventually turn insulin-resistant to protect themselves; the pancreas compensates with more insulin for 5–10 years, so fasting glucose and HbA1c stay normal while insulin runs high; then beta cells fail — prediabetes (fasting glucose >100 mg/dL) appears around year 11 and diabetes (fasting >125 mg/dL) roughly 5 years later, while glucose-lowering medicines only mask the symptom.
- No-blood-test warning signs he lists: skin tags and dark velvety neck patches (acanthosis nigricans — insulin is also a growth hormone), waist size (guideline ~90 cm for men, ~80 cm for women; his simpler rule is waist no more than half your height), high blood pressure (he cites a study attributing 50% of hypertension to insulin resistance, via insulin raising aldosterone and salt/water retention), and ED in men / PCOS in women (he cites research that 70% of PCOS is insulin-resistance-driven, and ED in your 30s signalling 3–4× higher cardiovascular-event risk in your 40s–50s).
- The test he wants everyone to take is fasting insulin (available at almost any lab, under ₹500) — with a warning: labs call up to 25 "normal," but those are population averages, not optimal ranges. His targets: 5 or below is good, 5–10 is early resistance, 10 and above is insulin resistance.
- The "thin-fat" phenotype: thin outside, fat inside. He calls BMI the worst proxy for Indian health — Indians develop metabolic disorders at lower BMI, so India's cutoffs are already revised to 23 (overweight) and 27.5 (obese) versus the Western 25/30. He credits Pune researcher Dr Chittaranjan Yajnik as a pioneer of the Indian thin-fat phenotype research.
- Where fat is stored matters more than weight: subcutaneous fat (under the skin) is a relatively healthy store; visceral and ectopic fat (around and inside organs) drives insulin resistance — and Indians are predisposed to visceral storage, storing fat by enlarging fat cells (hypertrophy) rather than multiplying them (hyperplasia). He traces this to epigenetics shaped by 31 famines during 190 years of British rule, citing a China famine study in which the generation in the womb during famine had 75% higher diabetes/metabolic risk and the grandchildren's risk was about double.
- His metabolic-syndrome checklist (the "harmonized criteria"): any 3 of 5 — fasting glucose above 100, triglycerides above 150, HDL below 40 (men) / 50 (women), blood pressure above 130/80, waist above 90 cm (men) / 80 cm (women).
- On sugar and "invisible sugar": the body needs zero grams of dietary sugar — the liver makes all required glucose (the brain needs roughly 120 g/day) via gluconeogenesis. Roti and rice are ~70% carbohydrate and become glucose just as sugar does, so the body doesn't distinguish them; an ICMR study he cites says 70% of Indian calories come from carbohydrates, the only non-essential macronutrient. Insulin, he argues, is the trigger that decides whether calories are stored or burned — his "proof" being diabulimia, where type 1 diabetics who skip insulin cannot gain fat even on very high calories (he stresses this is extremely dangerous and not to be tried).
- Practical food guidance: he self-tested 10 flours on a continuous glucose monitor and found the ancient grains jau (barley) and bajra (pearl millet) gave much gentler glucose spikes than wheat (including emmer); coarse-ground flour with its fiber beats fine packaged flour; for rice, processing matters more than variety — choose unpolished rice over polished basmati. Fruit should be treated as dessert: eaten after a meal, never juiced (juice is effectively pure sugar) and never as a meal on an empty stomach; non-tropical fruits and berries carry less sugar than tropical fruits like mango and banana.
- He links insulin resistance to the brain and fertility as well: neurodegenerative diseases are increasingly called "type 3 diabetes" (insulin helps clear amyloid and supports memory formation; he cites research finding defective insulin receptors in dementia patients' brains), and he calls fertility effects a "type 4" — 1 in 5 Indian women of reproductive age has PCOS, and in men insulin resistance promotes aromatase (converting testosterone to estrogen) and opposes nitric oxide, impairing erections.
- His "Big Rocks" of metabolic health: (1) nutrition, (2) activity/exercise, (3) stress and sleep. Strength training is, he says, the optimal activity for metabolic health because muscle is the body's glucose sink — bodyweight work counts — ideally 4 sessions a week of 40–45 minutes. Chronic stress keeps cortisol high; cortisol directly opposes insulin and preferentially adds visceral ("stress belly") fat.
- His nutrition principle is "control carbohydrates, don't fear fats, prioritize protein" — he clarifies he is not anti-vegetarian, but says vegetarian diets make protein harder; his top vegetarian protein sources are paneer, tofu, and soya. He also notes Singapore and Hong Kong recently acted against two Indian spice brands over alleged cancer-causing contamination, and that modern hybridized grains and fruits (Green Revolution dwarf wheat, IR8 rice) are structurally different from what previous generations ate.

## Claims to be careful with
- The framing that the body needs "zero grams" of sugar and that carbohydrates are simply "non-essential" is Sarin's position; it is contested in mainstream nutrition guidance, which recommends carbohydrate intake within balanced diets.
- Diabulimia is presented as "absolute proof" that insulin alone decides fat storage — an oversimplification of energy balance, and a dangerous eating disorder he himself warns against.
- The "type 3 diabetes" (for dementia/Alzheimer's) and "type 4 diabetes" (for fertility) labels are emerging/popular terms, not standard clinical diagnoses.
- Several statistics are cited from his book without full sourcing in the episode — including 50% of hypertension being attributable to insulin resistance, 70% of PCOS being insulin-resistance-driven, ~80% fatty liver among IT workers, and the famine-epigenetics risk figures (75% higher risk, 2× in grandchildren).
- The claim that Singapore/Hong Kong acted against Indian spice brands over cancer-causing substances refers to alleged contamination findings; the episode presents it as settled, which listeners should verify.
- His flour rankings come from his own n=1 continuous-glucose-monitor self-testing, which he acknowledges varies by person.

## Summary
Metabolic health coach and "Sick Nation" author Karan Sarin argues that India's epidemics of diabetes, heart disease, fatty liver, PCOS, and erectile dysfunction are one disease — insulin resistance, driven by a carbohydrate-heavy diet (70% of calories, per ICMR) that keeps insulin elevated all day for 10–15 silent years before glucose numbers move. He explains why thin Indians can be metabolically fat, why fasting insulin (target ≤5) matters more than glucose or BMI, and lays out his fixes: fewer and better-chosen carbs (jau/bajra over wheat, unpolished rice, fruit as dessert, never juice), strength training, and managing stress and sleep.
