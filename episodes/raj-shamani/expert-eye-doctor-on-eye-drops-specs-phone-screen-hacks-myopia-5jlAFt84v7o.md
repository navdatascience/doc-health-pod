## Important tidbits
- Eyelash extensions can harm the eyes: the glue blocks the openings of the oil (meibomian) glands along the lash line, and patients come in with swollen, burning eyes and dry-eye-type problems afterwards.
- The doctor's recommended screen time is only 2–3 hours a day; for under-18s in the developing age, constant near work trains the brain that near vision matters and far vision doesn't, driving myopia.
- Keep the phone farther away: the closer the phone is to your eyes, the harder the eye muscles must work to converge; phones even have a built-in "screen distance" feature that warns you when the phone is too close.
- Diabetics should get an eye check-up once a year and should not self-treat: the eye is the one place where blood vessels can be visualised directly, so vessel changes can be seen and future problems predicted.
- Temporary blurring of distance vision after long reading sessions (which can take up to ~30 minutes to clear) is a muscle spasm and is normal — nothing to worry about.
- "Computer glasses" are not blue-light glasses: they are mild +0.25 convex-power glasses that share roughly half the convergence strain of screen work; he says they have no long-term side effects and do not change your number.
- Lubricating drops such as Refresh Tears can be used 3–4 times a day long-term without harm — but he stresses the difference between a lubricant (a moisturiser for the eye) and an actual medicine (like a steroid).
- Myopia figures he cites: around 90% of people in Korea/East Asia have a spectacle number, about 60% of Indian teenagers, and about 42% of 15–19-year-olds in the US/UK.
- Lack of outdoor sunlight is a major driver: sunlight triggers retinal chemicals that prevent the eye from elongating; Singapore and Japan have made 2–3 hours (minimum ~120 minutes) of outdoor activity mandatory in school hours, and he advises at least 2 hours outdoors daily for under-18s.
- Myopia progression in children can now be slowed by roughly 60% using diluted atropine drops, "MyoSmart" glasses, or orthokeratology (Ortho-K) — night lenses worn while sleeping and removed in the morning so the child needs no glasses during the day; soft contact lenses must never be slept in.
- He describes a new AI-based laser procedure (Wavelight Plus Innoveyes) that builds a digital clone of the eye, runs a virtual surgery on it, and can give vision of 6/5, 6/4 or even 6/3 — sharper than the standard 6/6; the procedure takes ~5 minutes with ~10 seconds of laser, and costs about ₹1.3 lakh at his hospital versus ~₹25,000 for standard LASIK and ~₹1.1–1.2 lakh for Contoura/SILK.
- Presbyopia (reading-glasses power) comes to essentially everyone after age 40–45 as the natural lens loses flexibility — LASIK or no LASIK — because ageing changes cannot be stopped.
- On vitamin D: get your levels checked before supplementing; taking a weekly vitamin D dose indefinitely without checking levels can cause toxicity.
- Evaporative dry eye comes from blocked meibomian glands (makeup blocks them too); tears have three layers with an oil layer on top. Intense pulsed light (IPL) therapy — five light shots that activate the parasympathetic pathway so glands produce more oil — plus lid massage over 3–6 sittings can rebuild the flow.
- Sunglasses act as a filter blocking ~90% of light; cheap ₹50–100 ones are style only. Look for UV-A and UV-B protection (excess UV can contribute to cataract and macular degeneration) and polarised lenses to cut glare while driving in harsh sun — not at night.
- Blue-light filter glasses are largely a marketing gimmick: blue light at night mainly matters because it suppresses melatonin, and phone features like night-shift mode, grayscale and screen-distance warnings do the same job.
- No supplement can cure eye strain — strain is muscle fatigue. Eye exercises (pencil push-ups), a bigger screen, bigger font and more distance are what help; lutein/zeaxanthin-type vitamins may help only somewhat in ARMD-type conditions.
- Basic eye hygiene needs almost nothing extra: follow the 20-20-20 rule, go outdoors, and don't splash water into your eyes every morning; the eye maintains itself, and you should see a doctor when something feels abnormal.
- Excessive non-veg eating does not damage eye health — he dismisses claims linking it to optic-nerve or circulation damage in the eyes.

## Claims to be careful with
- His attack on Ayurvedic eye drops (onion, ginger, lemon juice and honey) that claim to treat "99% of eye problems" — he says cataract is age-related protein denaturing of the lens and can only be treated by surgery, and that retinitis pigmentosa, colour blindness and optic atrophy have no cure to date; he issues an open challenge to anyone to document cataract reversal with graded photographs of ~100 patients. This is his position as an allopathic surgeon in an ongoing Ayurveda-vs-allopathy debate, and he promotes procedures offered at his own hospital.
- The AI laser "eagle vision" results (6/3 vision, a legally blind girl in the UK now seeing better than a normal person) are presented by the doctor who performs and sells the procedure — treat as promotional claims.
- His prediction that close to 100% of people will eventually become myopic, and the country-wise myopia percentages, are stated without cited sources in the episode.
- His claim that cataract drops "cannot exist" and that no eye doctor among ~27,000 in India has seen one work is an argument from his own experience, not a cited study.

## Summary
Ophthalmologist Dr Rahil Chaudhary explains why myopia is exploding (near work plus too little outdoor sunlight), how its progression in children can be slowed, and what modern spectacle-removal options — from Ortho-K night lenses to a new AI-planned laser he says can beat 6/6 vision — can and cannot do. He also debunks blue-light glasses and eye-strain supplements, explains dry-eye treatment, and strongly criticises Ayurvedic eye drops that claim to cure cataract and other incurable conditions without surgery.
