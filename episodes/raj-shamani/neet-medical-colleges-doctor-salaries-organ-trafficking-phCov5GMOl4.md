## Important tidbits
- About 22 lakh students sit NEET-UG every year for roughly 1 lakh MBBS seats, of which only ~40,000 are government seats — and the few hundred top government seats (AIIMS-type) are the most fiercely contested.
- A fresh MBBS doctor starts at around ₹50,000/month; the average is ~₹1.25 lakh/month, and pay is often higher (up to ~₹1.5 lakh) in peripheral areas where doctors are scarce.
- Specialisation is effectively compulsory today: about 2 lakh doctors compete in NEET-PG, and even after 8 years of study a new MD/MS doctor starts at only ~₹70,000–80,000/month. Real earnings, he says, begin only ~10 years into practice, as doctors are valued more with age and trust.
- PG residents work 100+ hours a week on a stipend of ₹50,000–70,000 and can face violence from patients — a major reason doctors leave for the US, where salaries start above ₹1 crore with fixed working hours and health benefits.
- Government college fees are almost negligible (AIIMS is nearly free), and government seats cannot be bought: every year, scamsters fool students with offers like "pay ₹1 crore and we'll get you into AIIMS," take the money and disappear.
- Private-college PG fees vary enormously by state because of fee caps: UP has capped fees at ~₹13 lakh/year, Maharashtra has no cap and charges ₹40–50 lakh/year, and Karnataka/Tamil Nadu charge up to ₹80–90 lakh/year. Private colleges also block seats under a "management quota," where donations are now largely folded into heavy official fees.
- A medical college is judged by patient inflow, not academics alone — government colleges are considered best because trainees see the widest variety of diseases. Among private colleges he rates Manipal among the best (great patient load, culture and infrastructure), followed by the likes of Subharti (Meerut), Sharda and DY Patil (Mumbai).
- Radiology is the highest-earning branch — he claims a radiologist can earn ₹10 lakh/month within five years. Dermatology is second because it has become a commercial/cosmetic branch (people will pay anything for hair and skin), followed by medicine, surgery and obstetrics.
- USMLE vs NEET-PG: USMLE tests clinical, scenario-based decision-making "like a doctor," while NEET-PG historically asked one-line factual recall questions; it is only now becoming more clinical. US residents earn ~₹70–80 lakh/year from day one with structured hours; about 10,000 of each year's Indian graduates head to the US, and he states 1 in 5 doctors in America is Indian.
- The foreign-medical-graduate (FMG) route exists mainly because of cost: Russia (since the 1990s), the Philippines, Georgia, Uzbekistan and Kazakhstan historically charged ~₹1–1.5 lakh/year versus Indian private colleges. A big drawback is language — teaching was in the native language until recently, and even "English-medium" teaching is often a localised version.
- The FMGE licensing exam's pass rate is only 20–25%. He blames "dabba" (box) colleges — institutions with zero infrastructure that are nevertheless NMC-recognised. His advice: before sending a child abroad, check the college's actual FMGE pass rate, confirm the student's seat is within the college's licensed seat count, and speak to students already admitted there.
- A common FMG scam: contractors admit more students than a college's licensed capacity (e.g., 500 students for 300 seats) assuming the licence will expand; when it doesn't, the extra students are forced to resign and sent back to India with no recourse.
- Returning FMGs are often made to work as unpaid labour in internships (zero stipend). An NMC rule that would have required FMGs to work three years unpaid before getting a licence was withdrawn after protests — an example, he says, of rules being made without thought and reversed on protest.
- On fake doctors: outright fraudulent degrees are, in his estimate, under 1%. The bigger phenomenon is "quacks" — compounders who work with a doctor, learn routine patient management (most patients present the same few complaints), and then set up as doctors in their villages.
- His own business story: DBMCI's offline coaching centres cost ₹50–60 lakh/year each to run and the business is brutally seasonal (if students don't join by March, the whole year is a loss). By 2019 the company was in a cash crunch and staff accepted ~6-month pay delays. He mortgaged his house, put ~₹1 crore into the eGurukul app (soft-launched Dec 2019, launched 26 Jan 2020), returned to profit by July 2020, crossed ₹200 crore by 2023, and sold to Japan's M3 (via NHPL, parent of Marrow) in November 2023 — eGurukul was bought 100% upfront, DBMCI in a 51%/49% structure with the second tranche linked to EBITDA (~9.7x EBITDA on the offline business), plus a standard 3-year non-compete in medical education.

## Claims to be careful with
- The claim that a radiologist can earn ₹10 lakh/month within five years, and that 1 in 5 doctors in America is Indian, are the guest's assertions and are not independently verified in the episode.
- All salary, stipend and fee figures are the guest's estimates from his experience running a medical-education business, not official statistics.
- His characterisation of doctors as "the worst-treated community in the country" is an opinion, and his explanation for low FMGE pass rates (blaming poor-quality foreign colleges) is one side of a debated issue.

## Summary
Dr. Nachiket Bhatia breaks down the economics of becoming a doctor in India: 22 lakh aspirants chasing 1 lakh NEET-UG seats, near-worthless standalone MBBS degrees, low starting salaries even after 8 years and a PG, extreme fee disparities between government and private colleges, the USMLE escape route, the traps awaiting foreign medical graduates, and why he believes doctors are India's worst-treated professional community — before recounting how he rescued his coaching company DBMCI with the eGurukul app and sold it to Japan's M3.
