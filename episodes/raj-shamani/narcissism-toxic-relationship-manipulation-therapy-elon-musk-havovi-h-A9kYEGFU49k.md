## Important tidbits
- Psychologist vs psychiatrist: start with a psychologist/psychotherapist; a good professional will refer you to a psychiatrist if you need one, and a psychiatrist will likewise refer you for therapy. Recent loss and grief call for a therapist; not all sadness is depression, and depression needs medication only at certain levels.
- Signs of a narcissistic partner: the world revolves around them; they are volatile, with temper tantrums and anger; they can look beautiful and charming outside the home while being verbally abusive inside it; they make you believe there is no world outside them, until you start doubting your own reality, behaviour and memory.
- "The person who needs therapy won't go to therapy" — a recurring pattern she describes with narcissists.
- Narcissistic parents run the house on control dialogues — "you exist because of me, the house runs because of me, you earn because of me" — and when a child becomes independent and separates, the parent experiences it as losing control and may plot and scheme for months, including socially ostracising the child ("we raised him and he left").
- Leaving a narcissist requires planning, especially financial independence; "separation abuse" after leaving is real. Victims of narcissistic abuse live in constant hypervigilance — a jolted nervous system that cannot relax or express itself, because anything can happen at any point.
- Her core position: narcissists do not change. They thrive on apologies — forgive them and they repeat the behaviour (she credits Dr Ramani's framing). It is a personality, not a phase; the solution is distance and no contact, even though Indian culture teaches "parivaar nahi todte" (families must not break) — she counters that a relationship with no caregiving is a family only on paper.
- Children of narcissistic parents grow up constantly seeking the parent's validation: they choose careers the parent wants (the doctor's child becomes a doctor, the engineer's child an engineer) and can put their whole life on the line just to be seen — she discusses the film Animal's father–son dynamic as an illustration.
- "Hope cocaine": the hope that "tomorrow will be different" keeps victims hooked; with a narcissist, tomorrow does not change.
- Her definition of toxic: not someone who is bad to you, but someone who is bad on some days and good on others — the inconsistency makes you unable to decide whether to leave or stay, and that loop is the toxicity.
- Environment can create narcissism in children too: helicopter/snowplow parenting, constant micromanagement, never teaching a child to fail, and empty praise — she says the word "awesome" has lost its meaning because praise is no longer tied to competence and confidence-building, and resilience is falling with each generation.
- She uses Kanye West as an example of grandiose narcissism controlling a partner's life — recounting how Kim Kardashian said on her show that he threw out her wardrobe and for a period she wore only blues, whites, beige, blacks and greys of his choosing.
- Narcissistic bosses dehumanise: employees are treated as commodities, driven relentlessly, messaged at all hours, and subjected to temper tantrums (including throwing things). Her advice: distance yourself, change your boss or team if you can, and complain to HR — bullies keep going because nobody ever complains.
- Some powerful CEOs protect a "nice, empathetic" public image by hiring a gatekeeper — an abrasive mid-manager who does the dirty work — which she calls a very common pattern in large companies.
- Mood swings are a symptom, not a personality disorder in themselves: borderline personality disorder involves many mood swings, but PMS, hunger or a bad night's sleep can also cause them. Her baseline advice for everyone: sort the primitives — eating, sleeping, exercising, moving, resting.
- You always have a choice, but a narcissist makes you feel you don't. Recovery means building your own "village" of safe people and not getting isolated; courage comes first, bravery follows, and patterns are hard to break.

## Claims to be careful with
- The guest labels public figures from a distance — Elon Musk is called a "raging narcissist" and Kanye West is analysed as a narcissist; these are her opinions, not clinical diagnoses of people she has treated.
- Fictional characters (e.g., Harvey Specter) and films (Animal) are used as clinical illustrations; they are interpretive examples, not evidence.
- "Narcissists never change" is stated as an absolute; it reflects her clinical stance and Dr Ramani's school of thought rather than a universal consensus.
- Broad generational claims — that younger generations' resilience is "abysmal" and falling with every generation — are her impressions, not cited data.

## Summary
Clinical and forensic psychologist Havovi Hyderabadwala breaks down how to recognise narcissists in partners, parents and bosses: the public charm and private abuse, control dialogues, hypervigilance in victims, and "hope cocaine" that keeps people stuck. Her prescription is unsentimental — narcissists don't change, so plan your exit, build financial independence and a support "village," and go no-contact where possible — alongside a wider discussion of therapy, helicopter parenting, empty praise and why mood swings alone don't make a personality disorder.
