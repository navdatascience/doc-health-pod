## Important tidbits
- Karan Johar says he has body dysmorphia: he cannot look at himself without clothes on.
- He recounts being mocked throughout childhood for his way of speaking, walking, body language, looks and weight, and for being effeminate; his parents never made him feel different — his father celebrated everything about him and would proudly show his dancing to friends, which he calls his safe space.
- About two years after his father's death, watching Mira Nair's The Namesake at the Toronto Film Festival triggered a breakdown — he cried for roughly two hours walking back to and sitting in his hotel room, and felt as if a weight had been lifted; he says tears are a celebration of emotion, not weakness, and he is not ashamed to cry in front of his children, mother, or friends.
- He distinguishes emotion from vulnerability: he refuses to show trolls and attackers that he is affected, and says he has become numb/indifferent to hate rather than "over it," because absorbing hate daily would turn his own thoughts negative — and he wants his children to see a positive, happy father.
- His weight-loss journey began with full blood work that revealed a thyroid fluctuation which, he says, he had unknowingly lived with for 15–20 years; for years the scale would not move despite health farms and efforts, and it only moved after the thyroid levels were corrected.
- After that, he did OMAD (one meal a day) for about seven months — eating a single meal around 8–8:30 pm, with no lactose, no gluten, and no sugar, rigorously for roughly six to seven months.
- More recently (about a month and a half before recording) he deliberately put some weight back on, reintroducing a little lactose and carbohydrates in the form of rice — still no gluten — and started weight training and playing padel.
- On constant Ozempic/GLP-1 speculation in his comments, he argues by "emotional mathematics": he has never hidden the biggest truths of his life (he wrote them in his book An Unsuitable Boy), so if he had taken Ozempic or Mounjaro he would say so openly — and would even try to monetise it as a brand ambassador.
- He describes a panic/anxiety attack at a big event, which he links to the post-Covid period: his hands and feet shook, palms sweated, he felt he could not breathe and feared it was a cardiac arrest; a friend took him aside and gave him water, and the episode lasted about 5–10 minutes.
- He went to his family doctor the same night (around 11 pm), then saw a psychologist and started medication, which he still takes daily and hopes to slowly wean off; an earlier anxiety episode in 2016, he says, was connected to a broken heart.
- He now avoids parties and large social situations because of this anxiety — a major change for someone who was always a social "people person" — and links it partly to years of excessive people-pleasing catching up with him.
- His biggest professional insecurity is failing as a director: all his directorial films have been hits/profitable, and he fears the first flop; losing his father 21 years ago remains the lowest point of his life, against which trolling and stress feel trivial, and he says he never considered quitting Bollywood.
- On nepotism, he concedes that star kids get multiple opportunities after flops that outsiders do not get, but argues talent ultimately cannot be competed with — citing Alia Bhatt and Ranbir Kapoor as outstanding talents who deserve their success, alongside outsider journeys like Shah Rukh Khan and Amitabh Bachchan — and that destiny/luck (taqdeer) is a real component; he credits his own career partly to luck and to Aditya Chopra and Shah Rukh Khan.
- On his one-sided love: he says it never turned obsessive or toxic, he never wished the person ill, and he dealt with it by accepting it and writing a film, which was cathartic for him.
- He says he does not feel hatred or jealousy (he feels envy and indifference instead), rarely loses his temper — and then only over small inefficiencies at work — while staying calm in large crises.
- His one unfulfilled area, he says, is his personal life: he is not in a relationship, which he attributes to always working and to finding trust difficult given who he is.

## Claims to be careful with
- All health content in this episode is one celebrity's personal account, not medical advice: the OMAD regimen and the elimination of gluten, sugar, and lactose were his personal choices, and his thyroid treatment and daily anxiety medication were supervised by his own doctors.
- His remarks about Ozempic/Mounjaro are an argument that he would admit it if he had used them — an implication about his own case, not a verifiable claim.
- His description of a panic attack is personal experience; he himself first feared it was a cardiac arrest and sought medical evaluation the same night.
- His views on nepotism and destiny are opinions offered in an interview, not findings.

## Summary
In a wide-ranging interview, Karan Johar opens up about living with body dysmorphia, childhood bullying, and the grief breakdown that taught him to cry without shame; explains that his much-discussed weight loss followed the discovery and correction of a long-undiagnosed thyroid problem plus seven months of one-meal-a-day eating without gluten, sugar, or lactose; addresses Ozempic speculation by saying he hides nothing about his life; and describes the panic attack that led him to a psychologist and daily anxiety medication, alongside candid takes on nepotism, destiny, one-sided love, and never wanting to quit Bollywood.
