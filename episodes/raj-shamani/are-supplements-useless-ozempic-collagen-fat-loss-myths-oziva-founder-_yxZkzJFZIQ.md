## Important tidbits
- No weight-loss supplement delivers long-term benefit without lifestyle: Gadani says workout, strength training, and energy-in versus energy-out are non-negotiable, and any product that promises results with a "full licence to cheat" is misleading.
- His explanation of GLP-1 drugs: the body naturally secretes GLP-1, a hormone that signals the brain via the gut that the stomach is full; in people with diabetes or obesity this signalling is disturbed. Semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy) is, in his words, a 93–94% similar copy of that hormone, taken as a weekly injection, which effectively hacks the hunger-signal system.
- Supplements should be used to fix a root cause, not chased as fat-loss products: if someone eats like the rest of the family, works out, and still gains weight, he says the root cause may be gut dysbiosis, internal inflammation, or micronutrient deficiency — and that deficiency is the most common hidden reason he sees.
- His diagnostic route: blood tests for inflammation markers ("your blood test is your kundli," he says) and gut-microbiome stool tests, which he says are available at home for roughly ₹5,000–7,000 with reports in 8–10 days.
- From OZiva's lakhs of consultations, he claims about 70% of people's core problem is their diet; he calls Indians' biggest misconception the belief that their diet is already good, while outside/processed food quietly packs 1,200–1,500 calories that would take far more home food to reach.
- Label trap he demonstrates: a 100 g packet can headline "19 calories" in large print because that figure is for a 25 g serving noted in fine print — read the back of the pack and compare per-100 g values.
- Supplement scam he calls out: biotin gummies sold for hair growth are typically ~4 g gummies that are about 90% sugar.
- His anti-inflammatory eating framework is "FLAME": Fibre-rich food; Low-glycaemic food (e.g., khichdi, where protein and carbs go together); Antioxidants from colourful fruits and herbs like tulsi, ginger, and turmeric; Microbiome support through fermented foods like curd and buttermilk; and Essential fats (walnuts, olive oil) — good fats should not be feared.
- On gut bacteria: organisms such as Akkermansia muciniphila, Bifidobacterium animalis, and lactobacilli produce short-chain fatty acids like butyrate that support metabolism; polyphenols (for example from cinnamon) act as the "soil" that helps good bacteria grow, and microbial diversity is the goal.
- DHT blockers explained: testosterone converts to DHT (dihydrotestosterone) via the 5-alpha-reductase enzyme; excess conversion shrinks hair follicles and overactivates sebaceous glands (acne, itchy/scaly scalp). Herbal blockers act on the enzyme rather than on DHT directly; he cites OZiva's SesZen Bio (Sesbania agati leaf extract) as showing up to ~40% DHT-blocking activity in their studies, and warns hormones cause problems when either too high or too low (stamina, sexual function, hair).
- Three Indian superfood ingredients he recommends: moringa (weight management and sugar metabolism — he notes even PM Modi drinks moringa soup); cinnamon polyphenols (he cites studies of up to ~20% improvement in HbA1c and better insulin sensitivity, relevant because many Indians are unknowingly prediabetic with HbA1c above 5.8); and shatavari for women's hormonal health — he says about 30% of women go through PMS or PCOS, with mood swings, weight gain, and infertility.
- Overhyped home remedies, per Gadani: fenugreek and other hair oils are surface-level treatments that do not fix chronic problems, and honey-lemon water or cinnamon water alone will not produce weight loss — the doses are too low and unsupported by studies.
- Fat burners and fat cutters, he concedes, at best work in controlled study environments or perhaps ~5% of cases; he says the supporting studies are generally poorly designed, and such products only add incremental benefit on top of lifestyle change — there is no magic pill.
- Meal replacements have a legitimate use only when they swap calorie density for nutrient density: high protein (he suggests ~50–60% protein), high fibre, and no sugar, maltodextrin, or artificial sweeteners — helping satiety and craving control across a roughly four-hour gap.
- On collagen: it is a protein the body makes itself, present in skin, joints, nails, and hair roots, and production declines after about age 20; types 1, 2, 3, and 4 differ by structure. Most collagen products in the market are animal-based, while plant-based versions are positioned as supporting the body's own production.
- Plant-nutrition myths he addresses: individual plant proteins can be incomplete, but combining complementary sources (pea + brown rice protein + quinoa/soy) gives a complete amino-acid profile; he compares whey to a racing bike (fast but only on smooth roads) and plant blends to a hybrid SUV better suited to a disturbed gut; and "processed" is not automatically bad — what matters is the process and ingredients, with maltodextrin flagged as a high-glycaemic additive to watch for.

## Claims to be careful with
- Mihir Gadani is the co-founder of OZiva and sells several of the product categories discussed (hair vitamins with DHT blockers, moringa/ACV products, meal replacements, plant protein). Efficacy figures he cites — such as ~40% DHT-blocking activity and cinnamon's HbA1c benefits — come from studies he references, including his own company's preclinical and clinical work, and should be read as a founder's claims.
- His figure that ~70% of people's main problem is their diet comes from his company's consultation data, not published population research.
- The description of semaglutide as a "93–94% copy" of natural GLP-1 is his simplified explanation of how the drug works.
- His recommendation of gut-microbiome testing as a route to diagnosing weight-gain causes reflects his commercial and clinical viewpoint; the tests and price points are as stated by him.

## Summary
OZiva co-founder Mihir Gadani argues that supplements cannot compensate for a broken lifestyle — no fat burner, gummy, or meal replacement works long-term without exercise and dietary control — and explains how GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic mimic the body's own satiety hormone. He walks through root-cause thinking for stubborn weight gain (gut health, inflammation, micronutrient deficiency), label-reading traps and gummy scams, his FLAME anti-inflammatory framework, DHT blockers and hair fall, moringa/cinnamon/shatavari as Indian superfoods, why home remedies like honey-lemon water are overhyped, and the difference between animal collagen and plant-based alternatives.
