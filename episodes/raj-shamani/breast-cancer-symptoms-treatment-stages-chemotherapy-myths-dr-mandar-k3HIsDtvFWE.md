## Important tidbits
- The biggest misconception, per Dr Nadkarni, is "cancer = death": breast cancer is one of the most curable cancers. Cure rates by stage are roughly 90–92% for stage 1, 80–85% for stage 2, 65–70% for stage 3, and only ~10% for stage 4 — which is why catching it early matters so much.
- Breast cancer does not automatically mean removing the breast: in 75–80 of every 100 patients the breast is either conserved or immediately reconstructed to look normal.
- He stresses the disease is not the patient's fault — nothing she ate, drank or did "caused" it — and says 9 out of 10 women he treats carry this guilt, which delays them seeing a doctor.
- Cancer explained simply: a normal cell is programmed to die (apoptosis) after its lifespan and be replaced; a cancer cell "forgets to die" and keeps doubling (1→2→4→8…), forming a lump. The real danger is not the lump itself but cells that break off, travel via blood to lungs, liver, brain or bones, and destroy those organs' function — that is stage 4 and is incurable.
- Only about 2–3% of breast cancers are hereditary (e.g., BRCA1/2, as with Angelina Jolie); ~97% are lifestyle-related. BRCA genes also raise ovarian cancer risk and, mildly, large-intestine, pancreatic and prostate cancer risk in men.
- Lifetime risk for an urban Indian woman (Mumbai/Delhi) is about 1 in 25, versus 1 in 7–8 in the US and beyond 1 in 50 in rural India — he attributes the gap largely to lifestyle: rural women tend to eat simple food, do heavy physical work, have children earlier, breastfeed ~1 year per child, and don't drink alcohol.
- His lifestyle advice: keep BMI under 23.5 (risk is ~2.5× lower than at BMI above 31); 45 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous exercise with sweating, 5 days a week, can cut breast cancer risk by up to 40% by lowering ambient estrogen; limit alcohol; where possible, first baby before 25, second before 30, and breastfeed each for about a year (minimum 6 months) — though he clarifies he is not telling anyone when to marry or have children.
- Men get breast cancer too — roughly 1 man for every 200 women — because men have a small amount of rudimentary breast tissue; risk scales with the number of breast cells.
- The most common symptom is a painless lump. Painless lumps are more dangerous than painful ones: a painful lump carries under 1% cancer risk (usually abscess, mastitis or fibrocystic change), a painless lump 10–20%, blood-stained nipple discharge under 10%, nipple retraction ~5% — but he insists every abnormality should be shown to a doctor regardless of the percentages.
- Self-examination: once a month only (daily checking makes you miss gradual change), after periods end for younger women, on a fixed date for postmenopausal women; right hand for the left breast and vice versa, best lying down or in the shower with soap for glide; a proper exam takes 7–8 minutes.
- Screening schedule he recommends: self-exam from age 20; clinical breast exam from 20–25 (every 3 years until 40, yearly 40–55, every 1.5–2 years after 55); first mammography + sonography at 40 if no family history, then yearly to 55 and every 1.5–2 years to 75. With a strong family history, start mammography 10 years before the youngest first-degree relative's diagnosis age.
- Mammography radiation does not cause cancer (modern doses are tiny; screening studies showed up to ~20% mortality reduction, ~8% on re-analysis). MRI should not be used for routine screening — it is highly sensitive but poorly specific and over-diagnoses; it has only ~5 specific indications.
- A needle biopsy takes 10–12 minutes under local anaesthesia, results in 48–72 hours; patients go home within about an hour.
- Fertility after breast cancer is possible: because chemotherapy can shut down the ovaries, options include embryo preservation (best), egg freezing, ovarian tissue freezing, and ovary-suppressing injections during chemo that cut the risk of permanent infertility from ~60% to ~20%. He advises waiting at least 2 years after treatment before planning a baby because recurrence risk is highest in the first 3 years.
- Obesity raises risk mainly after menopause, and abdominal (truncal) fat is worse than hip/thigh fat because abdominal fat stores the most estrogen; long menstrual exposure (periods starting before ~11, menopause after 55) also raises risk, while early menopause lowers it.
- Alcohol is now classed as a Class 1 carcinogen — its breakdown products damage DNA and raise risk of cancers from mouth and tongue to stomach, liver and breast; the old "4–5 units a week is safe" idea no longer holds. Smoking has no proven direct link to breast cancer; there is no data yet on vaping and breast cancer.
- Treatment is multimodal: surgery and radiation treat the source locally; systemic therapy (chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hormone therapy, immunotherapy) hunts cells in the blood. Breast-conserving surgery requires ~20 radiation sessions (Mon–Fri over 4 weeks). Chemotherapy kills dividing cells, which is why it also hits the gut lining (mouth ulcers, acidity), hair follicles (hair loss) and bone marrow (low white cells → infection risk); nausea typically lasts 1–2 days after a cycle.
- Rare long-term risks exist: radiation can cause cancers in the treated field (he cites ~1 in 10,000 patients), and chemotherapy can rarely cause blood cancer — in his framing, chemo saves roughly 6,000–7,000 lives per 100,000 treated against a handful of such cases.
- Myths he busts: underwired bras do not cause breast cancer; deodorants do not cause it; breast implants do not increase risk; 8 of 10 breast lumps are benign; women under 40 can get breast cancer. On the Navjot Singh Sidhu claim crediting turmeric, neem and intermittent fasting for his wife's recovery, Dr Nadkarni notes she was simultaneously on chemotherapy and radiation, says dietary changes alone have never treated a patient in his experience, and warns such claims can mislead people away from treatment and cost lives.
- India's lower survival versus the West is driven by late diagnosis, affordability (one immunotherapy drug cost ₹96 lakh a year a year ago, ~₹15 lakh now; comparable surgery costing ~₹4 lakh in India can cost $100,000+ in the US), and health-system priorities; he names MD Anderson (Houston) as the world's biggest/best cancer centre.

## Claims to be careful with
- The specific statistics (1-in-25 urban Indian lifetime risk, up to 40% risk reduction from exercise, cure percentages per stage, ~28.1 per 100,000 annual incidence) are Dr Nadkarni's figures as stated on the podcast and are broadly consistent with the direction of published data, but exact numbers vary by source and population.
- His reproductive advice (first baby before 25, second before 30, a year of breastfeeding) is presented as risk-reduction evidence, not a prescription; individual circumstances differ and he himself adds that caveat.
- The statement that oral contraceptive pills used briefly at low dose do not raise risk, while combined hormone-replacement therapy beyond ~2 years after menopause does, reflects his summary of mixed study evidence — this remains an area of ongoing research.
- His characterisation of monthly self-examination as partly a "myth" (trials showed no mortality benefit, though it raises awareness) is a nuanced position worth noting rather than a dismissal of self-exams.

## Summary
Breast oncologist Dr Mandar Nadkarni dismantles the fear around breast cancer: it is among the most curable cancers when caught early (stage 1 cure ~90%+), the breast usually does not need to be removed, and it is almost never the patient's fault. He explains how cancer cells form and spread, why only 2–3% of cases are hereditary, how urban Indian lifestyles (weight, alcohol, late childbirth, little breastfeeding) are driving risk toward Western levels, and gives concrete screening schedules, prevention habits, treatment options from surgery to immunotherapy, fertility preservation, and a round of myth-busting — including a firm warning against crediting turmeric, neem or fasting for recoveries achieved on chemotherapy.
