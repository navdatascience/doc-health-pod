## Important tidbits
- Children repeatedly told they are "not good enough" — comments on dark skin, looks, or comparisons with a prettier/smarter sibling — grow up with low self-worth and say yes to the first partner who shows interest; she links this to many marriages at 21–22 that were never really chosen.
- Children copy their parents' relationship as programming: boys who watch fathers beat and abuse mothers normalise that behaviour; girls either repeat it or run to the polar opposite of their father — and choosing "anyone but my father" is also a wrong selection method.
- Children in toxic homes make impulsive escape decisions — eloping with the first person who offers — because chronic trauma impairs rational thought; they realise the mistake within a week, sometimes landing in drug abuse.
- Ages 0–3 are the maximum programming years. A baby's primary needs are holding, hugging, physical comfort and emotional language; parents over-invest in physical provisioning (food, facilities, tuition at age two-and-a-half) and miss emotional needs, producing anxiety, loneliness, self-doubt and low confidence.
- You cannot teach "don't hit" by slapping: two slaps buy temporary fear-based compliance but no learning. Instead, name the child's emotion, stay calm, and redirect the anger (e.g., punch a pillow). What the child actually learns is the parent's reaction to the behaviour.
- Trauma is subjective — it is not what happened to you but what happened inside you afterwards. Siblings in the same home take opposite positions (one becomes the perfectionist "golden child," the other the rebel), and each child unconsciously triggers different reactions in the parents, so no two children get the same parents.
- Her suicide research found the biggest contributors to suicidal behaviour are emotional abuse and emotional neglect — constant criticism and nagging, threats ("no food," "you won't enter the house," "the police will take you"), withholding love, and blaming the child for the parents' fights — more than physical or sexual abuse.
- She distinguishes toxic shame ("I am useless, I don't deserve to live," which shrinks and incapacitates a person) from healthy shame (which stops you doing wrong in society), and toxic fear (extreme threats) from healthy fear (don't drink and drive). Toxic shame operates in adult relationships too, e.g., partners publicly mocking a spouse's weight, hair or social awkwardness until their normal functioning collapses.
- Adults abused in childhood unconsciously attract abusive partners because abuse feels familiar and "normal"; with no training in self-respect or boundaries, they tolerate what a securely raised person would leave on day two. This is how couples stay in toxic relationships for life, feeding each other's patterns.
- Codependency: a wife whose self-worth comes from caretaking a dependent husband will unconsciously sabotage his recovery, because her value comes from being needed. Likewise, parents who keep children dependent ("my kids cannot survive without me") leave them unable to make decisions or solve problems — visible when over-protected children go abroad and break down over groceries, dishes and laundry.
- Trust and abandonment issues begin when children are beaten unpredictably or emotionally ignored: the brain stays hypervigilant, the amygdala (fear centre) becomes hyperactive and the calming parts underdevelop. The stress starts in infancy — a baby whose wet diaper or hunger goes unanswered learns nobody is coming.
- For a child mid-tantrum, she advises: sit down, hold the child's hand, rub their back — a dysregulated nervous system settles this way most of the time (she says ~90%), whereas lecturing or shaming escalates it. Emotional neglect — parents too preoccupied with work, marital or in-law stress to read the child's cues — is abandonment even in an intact home.
- Resilience can be built: perceived social support, one "buffering adult" (like a loving grandmother), passions and hobbies, and letting children make mistakes and solve their own problems are major contributors to emotional resilience.
- Self-harm (mostly pre-teens and teens, and different from suicide) has several causes: releasing bottled-up pain and anger the child was never allowed to express, feeling alive when trauma has left them numb and dissociated, attention-seeking, and increasingly peer pressure; it can become addictive, like a pressure-cooker whistle.
- A baby never solves a marital problem — it adds stress (sleepless nights, postpartum depression, fights over parenting styles), and the child grows up believing it must fix the parents' marriage. Her direct advice: "If your marriage is not working, please do not have kids." She calls the parallel belief that marriage can "cure" homosexuality equally destructive.
- Using "trauma" (or ADHD) as a permanent excuse — a puppet-master to blame instead of taking ownership — blocks healing, as does only blaming parents. Healing works level by level: regulate the nervous system (deep breathing, pranayama, chanting, humming — she cites her own published work that 10 minutes of daily humming lowers blood pressure, and a study where 5 minutes of coherent breathing cut domestic violence by ~50%), then correct false belief systems, then learn to manage emotions.
- Psychiatrist vs psychologist: psychiatrists are medical doctors who prescribe; psychologists assess; the missing middle is psychotherapy — inner-child integration, trauma-informed care, memory reconsolidation and EMDR — which she considers the most effective route for trauma. She argues benzodiazepines are poorly effective for PTSD relative to side effects, and cites recent research questioning the serotonin theory of depression.
- Her machine-learning research with IIT colleagues combines childhood-trauma scores with perceived social support and loneliness to predict suicide risk; crucially, high trauma alone does not lead to suicide when emotional resilience is high and depression and loneliness are absent.
- Forced forgiveness doesn't work: forgiving intellectually or spiritually ("it was his karma, I have forgiven him") leaves unprocessed anger stored as body/implicit memory that erupts years later. Real forgiveness comes after the emotional charge is discharged in therapy (e.g., imagery-based "relationship drama," grief work), because roughly 95% of behaviour runs on automatic body programs, not logic (her framing).
- Domestic violence flows in every direction: mothers who are themselves victims displace their frustration onto the most helpless target — their children (she cites US research that ~80% of child abuse is by parents). Violent wives are increasingly common in her practice, and abused men stay silent from shame, stay for the children, or lack the self-worth to say stop, coping instead through alcoholism, workaholism, porn or shopping addiction, or building an "alternate family" elsewhere.
- About 70% of her clients are women and 30% men; men avoid therapy because vulnerability clashes with masculine conditioning ("a man must not feel pain, must not ask for help") — and she believes if men took help, fewer women and children would need to.
- Cheating, in her clinical experience, usually follows unmet needs: when a couple has had no sexual intimacy for 10–15 years, she expects an outside attachment nine times out of ten. She has never seen an open marriage work for any client — possessiveness inevitably complicates it.
- Signs of couples who stay functional for decades: realistic expectations (if three of five boxes are ticked, compromise on two), something genuinely common (work, passion, hobby, purpose), trust, sexual compatibility, honest communication — including calmly discussing what triggered a partner instead of avoiding topics out of fear — and giving each other space, because nobody owns another human being, not even their own child.
- Parenting balance: follow an 80/20 rule — let eight things go, stay firm on two. Saying yes to everything creates selfish, entitled children with no coping skills or resilience. Mothers who stake all validation on husband and children often fall into depression at the empty-nest stage; she urges mothers to keep an hour for their own hobby and identity.
- If forced to choose, she prioritises mental fitness over physical fitness — the body is the slave of the mind — while stressing they are inseparable: lifestyle choices (poor sleep, stressful job, binge eating, no exercise) are the biggest contributors to mental-health problems. She explains psychoneuroimmunology: chronic childhood stress imbalances the HPA axis, inflames and compromises the immune system, and is linked to autoimmune disorders.

## Claims to be careful with
- Many figures are quoted from studies she references without on-screen citations: ~80% of child abuse being committed by parents, a tantrum settling ~90% of the time with touch, ~50% reduction in domestic violence from five minutes of coherent breathing, and the study figures of roughly 3x heart disease, 2x lung cancer and ~20 years lower life expectancy after severe childhood trauma. Treat these as her cited research.
- Her dismissal of the serotonin theory of depression and of benzodiazepines for PTSD reflects her therapeutic position and selected research, presented as settled in the episode.
- Memory reconsolidation/RTM and EMDR are described as the most effective trauma techniques, and claims that ~90% of clients report change in one session are her practice's own assertions.
- The "95% of behaviour is automatic, logic runs only 10–15%" split is a simplifying framework she uses, not a measured quantity.
- Her statement that women are more prone to trauma-linked autoimmune issues because they have fewer socially accepted coping outlets is offered as her hypothesis ("I don't know why" is partly conceded in the episode).

## Summary
Psychotherapist Riri Trivedi explains how childhood emotional abuse and neglect — criticism, threats, withheld love, unpredictable or preoccupied parents — programme low self-worth, trust and abandonment issues, codependency and partner choices in adult life, and why the same home produces opposite siblings. She argues a baby cannot fix a marriage, that "trauma" must not become a lifelong excuse, and that healing requires regulating the nervous system, revising beliefs and reprocessing body memories (memory reconsolidation, EMDR) rather than medication or talk alone — while resilience, social support and one buffering adult can protect even a badly traumatised child.
