## Important tidbits
- PM2.5 particles (2.5 micrometres and smaller, down to ~0.03 microns) are so small they cross from the lungs into the bloodstream and reach every organ — brain, heart, liver, kidneys and the reproductive system — and particulate matter has been found in the placenta, correlating with lower birth weight in babies.
- He frames Delhi's air starkly: a child born in Delhi is effectively "smoking 20 cigarettes" on its first day just by breathing; a Mumbai newborn "smokes" about 4–5.
- We breathe about 11,000 litres of air a day (versus 2–4 litres of water and ~500 g of food), and he says an average Indian inhales up to 15 kg of dust and pollutants a year — yet people filter their water and food, not their air.
- The WHO classifies particulate matter as a Class 1 carcinogen and recommends annual average exposure stay below 5 µg/m³; he notes Mumbai residents breathe ~50 µg/m³ air all year without realising it.
- Indian lungs are, per a study he cites, 10–15% smaller and weaker than Western lungs — he links this to India's poor Olympic performance and argues fixing air quality would improve sports performance.
- Lungs have roughly 80% more capacity than needed for a sedentary life, so pollution damage accumulates silently — by the time symptoms appear, most of the reserve is gone and no purifier can undo it.
- Clean air is about 20.9% oxygen, 0.04% carbon dioxide and ~78–79% nitrogen; he divides air pollution into three buckets: particulate matter (PM10, PM2.5, PM1), gases such as CO2, and VOCs.
- Almost all PM2.5 comes from combustion — vehicles, industry, and in Delhi winters, guards burning rubber, wood or coal to stay warm, trapped near the ground by temperature inversion.
- CO2 builds up in closed bedrooms: his own bedroom hit ~2,750–2,800 ppm before he intervened; the target he uses is under 800 ppm. High CO2 "slows the brain down" — it is why people wake groggy, hit the snooze button and feel tired despite 8–10 hours of sleep; he compares sleeping in a badly ventilated closed bedroom to drinking two beers at night.
- A study he cites links high CO2, VOC and PM2.5 levels to lower test scores in students; since keeping his bedroom CO2 under 800 all night, he says he has not needed an alarm clock for eight years and wakes up energetic.
- Houseplants are not a substitute for a purifier: plants absorb CO2 and release oxygen but do very little for PM2.5, and dust blocks the stomata (pores) on their leaves — he had 14 plants in his room and CO2 only fell to ~2,750. Leaves must be wiped regularly for plants to work at all.
- Kitchens should be negatively pressurised so air always flows out; standard chimney hoods and the inline exhaust fans designers prefer are inadequate for heavy Indian frying — he says inline fans deliver only ~10% of the capacity of a proper wall exhaust fan.
- Three things go wrong with poor kitchen ventilation: odours spread through the whole house, the person cooking is exposed far more, and lingering odours react with other gases over time to become more harmful, also affecting stored food like bread and vegetables.
- He names Ghaziabad among India's worst-air cities, with Agra and Gwalior rotating at the top, and Byrnihat in the North East as one of the world's most polluted towns; he cites a Lancet figure of India losing ~$150 billion a year to air pollution and says Delhi residents lose ~9 years of life expectancy on average.
- On solutions, he describes fresh-air machines with filters that lasted 1–2 years in Delhi conditions (the first filter is washable every 2–3 months), and BreatheEasy's made-in-India "Vayu" system at roughly ₹1 lakh for a whole apartment, plus a planned "clean air as a service" monthly subscription.

## Claims to be careful with
- The "20 cigarettes on day one" equivalence for Delhi newborns is a rhetorical framing from his company's advertising, not a literal clinical measurement.
- He presents air pollution as a major driver of India's rising infertility/IVF rates; the episode asserts "clear science" but cites no specific study, and he acknowledges other lifestyle factors exist.
- The economic figures (e.g., $150 billion/year, $887 billion) and the "9 years of life lost in Delhi" statistic are quoted loosely from memory without sources shown.
- The guest sells air-purification products (IQAir distribution and BreatheEasy's own systems) and the episode promotes them, including pricing — his product claims (e.g., filter life, what the Vayu unit can and cannot handle) are a vendor's, not independently verified.
- The claim that high bedroom CO2 is "as bad as drinking two beers" and that pollution directly causes diabetes, Alzheimer's and stroke is stated more definitively than the episode's evidence supports.
