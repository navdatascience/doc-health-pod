## Important tidbits
- India's diabetes risk is partly epigenetic: children of parents who lived through famines (e.g., the Bengal famine, wartime food policies) express genes that store fat readily — an average-looking Indian can be at higher diabetes risk than an obese American, and even a slight dietary imbalance plus no exercise tips Indians over.
- Elaborate cuisines historically come from inequality: royal kitchens (Akbar's recipe book) existed because the rich could pay many cooks; the demand for fresh, tawa-hot food three times a day similarly rests on gender inequality — rice cultures needed far less kitchen labour than roti cultures, and women in rice-growing regions entered school/literacy earlier.
- The typical urban Indian diet's core problem is too little protein; US and Indian diabetes rates are broadly similar, Europe/Mediterranean (Spain, Italy, Greece) is healthier, and Indian school tiffins of fresh food compare well against processed US public-school lunches.
- Fridge myth debunked: refrigeration barely reduces nutrition — maximum nutrient destruction happens during cooking, not cooling. Macros, minerals and fat-soluble vitamins don't change in the fridge; only some vitamin B/C and spice antioxidants dip slightly. Food keeps safely about a week in a properly cold fridge, and leftovers are healthier than ordering out — restaurant dal can carry ~600 calories vs ~200 at home because of added fat, and a leftover plate is usually more diverse.
- Microwaves do NOT cause cancer: microwave radiation is non-ionising, lower in energy than visible light, and cannot alter DNA — the sun's UV is the bigger cancer risk, yet people skip sunscreen. For reheating, the microwave actually destroys the fewest micronutrients versus stovetop reheating; induction is the most energy-efficient cooking method (water boils in ~50% less time) but needs magnetic cookware (iron, steel, cast iron — not aluminium).
- Non-stick (PTFE/Teflon) cookware: the coating is chemically inert — the same material is used on heart stents precisely because the body doesn't react to it; swallowed flakes pass through harmlessly. The real harms were to factory workers and local water during manufacture, and fumes only if you burn an empty pan at very high temperature. In his risk hierarchy, smoking, drinking and overall diet dwarf non-stick and microwave worries; no country's health advisory bans non-stick.
- There are no superfoods and no super-villains: turmeric is fine in food, but 500 g of turmeric holds only ~1 g of curcumin, and turmeric water three times a day can damage the liver and thin the blood. Every spice is a plant poison evolved against insects/microbes — clove oil is a toothache painkiller but toxic in excess, and ~5 g of nutmeg can poison you (it is traditionally used in tiny amounts in milk to make children sleepy). Normal home cooking doses are the right range.
- "Detox" is a scam word: only the liver and kidneys detox; lemon/coriander detox waters do nothing — the benefit people feel comes from eating less and simpler for a few days, giving those organs rest. Fasting beyond 24–48 hours should be medically supervised, especially for diabetics. Likewise you cannot "boost" immunity with food or supplements; immunity rises from actual infection or vaccination — a balanced diet, calorie control and exercise just keep the system healthy.
- Spinach: Popeye was an anaemia-era propaganda cartoon. Excess spinach raises kidney-stone risk (oxalates); every food has a dose curve — none is benefit-only. Plant iron absorption is the real anaemia issue for vegetarians.
- Vegetarians can complete protein via dairy/egg (complete animal protein) or processed soy/plant-protein combinations, but vitamin B12 must be supplemented on a fully plant-based diet; he pushes back on activists demanding universal veganism as unaffordable and impractical for most Indians.
- Lactose intolerance is largely genetic geography: the lactose-tolerance gene came with Central Asian migrations ~4,000 years ago, so ~70% of North/West Indians are tolerant while ~70% of South Indians are intolerant (with wide caste/community variation, and it can appear at any age). North India's dairy culture exists because flat river plains supported large pastures; the Deccan couldn't. He argues intolerance rates haven't changed — awareness has.
- Insects are among the highest-quality protein sources (~100% protein, low carbon footprint); winged termites fried with garlic and curry leaves are eaten in Tamil Nadu, and Western startups now make insect protein powders.
- His closing line: you can even eat Maggi and be healthy — just not daily; add vegetables and an egg. Don't believe oversimplified food rules (e.g., "never salt with curd" — South India eats salted curd dishes and has higher life expectancy than the North).

## Claims to be careful with
- The specific percentages (~70% lactose tolerance/intolerance by region, restaurant dal at 3× calories, ~1 g curcumin per 500 g turmeric) are his stated figures from the conversation, not cited studies.
- His reassurance on non-stick cookware addresses the end-user risk; the manufacturing/PFAS pollution concern he acknowledges is a separate, live debate.
- The epigenetics/famine explanation for Indian diabetes risk is a real research area but he presents a simplified version of it.
- His dismissal of "detox" and "immunity-boosting" products is a position against marketing claims — individual products were not tested on the show.

## Summary
Food-science author Krish Ashok dismantles Indian kitchen anxieties one by one: fridge food loses almost no nutrition, microwaves can't cause cancer and preserve nutrients best for reheating, non-stick coatings are inert (the same material coats heart stents), turmeric and other spices are dose-dependent plant poisons rather than superfoods, "detox" and "immunity boosting" are marketing scams, and lactose intolerance is ancient migration genetics rather than a new epidemic. India's real dietary problem, he argues, is low protein layered on famine-shaped epigenetics — and even Maggi is fine occasionally with vegetables and an egg.
