## Important tidbits
- His central distinction: hospitals do a great job of extending lifespan, but nobody measures healthspan. His goal is to increase healthspan — the years lived active and independent — not lifespan.
- He coined "IDC", the Invisible Decline Curve, for decline in parents that children cannot spot. Its three signs: (1) confidence drops — "I can't do this anymore" about things they used to do; (2) decision-making goes — they can't decide even small purchases or whether to start a medicine without asking their children; (3) giving up — "my time is done, I'm ready to go," waking up with no plan for the day. By the time all three are visible, a sudden event (a fall, a major illness) changes life permanently.
- Forgetting something like whether they had breakfast can be stage 1 of dementia, he says; in his communities they use puzzles, games, activities and being among people to try to hold memory loss at stage 1 instead of dismissing it at home as normal ageing.
- The "great nurse" paradox: a nurse (or family) who does everything for parents makes their life "easy" but hyper-dependent, and unknowingly accelerates decline. In his communities the staff philosophy is the opposite — "we are here if you need us; do your own things yourself" — because doing things yourself is what keeps you capable.
- What people around 60–65 want most, he says, is companionship, agency and independence — the recurring line is "I don't want to be a burden on my child." He calls retirement itself "the biggest disease": doing nothing, forwarding WhatsApp messages and watching TV is the worst pattern.
- The number one reason for ageing in the world, in his framing, is lack of purpose. Politicians, he argues, live the longest because they unknowingly do the anti-ageing things: they are physically active, mentally stimulated at all times, have an insanely high sense of purpose, and are surrounded by people.
- Blue Zones — Sardinia (Italy), Loma Linda (California), Okinawa (Japan), Ikaria (Greece) — have the highest numbers of fully independent centenarians despite different genetics, food and weather. The common factors he lists from Ikigai research: people don't retire (high purpose), strong community, mental activity, movement built into daily life, and being surrounded by nature. He cites research that Ikigai-style principles alone can increase healthspan by about five years.
- Okinawa's "Hara Hachi Bu": eat until you are 80% full, eat slowly, and eat with your "moai" — a core group of friends — sitting together for 15–30 minutes. He notes Ayurveda's Mitahara says the same: half the stomach solid food, one quarter liquid, one quarter left empty.
- Loneliness, he says, is the number one reason for death in the world — it just never appears on a death certificate. Per the research discussed in the episode, chronic loneliness is as harmful as smoking 15 cigarettes a day, raises early-death risk by about 26%, and increases the risks of dementia, heart attack and type-2 diabetes; someone lonely for over five years has roughly a 76% chance of developing a chronic disease.
- How to spot loneliness: a person who always seems disconnected, answers in a single word or line, talks to nobody, stays inside their room or in a device, and comes out only for meals — even inside a joint family. Physical togetherness is not the same as meaningful conversation.
- Falls are the second biggest cause of early mortality in older people: about 70% of falls happen in bathrooms, and about 70% of those at night. Fixes he describes: a bedside sensor that switches on a dim bathroom light the moment feet touch the floor, grab bars, hand showers (elders dislike wetting their hair daily, so rain showers are a poor fit) and a sturdy shower chair.
- Marble is, in his words, the worst flooring for elders — it gets cold, is slippery, and water is invisible on it; Indian stones like Kadapa and Shahabad are safer. Also: remove raised door thresholds and balcony sliding-track bumps, choose fabric sofas over hard wooden handles, avoid sharp table corners (curve them or add rubber beading), and be careful with carpets, which obstruct walking sticks and wheelchairs. Kitchen and wardrobe hardware should bring items down to standing height so elders never climb stools or tiptoe.
- The Primus model: standalone senior-living and multi-generational communities for roughly the 55–75 age group, designed so a home has three jobs — prevent falls, manage emergencies better, and push people out of their flats into common areas (e.g., the restaurant is placed so residents cross indoor games, outdoor games and the medical centre to reach it). Services include health, nutrition, concierge, housekeeping and events. His other venture, Marzi, retrofits existing homes to be elder-friendly. He says he is building about 3,000 homes across six cities.
- 98% of his sales are self-funded by the elders themselves; children's role is mostly to vet the company. He also cites his tracked data that daughters are the primary caregivers — they initiate, follow up and call the most — and elder-abuse figures he quotes: sons account for about 52–64% of abuse, daughters-in-law about 34%, daughters under 20%, with verbal abuse the most common form.
- On India's guilt: children who live apart are not considered "good kids," and kids overcompensate guilt by giving parents money instead of time. He says senior living is undergoing the same normalisation love marriages and IVF did — from "old-age home/abandonment" to a lifestyle choice — and that uptake correlates with the elder's education level (graduates above 60), which is why demand is currently stronger in South India.
- Grey divorce is rising: he cites a roughly 40% increase in divorces among people over 50 in India, alongside "silent divorces," and growing senior singles clubs for the 50+ — elders, he notes, want love and companionship no differently from the young.

## Claims to be careful with
- "Loneliness is the number one reason for death in the world" is the guest's framing; loneliness is not an official cause-of-death category, as he himself notes.
- The claim that politicians are the longest-lived profession is his assertion based on observation, not cited data.
- Statistics quoted in the episode — 26% higher early-death risk from loneliness, ~76% chronic-disease chance after 5+ years of loneliness, elder-abuse percentages (sons 52–64%, daughters-in-law ~34%, daughters under 20%), ~70% of falls in bathrooms, and a ~40% rise in over-50 divorces — are presented by the guest/host from research they reference loosely and are not independently verified here.
- The "healthspan increases by five years from Ikigai principles" figure is attributed by the guest to research without a specific citation.

## Summary
Adarsh Narahari, founder of Primus Senior Living, argues India should obsess over healthspan rather than lifespan. He describes the "Invisible Decline Curve" children miss in their parents — fading confidence, lost decision-making, and giving up — and says well-meaning over-care makes elders dependent and speeds decline. Drawing on Blue Zones and Ikigai (purpose, community, mental activity, daily movement, nature), he treats loneliness as the world's biggest hidden killer and falls as the second, and lays out practical fixes: fall-proof homes without marble or raised thresholds, night-time bathroom lighting, designs that push elders into shared spaces, and letting parents stay independent, surrounded by people, with their bucket lists ticked.
