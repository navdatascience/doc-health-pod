## Important tidbits
- Skincare by age: in your 20s, keep it basic — face wash, moisturiser, sunscreen (a vitamin C serum is optional if you have no acne) — and lifestyle matters most. In the late 20s–30s the ~28-day collagen cycle slows, so add peptides (she prefers them because not everyone tolerates retinol). Retinol can be introduced slowly from the early-to-mid 30s and is the best anti-ageing ingredient if your skin tolerates it — she is 49 and does not use it herself because it irritates her skin. After 40, menopausal changes raise the need for hydration; use more hydrators, limit retinol or switch to gentler liposomal formulations. She rates the host's skin 6–7 out of 10: great features, but some pigmentation, uneven tone and pores, fixable with routine first, then minor treatments.
- Men age slower than women skin-wise, she says, because women go through far more hormonal changes (puberty, pregnancy, menopause, PCOS), while men's collagen bundles stay thicker and more compact and their oil-gland activity actually preserves the skin. Men's skin is also thicker with more sun exposure. Her bar for male patients is low: if a man applies even just sunscreen, that is the best anti-ageing — "something is better than nothing".
- Skin-lightening lasers: the Q-switched laser works on a nanosecond, photoacoustic principle — it physically breaks pigment into pieces the body then clears. Expect 3–4 sessions, roughly once every 15 days, costing about ₹5,000–7,000 per session depending on the doctor and machine. Pico lasers are the picosecond upgrade (like a newer iPhone), but she says India lacks true pico machines and Q-switch is good enough for pigmentation; pico is mainly suggested for tattoo removal. Side effects depend on the operator — wrong parameters can burn, worsen pigmentation or scar — and overdoing any treatment (as people did with peels) makes skin sun-sensitive.
- Microneedling radiofrequency (MNRF) combines controlled micro-injury — which triggers the skin's natural "rebuild" response — with heat energy (~30–40°C) that tightens and restarts slowed skin. A key advantage for Indian skin: it is "colour-blind", so unlike lasers there is no worry of it increasing pigmentation. She has used it for over 10 years, including on herself.
- Collagen products, honestly assessed: collagen creams do not rebuild collagen — the molecules are too heavy; they only moisturise. Oral collagen is just amino acids/protein; the stomach breaks it down without knowing it is "for skin", so it can help hydration and dry skin (she prescribes it for that) but will not erase lines or pores, and there are not enough studies. Collagen is actually built by diet, exercise, diligent sunscreen, and cutting down smoking, alcohol and sugar.
- Masks to never buy: sheet masks (fibre-based, the hydration sits on the surface and washes off, plus environmental waste), charcoal peel-off masks (they strip oil so aggressively the skin can tear), and viral oddities like bird-poop masks (popular in Japan) and menstrual-blood masks (bacteria and fungi — real infection risk, unlike clinical PRP). What she does prescribe: clay masks from good brands, patch-tested first, matched to skin type.
- Lip care: lips lack a normal epidermal layer, so lip masks hydrate and protect but cannot remove pigment; even lasers only help lip pigment somewhat. Lip scrubs are a no — any physical scrub creates micro-tears and friction, which makes Indian skin pigment more. Lip oils are good if they suit you, but her first recommendation is plain ghee from home. Beware "toothpaste dermatitis": the flavours, menthol and strong ingredients in regular toothpaste can strip the lip skin and cause dryness, darkening, itching and cracks. Lips also need SPF. Dark lips can come from vitamin B12 deficiency or lip products themselves; the fix is to remove the cause, use SPF lip balm, a night lip mask for hydration, then in-clinic options like lightening lasers and lip boosters.
- Sunscreen done right: use the correct quantity — two finger-lengths. Reapplication over makeup is possible: load sunscreen on a compact puff, blend it over, then re-powder; or use makeup that itself contains SPF and double-layer (she sometimes applies two sunscreens plus SPF makeup). If you can, apply sunscreen only on reaching the office after a splash. Oral sunscreens also exist — antioxidants that cut down free-radical damage.
- Why we tan: there is an immediate tan — melanin forms a protective "umbrella" when UV hits, and it is normally reversible — and a late tan, where pigment persists and keeps building, which is very common in Indian skin (European skin tends to burn and recover). The effect is cumulative: patients show up 10 years later with pigmentation from sun they took a decade ago. On studio and screen light, she says the evidence is still on the fence, but continuous exposure to strong visible/blue light appears to affect skin comparably to UV in her experience.
- Face, body, hand and foot creams are not interchangeable: skin thickness and keratin differ by site, so an eye cream will not penetrate palms and a strong hand cream can react on thin eye skin. Foot creams need bond-dissolving ingredients — urea, salicylic acid, glycolic acid — to penetrate thick soles, which is why plain body cream will not heal cracked heels. Her cracked-heel protocol: wear slippers at home, reduce work in water, wear sneakers or covered shoes outside, get regular (even monthly, at-home) pedicures so dead skin does not build up, and apply foot cream; for psoriasis-type feet, moisturiser sealed under cling film overnight pushes the effect deeper. In humid weather, balance it: antifungal dusting powder before socks for the office, moisturiser at night, and breaks from constant socks to avoid fungal infection.
- Niacinamide is, in her phrase, the "Kader Khan" of skincare — a brilliant supporting actor, not the hero. It boosts ceramides (the skin barrier's gatekeepers, alongside lipids and fats), calms the inflammatory cycle, and helps itching, rashes and acne; it works best as an adjunct that makes other treatments (benzoyl peroxide, pigmentation creams) perform better. Use 2–5% — higher percentages can themselves irritate.
- Combination disasters to avoid: vitamin C with retinol at the same time (both acidic/exfoliating — use vitamin C in the morning, retinol at night); benzoyl peroxide with vitamin C or with niacinamide (the pairing can negate the effect and irritate); retinol with AHAs/BHAs on the same day (alternate days is fine). Gentle ingredients — niacinamide, hyaluronic acid, glutathione-based products — can be layered together, and vitamin C + vitamin E is a synergistic pair. Also avoid stacking the same ingredient across your moisturiser, sunscreen and serum — the build-up irritates.
- "Skincare overload" is real: layering 10 products with different pHs breaks the skin's barrier "gate", letting pollution, infection and sun sensitivity in while water and lipids escape — causing acne, rashes, eczema, faster ageing and more pigmentation, especially in humid Indian weather where heavy layering clogs skin. The skin only needs protect and repair: sunscreen to protect, cleanser and moisturiser to repair; then add one targeted active per concern (vitamin C for sun exposure, benzoyl peroxide for acne, kojic acid/arbutin for pigmentation). The same caution applies to Korean skincare: it is formulated for a dry climate and different genetics, so its hydration principles suit dry Indian skin, but 10-layer routines will flare oily, acne-prone skin.
- The next big trend, she says, is regenerative medicine — using your own body's material to repair skin: PRP-style growth factors extracted from your blood and put back into skin or hair, and exosomes (tiny cell vesicles carrying RNA and growth factors). In her clinic's 6–8 months of use, exosomes have improved melasma and pigmentation — sometimes visibly after one treatment — and helped eczema, atopic dermatitis, rashes, acne and anti-ageing. DNA-based customised skincare exists but she doubts how far it has succeeded, and on PRP-derived serums from startups her verdict is "too soon": no marked difference over her tried-and-tested retinol, so the policy is wait and watch without abandoning the old.
- Expensive does not mean better: a ₹25,000 moisturiser versus a basic pharmacy brand is mostly brand value and social-media flaunting; the effective ingredients (ceramides, glycerin, panthenol) are in the affordable one. Some costly products do justify price through research, raw-material quality, stability and years of regulatory approvals — but a basic face wash or a good sunscreen need not cost much, and good products exist at ₹500–700. On DIY trends: rice-water toner is fine for dry, sensitive skin but should be skipped by acne-prone, oily skin, and she advises against homemade versions (skin pH is ~5.5; home preparations risk wrong pH and adulterated ingredients) — use a properly formulated bottled product. Coffee-and-sugar lip scrubs are useless for lip colour: pigment sits in the deep basal layer where scrubs cannot reach, and the granules traumatise skin that pigments in response to trauma.
- Her closing point — the most misunderstood thing about skin: people treat it as simple and fixable with one cream, but skin is a complex, dynamic organ; a single rash can have ten possible diagnoses, so she refuses to treat from photos. Skin tuberculosis and leprosy, she notes, are often diagnosed very late precisely because people keep self-treating what looks like an ordinary rash with creams.

## Claims to be careful with
- The exosome results (melasma and pigmentation improving after a single treatment) are her own clinic's early experience over 6–8 months; exosome therapies remain largely unapproved and unregulated in many jurisdictions, and controlled evidence is limited.
- Her suggestion that continuous studio/screen light affects skin "equivalent to UV" is explicitly contested — she acknowledges studies disagree.
- Oral "sunscreens" are antioxidants; they do not replace topical sunscreen, a point she herself maintains by stressing topical use.
- The efficacy figures are conversational hyperbole at times (microneedling "works 1101%"), and home remedies like ghee for lips reflect her practice preference rather than trial evidence.
- The episode is part of a series presented with Cetaphil, a brand she recommends by name in the conversation, so product mentions carry a sponsorship context.

## Summary
Dermatologist Dr Madhuri Agarwal cuts through viral skincare trends with a barrier-first philosophy: match your routine to your age (basics in your 20s, peptides in your 30s, cautious retinol, hydration after 40), never buy sheet, charcoal, bird-poop or menstrual-blood masks, skip lip scrubs in favour of ghee and SPF, beware toothpaste as a hidden cause of dark lips, apply two finger-lengths of sunscreen and reapply it even over makeup, avoid disastrous ingredient pairings and 10-step overload, and treat hyped frontiers — collagen creams, exosomes, DNA-based products — with a wait-and-watch eye, because skin is a complex organ that cannot be diagnosed from a photo.
