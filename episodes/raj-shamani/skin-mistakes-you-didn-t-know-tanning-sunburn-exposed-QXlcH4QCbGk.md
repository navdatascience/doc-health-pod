## Important tidbits
- Her definition of healthy skin: not itchy, not red, with a maintained water–oil balance — glow and fairness are not the measure.
- "Sensitive" skin is something you are born with; "sensitized" skin is acquired through sun exposure, chemicals and hormonal changes. She cites roughly 70% of women and 50% of men abroad as having sensitive skin.
- Everyday triggers she names: UVB sunburn; air-conditioning dehydrating skin (office workers should moisturize at least twice a day); hard-water mineral deposits causing allergies; spicy food worsening rosacea and redness; and salon blackhead removal physically damaging skin.
- Melanin mechanics: fair skin tends to burn in the sun, while more-pigmented skin tans instead — tanning is the skin's defence response, not a health sign.
- Regional patterns: coastal humidity drives fungal infections, while North Indian winters drive dryness and sensitivity; genetics strongly predict your skin type.
- She warns India's fairness obsession fuels glutathione demand and steroid-cream misuse: per studies she cites, about 10 days of continuous steroid use on the face thins the skin permanently, and stopping brings acne, facial hair and severe flare-ups.
- Barrier repair centres on ceramide moisturizers — ceramides are the "cement" between skin-cell "bricks." Avoid non-prescribed steroids; check ingredient names ending in "-sone" (e.g., betamethasone, mometasone).
- Retinoids speed up cell turnover and are preventive rather than curative; she advises starting them only in the late 20s and with guidance — and no cream can actually remove established wrinkles.
- She argues sulfates and silicones are not inherently bad and that "clean beauty" is largely a marketing scam; historically, she notes, the term "sensitive skin" traces to reactions to PABA in sunscreens around 1970.
- DIY fruit masks disturb the skin's pH and microbiome and cause irritation — do not apply fruit directly to the face. She also warns against rural practices like applying lime (chuna) to moles or tying hair around skin tags, which cause scars and infection.
- Environment matters: air purifiers help skin, and Punjab's stubble burning is increasing atopic dermatitis and eczema in children.
- Her closing protocol: avoid your triggers, use a ceramide moisturizer, wear sunscreen, manage thyroid/hormonal/stress factors, hydrate, and cut back on spicy food, alcohol and smoking.
- Note: the episode is part of a Cetaphil-sponsored sensitive-skin series, which she discloses in conversation.

## Claims to be careful with
- The figures she cites (70%/50% sensitive-skin prevalence abroad; ~10 days of facial steroid use causing permanent thinning) are stated from studies she references but are not shown or linked in the episode.
- Her dismissal of "clean beauty" and defence of sulfates/silicones is her professional opinion, and the episode sits inside a sponsored series for a skincare brand, so product-adjacent advice should be read with that context.

## Summary
Dermatologist Dr Manjot Marwah separates born-sensitive skin from acquired, sensitized skin, walks through the everyday triggers Indians overlook — sun, AC, hard water, spicy food, salon extractions and steroid creams — and lands on a simple barrier-first routine built on ceramide moisturizers, sunscreen and trigger avoidance.
