## Important tidbits
- He describes himself as "excited, insecure, confident, loud, sensitive": confidence, he says, is gut instinct, and insecurity is what arrives afterwards when he starts overthinking.
- His biggest fear is wanting to be loved by everyone in the room — he calls it "an entertainer's curse."
- He grew up in the family guitar business, and describes his parents as two people who "loved and hurt each other harder" than anyone he has known.
- A formative wound: a teacher publicly shamed him in front of the school for wearing nail polish — he says public humiliation rooted in fear of the unknown is the most damaging thing you can do to a child, and that his art comes from pain and rejection.
- His turning point came on his first European tour in Amsterdam, meeting five fans late at night: he decided the project was about community, not about him — "in a pack we become fearless." He had roughly 10,000 Instagram followers at the time and about 2 million a year later.
- His creative rule: the best art is made selfishly, and an artist owes the world only the truth. Until his latest album at 27, he admits he made decisions so that people wouldn't leave.
- On relationships, he advocates radical honesty — "the best relationship is when two people are selfish": each person states what they want and they meet in the middle; the worst relationships, he says, are simply bad communication.
- His own family aired its dirty laundry through his songs and, after the honesty, had its best Christmas together — because unresolved issues disguise themselves as arguments about something else.
- Anger, in his metaphor, is a battery bar overflowing with unaddressed energy; boxing empties it. His trainer, welterweight champion Chris Van Heerden, taught him that in the ring you get knocked out the moment you get angry. Therapy worked for his father, who had anger issues; sport is what worked for him.
- After his third album he felt lost and used food and alcohol as control — eating until he was sick and drinking to blackout. His grandmother died of alcoholism. He says change has to start with yourself, and notes he has never been into drugs.
- He is a self-described "mommy's boy" who calls his mother when anxiety hits before big gigs.
- On body image: a long-standing insecurity about his body — going shirtless on the last tour was a deliberate battle, because "we judge ourselves harsher than the world does."
- Online hate, he says, is people projecting their own pain onto a mirror, made consequence-free by the internet.
- On identity and masculinity: Gen Z A/B-tests identity like a brand; he was himself at 18, lost it around 21–25 under the world's shouting, and resettled around 26½–27. Masculinity to him means freedom of expression — wear a skirt on Monday, box on Tuesday — and for 13-year-old boys caught between Tate-style and anti-men narratives, his answer is to lead with love.
- On fame's cost: the loneliest point is the silent hotel room after the stage high. He avoids Xanax and Valium because he fears dependence; he sleeps best around days 7–10 of a holiday and then gets sick when his body finally stops. He has moved from his old "shark" metaphor (stop moving and you die) toward stopping to assess before hunting again — he accepted Raj's "lion theory" of resting, then hunting hard.

## Claims to be careful with
- None stand out as general medical claims — the episode is personal testimony about addiction, anger, body image, and mental health rather than health advice; his statements about his own alcohol misuse and family history are autobiographical.

## Summary
YUNGBLUD (Dominic Harrison) talks candidly with Raj Shamani about the insecurities behind the performer: childhood shaming, parents who loved and hurt each other, anger managed through boxing, a post-album spiral into food and alcohol, body-image battles, and the loneliness of hotel rooms after arena highs. Running through it is his philosophy — community over ego, radical honesty in relationships, art made selfishly and truthfully, and a definition of masculinity that makes room for both nail polish and the boxing ring.
