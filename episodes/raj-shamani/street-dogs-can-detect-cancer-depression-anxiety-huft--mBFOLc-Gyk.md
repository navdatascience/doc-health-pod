## Important tidbits
- The human–dog bond began roughly 20,000–30,000 years ago: dogs descend from grey wolves, and the friendlier wolves started approaching early human settlements for food scraps and warmth from fires, while humans realised the wolves gave early warning of threats and helped with hunting. Humans then selectively bred across generations for traits like sweetness and loyalty, creating today's breeds.
- Her pick for the most intelligent dogs is India's street dogs — the Indies. Navigating Indian roads is no joke: they read a person in a second (who is kind, who is scared, who might befriend or feed them — they know which tea stall will give food), survive on their own, and many are cared for by feeders who feed them at midnight or 4–5 in the morning to avoid harassment. Despite injuries and hostility they are amazingly resilient and adapted to the climate, and she argues they deserve to be considered equal to any breed instead of being treated as second-class by Indians themselves.
- The most misunderstood breeds are pit bulls and Rottweilers, victims of media sensationalisation. Her core claim: any dog becomes aggressive when misunderstood — aggression comes from fear, not feeling safe — and every dog gives many signals before it ever bites. A growl is a "distance-creating signal", the dog politely asking for space because it feels threatened or uncomfortable; it is actually a sign of respect ("I am telling you instead of biting you"). If you punish a dog for growling five times, the sixth time it will bite without warning, because you trained the warning away.
- Dogs are constantly talking through "calming signals", documented by Norwegian trainer Turid Rugaas: lip-licking when it is not right after a meal signals stress; turning the head away (for example when a phone is pushed into the dog's face) means "I don't like this"; "whale eye" — the whites of the eyes showing — signals fear; stiffness during hugs is discomfort (hugging is a human behaviour dogs have merely learned to tolerate); dogs approach each other in a curve, never head-on, because a head-on approach is threatening; and suddenly sniffing the ground when someone gets angry is an attempt to defuse the situation. A happy dog, by contrast, shows relaxed ears, relaxed eyes, a loose body and a relaxed wag — you have to read the whole body.
- Her training philosophy is love and understanding over punishment and fear. On house-soiling, she is blunt: the human should hit themselves with the rolled-up newspaper, not the dog. A puppy simply has a bladder and no training; the owner failed to take it out — puppies need to go out roughly every hour to a chosen surface (garden, balcony), and they learn fast. Punishing a natural body function creates trauma and behavioural issues. If a dog does its business indoors right after a walk, that is not typical — it could be an infection or territory marking — and remember most dogs are walked only two or three times a day and hold it for hours.
- The hardest thing to spot in a dog is pain, because in the wild an animal that shows pain is seen as weak and becomes prey first — so dogs hide pain until it is extreme. Watch for tiny changes: taking even 2–3 seconds longer to get up or sit, changes in eating, struggling with stairs, or reluctance to go for a walk. Never drag a dog that sits down and refuses; let the dog lead on walks in most cases.
- Walk your dog on a body harness, not a collar. Every pull on a collar constricts the throat and cuts oxygen; it can pull muscles and even fracture the small bones in the throat. A dog in pain while eating may growl, get labelled as having "food aggression", and be sent to a trainer — when the whole chain started with the collar. Dogs cannot tell you they are in pain.
- Dogs do show sadness and depression: lower energy, moving slower, sometimes not eating, and physical signs like excess hair fall, much as stressed humans shed hair. Dogs also grieve — after a family member dies they may be visibly low and stay with that person's belongings or the place they used to sit. And they can become happy again, which she calls amazing: despite every mistake humans make, dogs forgive and open their hearts again.
- Dogs read humans faster than humans read each other — energy, vibe, facial expressions — which is why, she believes, they detect anxiety and depression early, and there are many reported instances of dogs smelling out even cancer in humans, apparently picking up micro-level changes in the body. She recounts a recent news story of a puppy whose incessant howling alerted his family to cracks and tremors before a landslide; the whole village evacuated, the landslide buried the place, and there was not a single casualty. Night-time howling, though, is usually just pack behaviour — one dog starts and the rest join in. Barking and howling are simply dogs' spoken language, and we punish them far too much for communicating.
- Getting a dog is a full commitment: if you work all day and come home at 10 pm just to sleep, please do not bring one home — the dog is a small part of your life, but you are the dog's whole life. Large families suit most dogs, and Indies suit both large and smaller families; she advises avoiding exotic breeds (climate, boredom, unmet needs) rather than prescribing breed-by-home formulas. Abandonment is rampant: when the novelty wears off or teething starts, people drive dogs far away and dump them, and a pet dog that never learned street survival gets attacked by local street dogs; shelters are overflowing, and at least 70–75 dogs have been tied outside HUFT stores and never collected — people even bring dogs in "for a spa" and never return. She distinguishes pet owners (low connection — the dog tied outside for security), pet lovers (casual affection) and pet parents (who treat the dog as a family member, read food labels and research how to give it its best life) — and questions whether a living being can be "owned" at all.
- India's cat market is growing in late double digits, much faster than the dog market, driven by millennials and Gen Z: cats need no walks, litter-train almost instinctively, groom themselves, are very clean, and tolerate being alone far longer than dogs. They are most active at dawn and dusk, need netted balconies (they jump), eat smaller meals more often, drink water reluctantly — so hydration-rich food helps — and prefer flowing water, hence fountains. The myths that cats are selfish and aloof are, in her experience of the last 3–4 years, rubbish.
- Dogs live in the present: they are not ruminating over the past or worrying about the future, so their emotions are simpler than ours. That presence is also why heartbreak recovery is easier with a pet — the dog fills spaces and gaps you did not know you had. The host cites the neuroscience he has read: hugging and touching a dog releases oxytocin, play releases dopamine, and studies link dog ownership with resilience, better cognition and ageing, and better child development and confidence. Dogs also forgive within seconds — no ego — and teach you not to carry your work mood home: give the first 10–15 minutes at home to being playful and loving.
- "Zoomies" — the frantic running in circles (frenetic random activity) — are usually read as over-excitement and happiness, but she notes a recent article reinterpreting them as possible overstimulation: a dog that cannot self-regulate cannot think clearly either, just like a super-excited or super-anxious human. Owners compound the problem by giving dogs zero choices — food, walk time, route, sleep, everything is decided and imposed — and by ignoring mental health while watching only physical health. Separation anxiety exploded after Covid, when dogs went from 24 hours of company to being left alone; the fix is micro-dosing alone time — 2 minutes, then 5, then 10, building slowly to an hour — so the dog learns you always come back. No breed of dog can genuinely be left alone for long stretches; cats cope much better.
- Care basics: bathe a dog only once every 2–3 weeks, no more than twice a month, or the skin dries out — use wet wipes and brushing in between, and a pet-safe, nourishing shampoo; a pet spa follows the same frequency. Shedding is natural (double-coated breeds like retrievers and shepherds shed heavily when winter turns to summer), but excessive shedding can signal an underlying issue. Pet-proofing matters in Indian homes: marble floors are so slippery that dogs — whose feet are built almost like high heels — skid constantly, developing joint and muscle problems, so scatter rugs and carpets; net balconies for cats and soften sharp corners.
- Rotate your dog's food instead of feeding one packaged food for life: different proteins, vegetables and safe fruits (papaya, apple, a small piece of mango, watermelon) deliver different vitamins and minerals, build immunity and reduce food intolerances — a digestive system never exposed to variety gets upset by any new food. She feeds eggs, tofu, paneer, beans, carrots, pumpkin and bottle gourd, adds curd or buttermilk, and even stirs in herbs — cumin, ajwain, coriander, moringa, ashwagandha, turmeric, ginger. Plain boiled vegetables with rice are acceptable if adjusted toward more protein and fewer carbs, but spiced, salted, oily human food is not; the strict no-list is chocolate, alcohol, anything high in sugar, and onions. Supplements should be need-based — skin and coat, bone and joint (hip dysplasia, pain), immunity, digestion — never a blanket prescription. Pet insurance exists in India but is very new with very low penetration.
- On neutering, she calls it genuinely controversial: it is a major intervention in the hormonal system. The pros are no unwanted litters, no messy heat cycles, and research suggesting lower prostate-cancer risk and less aggression in males; the cons include incontinence in females (loss of bladder control), weight gain, and skin and coat changes — and neutering does not eliminate cancer altogether, only certain kinds. Her advice is to decide with a vet you deeply trust, based on your individual dog, weighing whether the benefits outweigh the negatives for that animal.

## Claims to be careful with
- The cancer-detection ability of dogs is presented anecdotally — Rashi Narang explicitly says she does not have enough knowledge to quote it from a scientific perspective, and the landslide puppy story is one she read in a news article whose location she could not recall.
- Neutering health claims (cancer prevention, reduced aggression) are contested; the host's vet reportedly said cancer chances would be "eliminated", which she corrects in the conversation — benefits and side effects vary by dog, sex and timing.
- Food-rotation benefits, herb additions such as ashwagandha, and the reinterpretation of zoomies as overstimulation reflect her reading and practice philosophy rather than settled veterinary consensus — notably, the host's own vet advised the opposite on food (one single food).
- Several segments promote her own company's products (HUFT's Sara's Wholesome Food, pee-lock powder, festive collections), so those parts are marketing as well as advice.

## Summary
HUFT founder Rashi Narang makes the case for treating dogs as family members whose language we must learn: read their calming signals instead of punishing growls, never discipline with fear, walk them on harnesses, watch for the pain they are wired to hide, rotate their food, and respect their emotional lives — from the street-smart Indies she champions to the grieving, forgiving, illness-sensing companions she believes can detect everything from depression to cancer.
