## Important tidbits
- The biggest misconception about acne, per Dr Lahiri, is that it is caused by bad hygiene. He says acne is driven mainly by family/genetic background, stress, and wrong products applied to the skin — Europe has among the highest acne rates despite far less pollution than Delhi.
- He says there is no evidence milk or dairy causes acne: Denmark, Canada, and New Zealand have the world's highest milk consumption without the highest acne rates, and village children with little access to dairy still get pimples.
- Fried food, dosa, uttapam, or biryani have no direct relation to acne, and there is no "better diet for skin" — veg or non-veg makes no difference; only avoid a food if you are allergic or intolerant to it.
- He dismisses leech therapy, snail therapy, applying urine, and "semen facials" as bogus with no science, and says the belief that being sexually active reduces acne has absolutely no evidence behind it.
- Never pop a pimple: bacteria lodged under your nails get pushed into an already inflamed spot, turning a papule into a nodule or cyst and causing scars and post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation; dermatologists instead use sterile comedone extractors and can inject steroid where needed.
- Toothpaste is formulated for teeth, not skin — its pH can cause contact dermatitis and dark pigmentation; apple cider vinegar and lemon juice (pH around 4.4–4.5) commonly cause irritant contact dermatitis and burns, including underarm burns he has treated. Neem leaves and multani mitti are, in his words, myths with no dermatological basis.
- Oil cleansing makes no sense to him because pimples arise from overproduction of oil, driven by androgens in the teenage years.
- Celebrities do not have flawless skin: they attend his clinic without makeup with the same pimples and skin issues as everyone else; what audiences see is makeup, grooming, and well-timed shoots.
- Topical steroid misuse (TSDF — topical steroid damaged/dependent face) is a crisis in India: he cites a 2009 study finding about 60% of steroid abusers applying the strongest steroids on the face without reason and about 90% doing so without prescription; he calls it a roughly ₹1,000-crore business, notes even babies absorb steroid from fairness creams, and says the current approach is to stop the steroid outright rather than taper.
- Scale of acne, as he states it: about 650 million people (~9.5% of the world's population) have acne at any given time, about 15 crore in India; 80–90% of teenagers, about 50% of young adults, 20–30% of 30–50-year-olds, and 10–20% of those above 50 are affected, and 80–90% of all humans get acne at some point — making it the eighth most common disease globally. "Acne" and "pimple" are the same thing (Latin vs English); doctors grade it mild, moderate, or severe.
- Adult acne in women — lower-face acne with premenstrual flare-ups — usually points to an internal hormonal problem: he says in 60–70% of such cases it is the dermatologist who first detects PCOS and refers the patient to an endocrinologist; post-menopausal acne appears as female hormones decline relative to male hormones.
- Obesity and metabolic syndrome are genuinely linked to acne: warning signs include acanthosis nigricans (darkened neck and underarms), excess hair growth, irregular periods, and hair thinning — and while fried food does not directly cause pimples, eating less helps this group through weight loss.
- Treatment ladder: mild acne needs only a non-comedogenic cleanser and moisturiser; moderate acne (with pus/infection) needs topical antibiotics and a doctor; severe acne needs oral antibiotics (he names doxycycline, azithromycin, lymecycline, minocycline) and oral isotretinoin — always under a qualified dermatologist (DVD/MD/DNB in dermatology), never self-continued beyond the prescription. Steroid-free spot correctors with niacinamide, glycolic acid, or salicylic acid are acceptable, and benzoyl peroxide, topical retinoids, and the newer trifarotene (useful for back acne, "backne," as it also fades pigmentation) are standard tools.
- Isotretinoin cautions he gives: it can cause birth deformities if taken during pregnancy, and depression and suicidal tendency have been reported — mainly in Western reports at around 60 mg/day, versus the ~20 mg/day he says is used in India; he states there is no evidence it causes erectile dysfunction or infertility in men.
- Skin and mind affect each other strongly: he says about 40% of his patients panic over a single pimple, teenagers' academic performance can deteriorate, and some need psychiatric support; scars are largely preventable with timely treatment, and he estimates 60–70% of scars are recoverable with lasers and peels.
- Practical rules: patch-test any new product behind the ear or on the elbow for about 12 hours before putting it on your face; teenagers' first-line care is topical treatment with a non-comedogenic cleanser, moisturiser, and SPF 15–30 sunscreen (titanium dioxide or zinc oxide); birth control pills can actually treat PCOS-related acne rather than cause it; and verify credentials before trusting a "skin doctor" — he estimates there are 10–20 quacks for every genuine dermatologist in India, and names can be checked with the state medical council.

## Claims to be careful with
- Dr Lahiri's blanket position that diet has no connection to acne is his strong clinical stance; he himself carves out an exception for obesity and metabolic syndrome, where he accepts food matters via weight.
- The statistics he quotes (650 million acne cases worldwide, the 2009 study's ~60%/~90% steroid-abuse figures, dermatologists detecting 60–70% of PCOS cases, 10–20 quacks per dermatologist) are given from memory in conversation and should be treated as his stated figures.
- The isotretinoin–depression/suicidal-tendency link is described by him as "reported" in the literature; he notes it has rarely been encountered in his Indian practice.
- His explanation that skin and the nervous system develop from the same embryonic cells is offered as the basis for the mind–skin connection and is a simplification.

## Summary
Dermatologist Dr Koushik Lahiri spends the episode dismantling acne myths: hygiene, milk, fried food, and sleep do not cause acne, he says — genetics, stress, and wrong products do — and leech therapy, urine application, toothpaste, apple cider vinegar, lemon juice, and multani mitti range from useless to actively harmful. He warns against pimple-popping and India's topical-steroid epidemic, explains when adult acne signals PCOS, lays out the mild-to-severe treatment ladder up to isotretinoin with its pregnancy and mood cautions, and closes with practical advice on patch-testing products and verifying a dermatologist's credentials.
