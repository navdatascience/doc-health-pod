## Important tidbits
- Serial killers, per Gill, show no genuine remorse — they fake and mirror other people's emotions convincingly; he attributes this partly to a smaller limbic (emotional) brain, so they cannot gauge the impact of the damage they cause, and says brain scans of such offenders show changes not seen in others.
- The MacDonald triad: three childhood red flags — fire-setting (arson) whenever given the chance, trying to seriously harm animals (dogs, cats), and bedwetting that returns at age 8–9 (enuresis/emotional regression). If all three appear, Gill advises seeing a specialist early; FBI profiler John Douglas wanted these markers taught in schools.
- Stress begins before birth: Gill cites University of Durham research (Nadja Reissland) in which stressed mothers passed the stress hormone cortisol to the fetus, which responded on ultrasound by scratching its face with the left hand, furrowing brows and sucking its thumb.
- An emotionally absent father combined with a constantly nagging mother is, per Gill, a damaging pattern that can produce deviant tendencies in a child; childhood physical abuse and normalised household violence (he cites the Nirbhaya case perpetrators) also shape later behaviour.
- Behaviour should be read through the ABC model — Antecedent (where the behaviour came from), Behaviour, Consequence — because visible behaviour develops over years, not overnight.
- Self-harm/cutting: Gill says the physical pain and sight of blood distract the mind from emotional pain; many children also cut where it stays hidden to get attention from emotionally unavailable parents. He cautions that teenage self-harm marks are not a reliable predictor of adult behaviour, because the emotional brain is nearly developed in teens while the frontal lobe ("the brakes") matures only around age 25.
- Statement analysis and the "power of pronouns" (research attributed to Dr James Pennebaker): people who drop the pronoun "I" — e.g. replying "love you too" instead of "I love you too", or narrating without any pronouns — may not have actually experienced what they describe; suspects also shift from singular to team pronouns ("we were all searching") to spread blame. Gill stresses this must be matched against the person's actual behaviour.
- Guilty knowledge creates "psychological distancing": a suspect avoids the victim's name and softens the crime's wording (never saying "stole", saying "I didn't pick anything up"), because naming it spikes their blood pressure; he gives Bill Clinton's "sexual relations with that woman, Miss Lewinsky" as a public example of distancing language.
- The first kill, per Gill, grows out of obsession and fantasy beginning around age 8; the adrenaline rush of the first act drives escalation and method improvement, followed by a depressive phase, then a renewed hunt — a repeating cycle. He cites the Green River Killer (Gary Ridgway), who passed a polygraph because he felt nothing, and BTK (Dennis Rader), who lived a parallel respectable life.
- Children copy observed violence quickly because of mirror neurons: in Stanford's Bobo doll experiment, 3–6-year-olds who watched an adult kick and attack the doll picked up the knife and gun props and stabbed it, while children who saw only play replicated play. Gill quotes neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky that aggression is a natural human behaviour; context (who holds the gun) decides whether violence gets support or criticism.
- Ordinary hero/rescue fantasies are normal and often movie-fuelled; the fantasies of killers like Ed Kemper (who beheaded victims so they would "look up" to him, as his mother demanded) are categorically different and arise from years of abuse plus multiple contributing factors — no single factor makes a criminal.
- Interrogation method: do your homework on the suspect first; let them talk more than you (elicitation, even playing dumb); US police reportedly don't touch the crime for the first 40–45 minutes to establish a behavioural baseline; use the "sandwich technique" of inserting the real question between casual ones; ask for the story backwards to break a rehearsed lie; and ask cognitive questions (what did you hear, smell, feel) that a liar has not prepared.
- In criminal settings, excessive unbroken eye contact is a red flag ("threat tracking" — the suspect watches how much you believe the story), not a sign of innocence; excessive yawning is also a stress sign because stress dries the mouth.
- Under high cognitive load, stress leaks through the hands: in Prof. Aldert Vrij's (University of Portsmouth) reverse-alphabet clapping exercise, hand movements slow, desynchronise or stop, and speech shows response latency — liars' hand movements often go "dead" (Gill cites Clinton's still hands during his denial versus his normally animated gesturing).
- Everyday lie-detection cues Gill lists: gestural retreat (leaning/pulling back while answering), eye blocking, ear touching/blocking when the listener dislikes what they hear, a sudden rise in blink rate (baseline is 12–20 blinks/minute when relaxed), lip compression ("when the lips disappear, the stress is near"), vague answers, and the question-reversal strategy — answering "Did you kill your wife?" with "Are you asking if I killed my wife?"
- Statement structure tell: an innocent account typically runs 25% before the incident, 50% during, 25% after; someone involved in the crime inverts it — long on the before and after, minimal on the crime itself.
- Good liars, per Gill, rely on confidence (the root of "con man"), creating a false past or future identity to detach from the present self, flooding you with vivid extra detail that paints a picture in your mind, and "holier than thou" appeals ("God is my witness", "mother swear"); bad liars stammer, fidget and struggle visibly.
- Guilty people often project exaggerated innocence and timidity (he notes rapists frequently present as incapable and victimised), whereas a wrongfully accused person typically shows anger — though the innocent may also plead, they usually show suppressed anger. Anxiety alone proves nothing: he compares it to white-coat hypertension, where blood pressure rises at the doctor's office (or police station) without any guilt.
- Power and culture: Malcolm Gladwell's power-distance research (cited from Outliers) links plane crashes to junior officers from high power-distance cultures not questioning the captain in time. Gill reads politicians' body language — Obama's relaxed gait and empathy, Putin swinging only one arm because KGB training keeps the other on the weapon, Trump's consistency and unapologetic self-belief — and notes the top 1% almost never publicly say "sorry".
- To look powerful instantly: the steeple hand position (with variations — upright to shut down opinions, rotating to signal willingness to engage), feet planted apart to claim territory, chin level (too high reads arrogant, too low reads like confession), open hands and confident touch ("physical expansion"). Common social mistakes: standing angled away from a group (read as arrogant), going off-topic instead of finding common ground, and not listening for the other person's pain point.
- Priming effects he cites: being handed a warm drink makes people describe a stranger as a "warm" person, candlelight dinners work partly because pupils dilate in low light, lowering your own chair height makes the other person feel powerful, and remembering and using strangers' names creates an instant connection (touch releases oxytocin).

## Claims to be careful with
- The MacDonald triad is presented as a warning sign, but Gill himself notes it was later contested; it is not a validated predictor of future violence.
- The claim that holding a steeple/power pose for 2 minutes raises testosterone and lowers cortisol reflects "power posing" research whose hormonal effects have largely failed to replicate.
- The "smaller limbic brain" explanation of serial killers and several single-cue lie signs (blink rate, yawning, pronouns) are simplifications — Gill himself repeatedly stresses using baselines, clusters of cues and context rather than any one signal.
- Crime statistics, case details and study findings (e.g. the Durham fetal-stress study, the pronoun research) are quoted from memory in conversation and not independently verified in the episode.

## Summary
Consultant psychologist and body language expert Gaurav Gill explains how serial killers are made rather than born — childhood trauma, the MacDonald triad, fantasy and escalation — and then gives a practitioner's toolkit for reading people: statement analysis and pronoun use, distancing language, interrogation technique, stress cues in hands, eyes and lips, and the body language of power drawn from politicians and everyday social life.
