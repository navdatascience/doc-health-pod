## Important tidbits
- The core problem with the Indian thali: to get just 10 g of protein from roti you would need to eat about nine rotis. Structurally, less than 10% of the calories in a typical Indian diet come from protein.
- Protein leverage: citing experiments by Raubenheimer and Simpson (360 cockroaches, with parallels in humans), he explains that groups fed a high-carb/low-protein diet overate and became fat, while high-protein/low-carb groups did not — animals and humans keep eating until their minimum protein requirement is met, so diluting protein with carbs drives overeating.
- Citing ICMR-linked data, he says 60+ crore Indians are already overweight or obese at a median age of just 29, estimates a ~50% lifetime probability of diabetes and ~70% probability of obesity for an Indian, and states that being diabetic and obese raises heart-attack risk by ~200% and cancer risk by ~300%; obesity has an established causal link with 13 types of cancer.
- Visceral fat is the real danger: subcutaneous fat under the skin is relatively harmless, but when fat cells overflow, fat wraps around organs (visceral adiposity) — in the liver it leads to liver failure, around the kidney to kidney problems, around the heart to heart disease.
- Protein fundamentals: proteins are built from 20 amino acids — 9 essential (must come from food) and 11 non-essential (the body makes them). Leucine, lysine and methionine matter most; per Stu Phillips' "leucine trigger" hypothesis, muscle protein synthesis only kick-starts when leucine in the bloodstream crosses roughly 2.5–3 g.
- All protein originates in plants: plants (via soil bacteria) are the only organisms that can fix atmospheric nitrogen into amino acids; ruminants like cows additionally use inorganic nitrogen through their rumen bacteria, which is why animal foods are denser in essential amino acids. Vegetarians get all 9 essential amino acids too, but must eat larger volumes (more rajma, chole, dal) to match.
- Judge a food by its "calories from protein" percentage, not grams alone: besan flour has 15 g protein per 100 g but also 73 g carbs and 380 kcal — only ~16% of its calories come from protein. His rule for packaged "protein" products (protein ice cream, chips, cookies): if under 50% of calories come from protein, it is not a protein food. The math: protein grams × 4 ÷ total calories.
- Reference numbers per 100 g: rice ~2.5 g protein, wheat ~10 g, besan ~15 g, khapli atta ~15 g, jowar/bajra ~9–10 g. Better vegetarian bets: soybean gives ~15–16 g protein for 150–200 kcal, and low-fat paneer ~23 g protein for ~150 kcal. WHO's baseline recommendation is 0.8 g protein per kg body weight.
- The kidney myth: the studies linking high protein to kidney damage used subjects who already had kidney disease (reverse causality). Studies feeding bodybuilders ~4.4 g/kg and normal people 2.5–2.8 g/kg found kidneys functioning perfectly well, even more efficiently. But people whose kidneys are already damaged must NOT eat excess protein — these are two different problems.
- The "protein causes cancer" claim comes from leucine activating the mTOR/IGF-1 growth pathway, which cancer cells also use — he argues this is an association with growth signalling, not established causation.
- Protein needs spike in repair: severe burn patients are given 3–4 g protein per kg body weight, and skin itself is protein. Combining resistance training with adequate protein raises muscle protein synthesis by ~27%. His ageing advice: do not carry your grandmother's vegetable bag or stop her climbing stairs — load-bearing keeps elders' muscle alive.
- His own routine at 54 (about 70 kg, roughly 130–140 g protein/day): he breaks the overnight fast with 2 scoops of protein shake (~50 g protein for ~250 kcal instead of a 500–700 kcal breakfast), eats a normal lunch of rice/roti, dal or rajma and sabzi plus a 12 g-protein skyr curd, takes one more scoop before leaving the office so he does not snack in the evening, and keeps dinner protein-rich (often paneer). Three scoops give him 75–80 g of protein with almost no added complexity.
- On timing: protein taken before a workout does NOT kick-start muscle protein synthesis, because breakdown dominates during training. Post-workout protein matters most for untrained people, who should take it within ~2 hours; regular trainees get the anabolic effect over the following ~48 hours regardless of exact timing. The first and last meals matter most: after a 10–12 hour overnight fast, eat 30–40 g protein at the first meal, and make the last meal protein-rich to reduce overnight muscle breakdown.
- On choosing a protein powder: whey is the benchmark (80–88% of its calories from protein, a ratio no whole vegetarian or non-vegetarian food matches); plant proteins generally cost more and fermented-yeast protein more still. His practical advice is to buy from a brand you trust and verify the calories-from-protein ratio on the label instead of believing marketing. He also admits the honest caveat: whole food is ideal, and the very long-term effects of protein shakes are not yet studied.
- His philosophy is "muscle-centric nutrition": muscle has only two jobs — mobility and metabolism. Fat loss does not guarantee muscle gain, but muscle gain guarantees fat loss, so think in terms of gaining and protecting muscle rather than losing weight. His viral vada pav moment makes the same point about testing beliefs: a lab test of a Kirti College vada pav showed 10 g of protein, and the reel was misread as "eat vada pav for protein" when his intent was to question assumptions about food.

## Claims to be careful with
- The health statistics he quotes (60+ crore overweight/obese Indians, ~50% diabetes and ~70% obesity lifetime probabilities, 200%/300% risk increases) are cited from memory of ICMR-linked reports and should be treated as his figures, not verified data.
- The protein-leverage evidence is extrapolated partly from insect and animal experiments; the human effect is presented more strongly ("100% your weight loss will happen if you raise protein") than the underlying research supports.
- His "zero evidence" statement on protein and kidneys applies only to people with healthy kidneys — he himself stresses that anyone with existing kidney damage must avoid excess protein.
- His personal high-protein routine and product views are one individual's example (he is a health educator, not a medical doctor), and his claims about specific protein brands are opinions.

## Summary
Health educator Prashant Desai argues that India's obesity and diabetes crisis is fundamentally a protein problem: the traditional thali delivers under 10% of calories from protein, so people overeat carbs chasing protein satiety (the protein-leverage effect), accumulate visceral fat, and lose muscle. He explains amino acids and the leucine trigger, debunks the protein-kidney and protein-cancer myths, teaches his "calories from protein %" test for judging foods and protein products, lays out protein timing around workouts and overnight fasting, and makes the case for muscle-centric nutrition over weight loss.
