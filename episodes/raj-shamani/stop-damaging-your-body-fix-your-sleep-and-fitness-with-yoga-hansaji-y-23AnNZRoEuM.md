## Important tidbits
- Her health framework has four checks: Ahaar (food), Vihaar (rest, recreation and movement), Aachaar (a fixed daily routine/timetable) and Vichaar (your thoughts). Most illness, she says, comes from mistakes in these four areas.
- For recurring colds and coughs: do jal neti — irrigate the nose with lukewarm salt water so irritants are washed out before they activate (she says irritants take about 36 hours to take effect). Do it once a day after coming home; don't do it if the nose is fully blocked; salt-water gargling is the fallback.
- How to eat ice cream without triggering a cold: let it melt completely in the mouth, mix it with saliva, swallow slowly, then drink some warm water. Similarly, chewing roasted chana after chocolate cleans children's teeth and throat.
- Self-checks for hidden ill health: dull eyes on waking, a coated or yellow tongue (possible B12 deficiency), gums bleeding during flossing (vitamin C deficiency), and bloating/gas all signal you should first change your food and nutrients.
- Walk 3–4 km every day (at least ~30 minutes), even if it's rounds inside your home or compound, and get up and move every hour if your job is sedentary. She cites a marathon-running Mumbai gynaecologist who still had a heart attack at 38 because he sat consulting all day.
- Thoughts make you sick, she argues: a hurtful comment raises blood pressure and pulse, shortens breathing and stops digestion — food then gets stored as fat/cholesterol or thrown off, causing joint pains, poor concentration and memory. She states that practically 92% of diseases are psychosomatic in nature.
- Her position on medicine: medicines help you live with diabetes, heart disease or depression but cannot remove the disease from the root — only how you live does that. But she is explicit: never stop your medicines and depend only on yoga; take support from allopathy, homeopathy or whichever pathy suits you when needed.
- Yoga's technique side (asana, pranayama, kriyas) done wrong can harm: breath retention (kumbhak) must follow a ratio — if you inhale for 6 seconds, hold for at most 12 seconds. She recounts a young army aspirant who force-held his breath for two minutes from a book and suffered a nervous breakdown, taking a month of gentle retraining to recover.
- In headstand, the body's weight must be borne by the arms and shoulders, not the neck, otherwise it causes cervical spondylosis; children's bodies tolerate inversions that adults' should not attempt carelessly.
- Morning routine without leaving the bed: take long breaths with eyes closed, do a full-body "stick pose" stretch, move the legs up and down one at a time, do Bhadrasana/butterfly with the legs, and breathe from the belly — this activates the intestines so you wake up properly and evacuate.
- Three poses everyone should do daily: Sarvangasana (shoulder stand — exercises the whole body against gravity; do it on an empty stomach), a meditative sitting pose (Padmasana, Sukhasana or Vajrasana, even cross-legged on a sofa, to keep the extremities flexible), and prone back-bends (Bhujangasana, then Shalabhasana, progressing to the bow pose) so the spine bends backwards, not only forwards.
- Her sleep protocol: eat dinner early and light (khichdi, soups), stroll for ~30 minutes after dinner (never lie down straight after food), bathe before bed (start lukewarm, finish cold), shut off electronics at least an hour before bed, read something light or funny rather than heavy philosophy, and note down 10 positive points from the day — "night is not there to solve your problems." Spend 7–8 hours in bed.
- Go to bed fresh, not exhausted: if you are tired, first wash off the fatigue (e.g., soak your feet in warm water). Fatigue should be removed before sleep or any other activity.
- For a better sex life, she recommends Bhadrasana, Setu Bandhasana (bridge pose) and Ashwini Mudra — repeatedly contracting and relaxing the pelvic floor/anal and vaginal muscles while pulling the bottom upward — practised daily as a routine. Partners should also relax and caress each other before sleep; she notes women need this care more than intercourse itself.
- Stress, via the Bhagavad Gita: a wise person does not dwell on the past (which brings sorrow) or the future (which brings anxiety and tension) — discipline your mind to stay in the present.
- For hair thinning: back-comb with a wide comb for 2–3 minutes daily, grip the hair with your fingers and shake the scalp, and do Sarvangasana for circulation to the head. Don't wet/wash hair daily; use lemon to remove dandruff; if the scalp is genuinely dry, apply warmed amla or bhringraj oil to the scalp (not the hair) and wash it off after 30 minutes; methi (fenugreek) is also traditionally used.
- For glowing skin: keep skin moisturised — she recommends applying milk (with a drop of curd or a little honey) to the face and body before bathing. She is against excess soap: use a friction bath (rub with a dry towel, then a wet towel), reserving soap mainly for hairy areas and the soles of the feet, and use steam for the face.
- For belly fat: Naukasana (boat pose), slow leg raises and leg movements in different directions lying/sitting/standing, and twisting poses — combined with eating only when hungry and only enough to satisfy hunger, not fill the stomach.
- Wake before sunrise to align with the circadian (sunrise-to-sunset) rhythm; if you can't, create an "emergency" or a responsibility at that hour — urgency is what actually gets people up, just as it does for a flight or a hospital visit.
- On weight lifting vs yoga: holding your own body weight against gravity in asanas builds some muscle, but not as much as progressive weight lifting; yoga's warning is against excess of anything — muscles should be adequate and the body flexible, not bulky at the cost of flexibility.

## Claims to be careful with
- The figure that ~92% of diseases are psychosomatic is her stated position, presented without a cited source in the episode.
- Claims that yoga and lifestyle can remove diabetes, heart disease and high cholesterol "from the root" while medicine only manages them reflect her philosophy; she herself cautions against stopping medication.
- The account of heart patients' cholesterol falling only after they switched to a vegetarian diet in The Yoga Institute's "bypassing the bypass surgery" research is described anecdotally, without published data shown.
- Remedies such as jal neti preventing colds, the ice-cream eating technique, lemon for dandruff, milk as a moisturiser and minimising soap are traditional/experience-based practices rather than clinically tested treatments.
- The story of breath-holding causing a nervous breakdown is a single anecdote from her father-in-law's time at the institute.

## Summary
Hansaji Yogendra of The Yoga Institute lays out a yogic operating system for health built on food, rest and movement, routine, and thoughts (Ahaar, Vihaar, Aachaar, Vichaar). She gives concrete practices — jal neti for recurring colds, an in-bed morning routine, three essential daily asanas, a detailed sleep protocol, pelvic-floor practices for sexual health, and natural hair and skin care — while insisting yoga is a science of awareness and a way of living that complements, and never replaces, medicine.
